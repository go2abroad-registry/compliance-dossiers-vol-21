Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies to a New Zealand International Company. The Company may be incorporated without any paid-up capital and may allot shares of no par value for zero or nominal consideration under sections 124-130 of the Companies Act 1993. No escrow account, capital deposit, or banked-capital requirement is imposed by New Zealand law. The Company may be 100% non-resident-owned, provided that at least one director ordinarily resides in New Zealand or Australia (section 152, Companies Act 1993).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification regime; corporate tax status is determined by statute. The relevant statutory mechanisms are: (a) a qualifying close-held company may elect to become a Look-Through Company (LTC) under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, producing full tax-transparent treatment of income and losses; (b) qualifying affiliated companies may elect consolidated group treatment; (c) a non-resident International Company may rely on the territorial scope rules to maintain non-resident status if its central management and control is exercised outside New Zealand; (d) hybrid mismatch provisions (subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007) apply automatically and cannot be elected away. For the CK_IC non-resident International Company, no election is required to achieve tax-exempt foreign-source treatment absent New Zealand-source income or a permanent establishment.

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies (actual or expected) in any 12-month period; a non-resident supplier of remote services or low-value distributed digital goods into New Zealand must register only if its New Zealand consumer-facing supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For remote screen-share, unattended machine telemetry, and IoT remote-support software: zero-rating applies under section 11(1)(mb) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 where the services are supplied to a non-resident person who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed. The supply must not be received by an individual physically present in New Zealand at the time of supply. Physical goods (e.g., IoT hardware, sensors, or screen-share appliances) are zero-rated only if they are exported by the supplier within 28 days of the supply to the non-resident recipient and are not subsequently returned or treated as supplied in New Zealand.

  • Customs Export Declaration (NZ Customs Service electronic declaration through JADE and the Trade Single Window)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (for physical IoT hardware / telemetry device exports)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (or bank SWIFT / foreign-exchange records evidencing offshore proceeds)
  • Commercial Invoice and Software Supply / SaaS Agreement
  • Packing List (where accompanied by physical goods)
  • Certificate of Origin or technical-support evidence (where the overseas recipient's non-resident status is challenged by IRD)

New Zealand has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no continuous transaction control system. E-invoicing is voluntary and operates on the PEPPOL standard through Inland Revenue's e-Invoicing service in myIR; GST is self-assessed on GST101 returns filed monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly via myIR, E-File, or gateway software. There is no API-based clearance mandate; however, registered businesses must retain electronically generated invoices and related records in a format capable of being accessed by IRD on request.

Inland Revenue may assess a shortfall penalty under Part 9 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. For fraudulent or intentionally misleading zero-rating claims, the penalty is up to 150% of the tax shortfall; an 'abusive tax position' attracts a 100% penalty, a 'gross carelessness' position 40%, and a 'not taking reasonable care' position 20%. Late payment and late filing penalties further accrue under section 139A, and use-of-money interest applies from the due date. Zero-rated export treatment is retrospectively clawed back if the exporter fails to substantiate exportation or the services were actually performed for a private consumer physically present in New Zealand; criminal prosecution is also available for tax evasion, false GST returns, and obstruction. The CK_IC structure must therefore retain contemporaneous IP geolocation, recipient residency certificates, bank routing evidence, and the underlying software delivery logs.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely convertible on both the current account and the capital account. There are no exchange control approvals, surrender requirements, or investment licensing hurdles for software/IT service export revenues. Profit repatriation in the form of dividends is unrestricted, subject only to the Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) framework (statutory rate 15%, potentially reduced under an applicable Double Tax Agreement) and the standard company solvency test under the Companies Act 1993. Capital injections and outward capital redemptions are also unrestricted, though the bank will submit an International Transaction Reporting Form (ITRF) for Balance of Payments statistics. The only quantitative limitation is the aggregate risk-limit credit assessment applied by the commercial bank, not a regulatory restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no export proceeds surrender rules or mandatory conversion ratios in New Zealand. All exporters, including digital service providers, are permitted to retain 100% of foreign currency earnings indefinitely in onshore or offshore accounts. Receipts may be held in any freely convertible currency (USD, EUR, AUD, GBP, NZD) and converted at commercial market rates at the discretion of the treasury function. The only administrative obligation is the statistical reporting of the underlying transaction via the bank's ITRF process when amounts exceed NZD 50,000 equivalent or when a payment crosses the border.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), operating in conjunction with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and the Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) for AML/CFT supervision; New Zealand maintains a fully deregulated foreign exchange environment with no separate foreign exchange control authority.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium for ancillary payment collection lines; Low as the sole primary banking relationship. Wise NZ Limited is a registered bank and can provide a NZD account with strong cross-border rails, while Airwallex NZ Limited, Ebury NZ, and OFX are licensed Financial Service Providers and registered AML/CFT reporting entities under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008; however, they are not prudentially regulated as banks and cannot issue a settlement account for the target payment rails (Stripe, Paddle) without a deeper bank aggregate relationship. Legal constraints: any offshore digital EMI not incorporated or licensed in New Zealand is prohibited from holding client funds or providing a local NZD settlement account without a New Zealand AML/CFT registration and, if deposit-taking, a banking license. In practice, an International Company should open a primary corporate account with a tier-1 bank and use fintech EMIs only as sub-service treasury nodes for FX conversion and vendor payables.

Legal constraints: any offshore digital EMI not incorporated or licensed in New Zealand is prohibited from holding client funds or providing a local NZD settlement account without a New Zealand AML/CFT registration and, if deposit-taking, a banking license. In practice, an International Company should open a primary corporate account with a tier-1 bank and use fintech EMIs only as sub-service treasury nodes for FX conversion and vendor payables.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not prescribe a fixed local currency threshold requiring automatic Local File or Master File preparation; however, taxpayers must comply with transfer pricing documentation requirements based on the size and risk of cross-border related-party transactions. In practice, contemporaneous documentation is required for large multinational groups (typically consolidated group turnover exceeding NZD 500 million) with significant related-party dealings; smaller companies must maintain sufficient documentation proportionate to the complexity of cross-border transactions to demonstrate arm's-length compliance. Inland Revenue may request documentation for any transaction exceeding NZD 1 million.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand's substantive general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s BG 1 Income Tax Act 2007 applies to void tax avoidance arrangements. For financial arrangements, the financial arrangements rules (s EW) require accrual-based spreading of income and expenditure. Although New Zealand does not have EU-style Economic Substance Regulations, the Inland Revenue applies a commercial substance test to determine residency, permanent establishment, and source of income of offshore companies managed from New Zealand. For an International Company (IC) structured as a non-resident entity, control and management exercised from New Zealand will create tax residency under New Zealand law, unless a DTA tie-breaker assigns residency elsewhere; thus a bare corporate structure without substantive activity in New Zealand or with management from New Zealand may trigger full New Zealand taxation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Tax Avoidance

Statutory sanction: Under Tax Administration Act 1994, a company is liable to a penalty of 150% of the tax shortfall for evasion or an abusive tax position, plus use-of-money interest. Criminal prosecution may result in fines up to NZD 50,000 per offense plus forfeiture of profits. Inland Revenue may also impose a civil penalty for not maintaining required tax documents.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable under the Tax Administration Act for tax withheld and not paid (e.g., RWT, NRWT, PAYE) under director penalty notices. Additionally, directors who participate in the use of a company to evade tax can be jointly and severally liable for the company's unpaid tax. Fraudulent conduct is punishable by imprisonment not exceeding 5 years. Courts may impose travel bans and asset freezing orders.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may assess that the entity is a New Zealand tax resident because its central management and control is exercised in New Zealand, or that it has a permanent establishment in New Zealand through dependent agent or fixed place of business. The full 28% corporate tax applies to global income sourced from New Zealand and worldwide income if resident. Penalties for non-filing of an IR4 after assessment include daily penalties up to NZD 500 per month and UOMI. If the structure is a tax avoidance arrangement, the GAAR applies to void the arrangement and re-characterize the income, with penalty of 40% or 100% of the shortfall, depending on whether the dominant purpose is tax avoidance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for tax shortfalls where they signed a tax return knowing it was false; penalties may be imposed on the officer under s 139B Tax Administration Act; for serious non-compliance, imprisonment up to 5 years for aiding and abetting tax evasion. Inland Revenue may also pursue personal recovery from directors for asset-stripped companies under the Companies Act 1993.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Artificial TSM Attribution

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue can adjust the taxable income to reflect an arm's-length price under s GC 6 (transfer pricing). A documented shortfall penalty applies: 20% of the resulting tax shortfall if the transaction is deemed not at arm's length due to inaccurate documentation; 40% if there is an 'abusive tax position' and the taxpayer knew or ought to have known the pricing was not arm's length; up to 100% for evasion. Interest on underpaid tax compounds. Transfer pricing adjustment may also trigger NRWT withholding adjustments on any deemed dividends.

Director personal liability: Directors as 'controllers' of the company's pricing policy may be personally penalized if they actively participate in setting false transfer pricing documentation. In severe cases, criminal liability arises under 'dishonesty' provisions of the Crimes Act 1961 (obtaining pecuniary advantage), with imprisonment up to 7 years. Directors may also be liable for a culpable civil claim for restitution of any wrongful benefit.
Anti-Money Laundering / Counter-Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT)

Statutory sanction: Fines up to NZD 250,000 for an individual and NZD 2 million for a company for each failure in the AML/CFT civil penalty regime; criminal penalties for a person who knowingly leaves proceeds of crime in an account may attract imprisonment up to 7 years. The Financial Markets Authority and Reserve Bank of New Zealand enforce the regime.

Director personal liability: Directors can be prosecuted as 'senior managers' under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009; personal fines up to NZD 250,000 per offense and imprisonment up to 2 years for a senior manager who knowingly fails to report a suspicious transaction. A civil pecuniary penalty of up to NZD 2 million can be imposed on an individual, and the director may be disqualified from managing any financial service provider.
Shell Company / Nominee Director Abuses

Statutory sanction: Under the Companies Act 1993, the Registrar of Companies can deregister the company. Fines for failing to file an annual return: NZD 1,000 per month for a company, and NZD 500 for every director per month. Providing false information to the Registrar carries a fine up to NZD 10,000 per offense, and imprisonment up to 5 years for fraudulent filing. Inland Revenue can also access company and bank records under compulsory information powers.

Director personal liability: Directors who are knowingly parties to the carrying on of a business recklessly or fraudulently may be personally responsible for all debts and liabilities of the company under s 322 Companies Act 1993. In a tax context, directors who breach their duties may be exposed to a civil penalty of up to NZD 200,000 and disqualification, plus criminal restitution.
