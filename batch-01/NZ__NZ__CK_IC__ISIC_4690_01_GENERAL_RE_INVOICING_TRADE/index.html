Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

Registrar of International Companies, Financial Supervisory Commission of the Cook Islands (FSC), Avarua, Rarotonga, Cook Islands; for any New Zealand-facing non-resident registration, the Companies Office of the New Zealand Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (NZ MBIE) and Inland Revenue Department (IRD) act as the national ancillary authorities.

Governing statute

Cook Islands International Companies Act 2012 (Act No. 19 of 2012), as amended by the International Companies (Amendment) Act 2018; Part 2 and related provisions: s 8 (application for incorporation), s 9 (certificate of incorporation), s 10 (licensed registered agent), s 11 (registered office), s 15 (share capital), s 16 (shareholders), s 17 (directors), s 23 (exemptions from domestic law). The Cook Islands is a self-governing territory in free association with New Zealand within the Realm of New Zealand, and the International Companies Act operates as the statutory charter for the Tax-Exempt Zone International Company regime; any operation maintaining a physical establishment in New Zealand must additionally satisfy the New Zealand Companies Act 1993 for any branch registration and the Income Tax Act 2007 for the transparent non-resident treatment.

Formation / constitutional filings

Form 1 - Application for Incorporation of an International Company, filed together with the Constitution (Articles of Incorporation) and the Registered Agent's consent and Registered Office consent, pursuant to s 8 of the Cook Islands International Companies Act 2012; issuance evidenced by the Certificate of Incorporation.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed by the Cook Islands International Companies Act 2012; incorporation may proceed with a single fully-paid no-par-value share subscribed by the initial member. There is no requirement to place capital in escrow, no capital deposit account with the Registrar, and no minimum currency threshold. Share capital may be expressed in any freely convertible currency, and non-resident ownership is fully permitted without exchange control restrictions.

Funding & unlock path: For the office-only and remote trading profile, the civil defense and occupational clearance comprises: (i) a valid fire safety certificate for the leased premises issued by the Cook Islands Fire Service for any Rarotonga office, or, for any New Zealand premises, a Building Warrant of Fitness and an approved Fire Evacuation Scheme registered with Fire and Emergency New Zealand (FENZ) where persons are employed; (ii) structural load compliance confirmed by the building owner's occupancy certificate; and (iii) a general workplace-risk assessment under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 covering workstation ergonomics, electrical safety, and emergency procedures. No special hazardous-materials civil defense license is required because the re-invoicing trade involves no dangerous goods storage; if the company later occupies a bonded warehouse in New Zealand, a NZ Customs Controlled Area approval and a FENZ-evacuated storage clearance will be required before goods are received.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed by the Cook Islands International Companies Act 2012; incorporation may proceed with a single fully-paid no-par-value share subscribed by the initial member. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone: the non-resident International Company carrying on an offshore re-invoicing trade from outside New Zealand has no New Zealand income tax liability on its foreign-source turnover, provided it has no permanent establishment in New Zealand, no New Zealand-source income, and is not managed and controlled in New Zealand. If a New Zealand nexus is created, New Zealand applies entity-level taxation to the NZ-attributable income at the 28% corporate rate, but the default treatment of the stated offshore trade is outside the New Zealand tax net.

Available election forms

New Zealand has no check-the-box election comparable to the United States. Elections that may be relevant to companies with New Zealand operations include: the Look-Through Company (LTC) election in Form IR461 (available only to close-held companies with NZ-resident shareholders, therefore not available to the non-resident International Company); the consolidated group election under the Income Tax Act 2007 for eligible NZ-resident companies; the voluntary GST registration election for suppliers with taxable turnover below NZD 60,000; and the explicit hybrid-mismatch rule elections permitted by Subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007 in the limited cases prescribed by the OECD hybrid mismatch rules. A corporation cannot elect to be treated as transparent for NZ tax when it is a non-resident company; entity status follows legal form unless an LTC election is validly made.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies made in New Zealand in any 12-month period; non-resident suppliers of remote services or low-value imported goods to New Zealand consumers also become liable to register once New Zealand consumer supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in 12 months. Offshore-to-offshore re-invoicing transactions with no supply to New Zealand are outside GST and do not count toward the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under sections 11 and 140 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, a supplier zero-rates exported goods where the goods are exported by the supplier or an associated person within 28 days of supply (or within a longer period accepted by the Commissioner) and the supplier holds adequate evidence of physical export. Services to non-resident customers are zero-rated where the services are supplied under a contract with a non-resident and are not in respect of land or goods situated in New Zealand. For a pure re-invoicing hub that never brings goods into New Zealand, the supply is outside the NZ GST system rather than a zero-rated supply; the taxpayer should retain contracts, title-transfer documents, carrier records, and payment flows to demonstrate that the goods and legal risk remain offshore.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CusDEC) filed via the Trade Single Window (TSW)
  • Bill of Lading (original or electronic) or Air Waybill
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Carrier/shipper evidence of export (e.g., port terminal arrival confirmation, mate's receipt, or supplier's export certificate)
  • Bank payment evidence / Inward Remittance Certificate proving receipt of foreign currency
  • Contract or purchase order confirming the offshore re-invoicing arrangement

New Zealand has no real-time fiscal e-invoicing clearance or digital stamping regime. E-invoicing is voluntary and follows the PEPPOL standard; however, suppliers to New Zealand government agencies may be contractually required to send/receive e-invoices through the PEPPOL network. Inland Revenue accepts electronic GST returns and records through myIR, but there is no API-based pre-clearance approval of individual invoices. Physical or electronic records must substantiate any zero-rated export supply.

Inland Revenue matches GST zero-rating claims against customs export declarations and bank remittance records. If an export zero-rating claim is not supported or goods are re-imported or diverted onshore, the supply is recharacterised as taxable, GST at 15% is assessed on the invoice value, and any refund is clawed back with use-of-money interest. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 can range from 20% (lack of reasonable care), 40% (gross carelessness), 100% (abusive tax position) to 150% (evasion), with criminal liability for knowingly providing false GST returns. False customs declarations also give rise to offences under the Customs and Excise Act 2018. Export record-keeping must be retained for at least seven years.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully open and liberalized capital and current account regime under IMF Article VIII obligations. The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is freely convertible with no statutory exchange controls, surrender requirements, or compulsory repatriation provisions for export proceeds. No RBNZ permission is required for foreign direct investment, profit remittance, or cross-border trade payments. Capital transfers, interest, dividends, royalties, and trade-related flows are entirely unrestricted. The regime is a market-determined, freely floating exchange-rate system, requiring tax reporting only through Inland Revenue (IRD) for non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) and thin-capitalization compliance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. New Zealand law (specifically the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013) imposes no statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or surrender requirement for export receipts. Exporters and offshore trading intermediaries may retain all FX proceeds in designated NZ bank foreign-currency accounts or legally recognized offshore accounts. Conversion to NZD is at the full commercial discretion of the treasury function, subject solely to the standard anti-money laundering (AML) / counter-terrorism financing (CTF) reporting thresholds under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) acts as the central prudential regulator for banking institutions. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) governs market-conduct and financial services licensing. For foreign exchange (FX) conversion, remittance, and balance-of-payments statistical collection, the RBNZ holds statutory authority, while registered New Zealand banks (registered under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989) execute all FX and cross-border clearing operations under RBNZ's compliance supervision.

High feasibility for ancillary and supplementary flows, but constrained for primary trade settlement. Digital EMI platforms (Airwallex, OFX, Wise) are viable as secondary clearing rails, but obtaining a direct banking relationship with a Tier 1 NZ bank remains mandatory for the primary re-invoicing hub. Legal constraints require the digital EMI to hold a New Zealand Financial Service Provider (FSP) registration under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008, and the EMI must be an RBNZ supervised entity for AML/CTF compliance. Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory New Zealand non-resident withholding tax rate on dividends is 30% for a non-resident company or non-resident individual not notified of a lower rate. Fully imputed dividends paid by a New Zealand resident company to a non-resident shareholder may instead be subject to non-resident withholding tax at 0% if the company has sufficient imputation credits and the shareholder provides a valid notice that the company has imputed the dividend (the 'fully imputed dividend' zero-rate rule under s. RF 8 and the Income Tax (Non-resident Withholding Tax) Regulations). An approved issuer levy of 2% applies to certain interest paid to non-residents if the lender is not associated in a way that would require NRWT.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand's transfer pricing documentation is governed by Part IVA of the Income Tax Act 2007 and the transfer pricing guidelines issued under s. GC 14. There is no fixed statutory threshold that mandates a Local File by reference to revenue alone, but Inland Revenue's Transfer Pricing Guidelines (QB 16/14 and the 2018 update) state that transfer pricing documentation should be maintained for cross-border related-party transactions that exceed NZ$10 million in annual uncontrolled value, and a Master File with supporting Country-by-Country report is required for multinational groups with group consolidated revenue of EUR 750 million or more (approx. NZ$1.3 billion). The local documentation threshold commonly cited is NZ$10 million of annual related-party cross-border turnover for a full Local File; for transactions below that, taxpayers are expected to maintain contemporaneous documentation of the arm's-length analysis. A re-invoicing hub with high gross turnover but thin margins to related parties is squarely within Inland Revenue's transfer pricing scrutiny; benchmarking documentation is mandatory to support the net margin and functional risk profile.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime like the UAE or the Cook Islands IFCA. Instead, economic substance is relevant for (i) tax residence determination under the place of effective management test, (ii) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), (iii) DTA anti-abuse clauses, and (iv) the transfer-pricing provisions requiring that the transactional profit split or the residual profit allocation follow the economically significant risks assumed and the functions performed. A CK_IC re-invoicing structure with no employees, no physical office, no decision making, no bank account control, and no risk management in the Cook Islands will lack the substance to claim Cook Islands residency if the effective management is in New Zealand, and will be vulnerable to Inland Revenue challenging the arrangement as a sham or tax avoidance. For residential purposes, Inland Revenue generally considers a company to be managed and controlled where the directors exercise strategic decision-making and control, regardless of the nominal registered office.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Re-Invoicing

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, s. 143A, knowingly providing false or misleading information to Inland Revenue is an offence punishable by a fine of up to NZ$50,000 for the first offence and NZ$100,000 for a subsequent offence. If the evasion involves an intent to defraud the revenue, s. 144 of the same Act imposes a maximum fine of NZ$50,000 plus the imposition of a penal tax of 150% of the tax shortfall under s. 141E. In addition, the evasion may constitute a crime under the Crimes Act 1961, s. 307, 'Fraudulent tax evasion', punishable by up to 7 years' imprisonment. Directors and officers may also be liable for civil penalties and disciplinary sanctions, and Inland Revenue may seek the imposition of a 'persistent offender' penalty with increased fines and publication of the offending.

Director personal liability: A director who knowingly participates in, authorizes, or permits the evasion may be personally liable under the Companies Act 1993, s. 383, for knowingly carrying on business with intent to defraud creditors or for fraudulent purpose, and may be liable for the company's unpaid tax to the extent of the fraudulent activity plus costs. The Tax Administration Act 1994 does not generally impose statutory joint and several liability for corporate tax, but a director can be personally liable under the Companies Act for insolvent trading in tax debts, and under the Crimes Act a director can be prosecuted in a personal capacity. Inland Revenue may also obtain a 'date of incorporation' director ban, an overseas travel ban under the Insolvency Act 2006, or a personal criminal conviction with imprisonment of up to 7 years. The corporate veil will be pierced where the company is a sham or façade, or where the director has acted in bad faith.
Shell Company and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Although New Zealand does not have a single 'economic substance' penalty, Inland Revenue will attack the arrangement using the General Anti-Avoidance Rule under s. BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, which permits the Commissioner to disregard the tax avoidance arrangement and reconstruct the income and deductions (s. GA 1). The resulting shortfall will attract an 'abusive tax position' shortfall penalty of 40% under s. 141D, or 100% if the dominant purpose is tax avoidance and the position is not reasonably arguable. The company structure may be void as a sham at common law, and the responsible agent may be liable for fines under the Tax Administration Act for obstruction. The Cook Islands International Companies Act provides for the revocation of the international company status if the company is used for an unlawful purpose or fails to maintain a registered office and resident representative; such revocation can expose all previously sheltered income to New Zealand tax without treaty relief.

Director personal liability: Nominee directors or the beneficial owner controlling the company may be personally liable if the entity is deemed a sham and the court determines that the nominal corporate form should be disregarded. The personal liability may include the full amount of unpaid New Zealand tax plus penalties and interest. Under the Financial Transaction Reporting Act and the Anti-Money Laundering provisions, senior officers who fail to implement adequate governance may face civil penalty orders, prohibition orders, and personal fines. In extreme cases of dishonest facilitation, a director can be charged with conspiracy to defraud Inland Revenue and sentenced to imprisonment.
Anti-Money Laundering and Structuring Violations

Statutory sanction: In New Zealand, the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 imposes on reporting entities (banks, financial institutions) strict due diligence obligations. An intermediary repeatedly facilitating suspicious transactions may be considered a 'financial institution' if its business includes remittance and may be subject to the same sanctions for non-compliance: civil penalties of up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate for each failure under s. 92, criminal penalties of up to NZ$10 million for willful contraventions, and fines for money laundering under the Crimes Act 1960, s. 243 (money laundering) punishable by up to 7 years' imprisonment. If the transactions also defraud the revenue, additional tax penalties apply. The Commercial Business General Re-Invoicing Trade scope carries an enhanced AML risk tier in the jurisdictions mentioned, requiring the application of enhanced customer due diligence where counterparties are in high-risk countries.

Director personal liability: Directors of a company that acts as a reporting entity under the AMLCFT Act are personally liable for each such non-compliance under the statutory strict liability provisions: the director of a body corporate is liable for a penalty not exceeding the maximum applicable if the body corporate contravenes, unless the director exercised due diligence. Individuals knowingly involved in money laundering can be sentenced to up to 7 years' imprisonment, and the proceeds of crime can be restrained and forfeited under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. For the re-invoicing entity itself, a travel ban and freezing of assets may be imposed, and senior managers may face forced removal under the directorship restrictions.
Transfer Pricing Documentation and Penalty

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may make transfer pricing adjustments that increase taxable income by the amount necessary to reflect an arm's-length allocation (ss. GC 6-GC 14). In addition to additional tax, the taxpayer will incur a 20% shortfall penalty for an 'unacceptable tax position' (s. 141C) or 40% for an 'abusive tax position' (s. 141D) if the amount of the adjustment exceeds NZ$150,000 and the taxpayer's position is not reasonably arguable. Late filing of a Country-by-Country report attracts a penalty of NZ$20,000 per report for a failure to file, under s. 142B, plus possible daily penalties. The company's cross-border transactions may also be recharacterized under the anti-avoidance measures, and the underlying economic substance failure may trigger the loss of treaty benefits for all related-party payments.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign incorrect transfer pricing documentation without reasonable care may be personally liable for aiding and abetting the company's penalty, and under the director penalties regime a director may be held liable for the company's unpaid tax where the company has knowingly entered into a tax avoidance arrangement and has insufficient assets. A director could also face a fine of NZ$50,000 or imprisonment of up to 5 years for a knowingly false statement under s. 143A/B of the Tax Administration Act. The High Court may order asset preservation and travel restrictions pending criminal investigation.
