Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Taake), New Zealand; online portal myIR (www.ird.govt.nz), with company registration administered by the New Zealand Companies Office, part of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (www.companiesoffice.govt.nz).

E-government filing portal

For a non-resident International Company (CK_IC) not incorporated in New Zealand: (1) Determine whether the company has a New Zealand income tax obligation, a non-resident withholding tax obligation, or a GST registration requirement. (2) Apply for a New Zealand IRD number using form IR742 (IRD number application – non-individual), filed by the company or its authorised New Zealand tax agent, accompanied by the certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents, directors' and beneficial owners' identity evidence, and the address of the New Zealand agent or care-of address. (3) If the company will make taxable supplies in New Zealand, or is an offshore supplier of remote services or digital goods to New Zealand consumers, file form IR356 (GST registration) or register through myIR under section 8B of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. (4) If the company engages employees in New Zealand, register as an employer for PAYE. (5) The IRD number is the single statutory tax identification number for income tax, GST, RWT/NRWT and employer obligations; no separate VAT identification certificate is issued by New Zealand.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone (non-resident International Company; foreign-sourced income outside the New Zealand tax base; no New Zealand permanent establishment; only New Zealand-source income or withholding tax exposure if a taxable nexus arises).

Available election forms

New Zealand does not maintain a US-style check-the-box regime. Available statutory classifications and elections relevant to an International Company include: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under the Income Tax Act 2007 for qualifying closely held New Zealand companies, allowing tax transparency; (2) branch equivalent election for a non-resident company operating through a New Zealand fixed establishment; (3) hybrid mismatch reclassification and offset elections under Part FH of the Income Tax Act 2007, including adjustments for dual-entity income or deduction mismatches; (4) election between resident withholding tax and non-resident withholding tax on interest and dividends; (5) GST grouping election under section 55 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 for related registered companies. A CK_IC will ordinarily rely on non-resident status and the foreign-sourced income exclusion rather than any active tax election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies made in New Zealand in any 12-month period. A non-resident offshore supplier of remote services or digital products to New Zealand consumers must register for GST once its supplies to non-registered persons in New Zealand exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period (section 8B of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985). Voluntary registration is available below the threshold but input tax credit recovery may be limited for non-residents without a New Zealand branch or taxable activity.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods from New Zealand is zero-rated for GST where the supplier exports the goods or delivers them to a carrier for export, and the goods are physically exported within 28 days of supply. For return merchandise, reverse logistics and quality-grading services, zero-rating is available where the returned, refurbished, re-graded or quality-assessed goods are physically exported from New Zealand to an overseas destination, the services are supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply, and the services are not directly connected with New Zealand land and are not performed for another person present in New Zealand. If goods are sold, destroyed, stored for domestic delivery, or otherwise not exported after processing, the supply is subject to GST at 15%. The supplier must identify zero-rated supplies in its GST return and retain complete export evidence for Inland Revenue inspection.

  • Customs export declaration / export entry confirmation from the New Zealand Customs Service issued through the Trade Single Window, including the unique customs entry number
  • Bill of lading, sea waybill, or air waybill evidencing physical departure of the goods from New Zealand to an overseas destination within 28 days of supply
  • Commercial invoice and packing list containing the return-merchandise authorization, overseas consignee details, and the relevant classification code (ISIC 5229 / NAICS 488510)
  • Inward foreign currency remittance certificate / bank transfer confirmation showing receipt of consideration from the overseas counterparty
  • Supporting export movement records (tracking numbers, customs broker certification, carrier proof of delivery, port or airport terminal records) substantiating the true cross-border reverse logistics movement

New Zealand does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, transaction-level digital stamping, or mandatory invoice-level API clearance system for GST. E-invoicing is voluntary and follows the Australia/New Zealand Peppol interoperability framework. Registered suppliers must issue a GST tax invoice or buy-created tax invoice within 28 days of supply, containing the prescribed details: supplier and recipient names and addresses, the supplier's IRD/GST number, taxable amount, GST amount, and invoice date. GST returns are filed electronically via myIR and enforcement is conducted through Inland Revenue risk profiling, data matching, and post-clearance audit rather than pre-clearance approval of invoices.

Inland Revenue can retrospectively assess GST at 15% and claw back refunds where zero-rating is claimed without valid export documentation or where the goods/services do not in fact qualify for cross-border zero-rating. Civil shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply at graduated rates: intentional evasion or similar acts 150%, abusive tax position 100%, gross carelessness 40%, and unacceptable tax interpretation 20%. Use-of-money interest and late-payment penalties accrue from the original GST due date. Deliberate false export declarations, falsified bills of lading or remittance certificates, and fraudulent refund claims constitute evasion and may lead to criminal prosecution, fines up to NZD 50,000, imprisonment up to 5 years, and an extended reassessment period of up to 8 years. The Commissioner may also use asset-demand notices, statutory garnishee orders, and director liability provisions to enforce recovery of the clawed-back refund plus penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalized foreign exchange regime under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the Foreign Exchange Control Regulations 1986 (as amended). The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. No general restrictions exist on the purchase or sale of foreign exchange by residents or non-residents. Profit remittances, dividends, loan repayments, and liquidation proceeds may be freely transferred offshore subject only to standard anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorism financing (CTF) verification obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Capital account transactions are likewise unrestricted, though financial institutions must comply with sanctions screening and suspicious transaction reporting. There is no requirement for central bank approval or a general foreign exchange license for ordinary trade-related remittances. Non-resident companies such as the CK_IC entity may hold New Zealand-dollar and foreign-currency accounts, repatriate capital, and pay overseas suppliers without exchange-control approval. The only material hurdles are documentary: origin of funds, beneficial ownership disclosure, and underlying trade-contract verification, all of which are standard banking compliance measures rather than exchange-control constraints. Overall convertibility risk is negligible; NZD is rated among the most freely tradable currencies in the Asia-Pacific region.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement. Under the Foreign Exchange Control Regulations 1986, there is no mandate obliging exporters to convert export receipts into NZD or to repatriate foreign currency within any prescribed timeline. A New Zealand International Company (CK_IC) engaged in cross-border reverse logistics may retain export proceeds and return-merchandise liquidation sale proceeds in foreign currency accounts (e.g., USD, EUR) indefinitely. There is no obligatory conversion ratio, no time limit for repatriation, and no requirement to sell foreign exchange to an authorized dealer. For accounting and taxation purposes, however, the entity should adopt a consistent functional currency policy and recognize foreign-exchange gains or losses in accordance with NZ IFRS and the Income Tax Act 2007. Banking institutions may impose internal risk-based policies requiring source documentation for large or unusual repatriation flows, but these are not statutory surrender rules. Accordingly, the effective surrender ratio is 0% and the repatriation timeline is at the entity's commercial discretion, subject only to standard AML/CTF and sanctions compliance.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-to-High, subject to licensing and restricted-scope constraints. New Zealand does not currently operate a dedicated e-money institution licensing regime comparable to the UK FCA or EU EMD regimes. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Airwallex, Currenxie, Wise) may provide non-resident account services through licensed partners or through an authorised financial adviser / money service provider framework under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Airwallex is specifically viable: it holds a New Zealand money service provider registration and can offer multi-currency settlement, local NZD account details, and SWIFT-compatible global payment rails. However, an offshore EMI must not hold itself out as a New Zealand registered bank, and any credit-balance holding is typically not covered by the New Zealand statutory depositor compensation scheme (which covers registered banks only). Legal constraints also include: (i) strict customer due diligence and beneficial ownership disclosure; (ii) inability of pure fintech EMIs to issue or confirm documentary letters of credit in New Zealand—such capacity resides with registered banks; (iii) the CK_IC entity must verify that its corporate constitution and ultimate beneficial owners are compatible with the EMI's client acceptance policy; and (iv) any inbound NZD funding through an EMI must be routed through local clearing in compliance with the Reserve Bank's settlement system access rules. Overall, the EMI route is a strong secondary clearing rail for relatively low-value, high-volume reverse-logistics receipts and disbursements (target rails include Local ACH and Airwallex), but the primary capital-escrow and trade-finance relationship should be maintained with a Tier 1 commercial bank to preserve full LC/guarantee access.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections into the CK_IC New Zealand operational or escrow accounts must be structured as either share subscription proceeds, shareholder loans, or intercompany funding advances. Because New Zealand has no exchange control, no central-bank approval is required, but the remittance must be economically supported and fully transparent to the receiving bank. Recommended routing: (a) execute a share subscription agreement or intercompany loan agreement; (b) instruct the remitting bank to transmit funds via SWIFT MT103 with a compliant purpose code/remittance narrative such as 'Capital injection under board resolution dated [date]' or 'Shareholder loan facility agreement dated [date]'; (c) the New Zealand receiving bank will apply its own lodgement of funds and may request supporting documents ahead of credit release—these should include the corporate authorization resolution, the signed facility agreement, and the source-of-funds evidence identified in the AML/KYC defense dossier; and (d) for large amounts, use a pre-emptive 'pre-advice' to the relationship manager to accelerate compliance verification. For funds sourced from outside New Zealand, the wire should be routed to the receiving bank's NZD/foreign-currency correspondent account, and if the entity prefers to maintain USD, the funds can settle in USD or be swapped into NZD only as the business requires. All inbound wires should be completed within 1–3 business days under normal corresponding banking conditions; additional screening may add 24–72 hours where sanctions filters are triggered. Purpose/transaction codes commonly used include: for share capital—'Capital transfer' / 'Equity participation' (IMF Balance of Payments code 3100/3101); for intercompany loans—'Loan repayment / extension of credit' (BoP code 3200); for trade receivables—'Sales of goods / merchant trade settlement' (BoP code 1000/1100). Where Airwallex or another EMI is used, funds are credited to a local NZD virtual account and then swept to the primary Tier 1 account, ensuring the capital escrow account retains audit-grade provenance.For outbound trade disbursements, the CK_IC entity should deploy a layered clearing strategy: (1) SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier payments and refunds above USD 10,000 equivalent, routed through the primary commercial bank's corporate internet banking platform, enabling full STP (straight-through processing) and a precise audit trail; (2) Local ACH (New Zealand's Bulk Electronic Clearing System operated by Payments NZ) for low-value, high-volume NZD settlement payments to New Zealand-based suppliers, logistics providers, and refund recipients, with settlement on a deferred net basis within one business day; (3) Airwallex or other EMI multi-currency rails for cost-effective cross-border SME payments, supporting USD, EUR, GBP, AUD, and CNY settlement without excessive correspondent chain deductions; and (4) Commercial Letters of Credit (LC) for high-value, non-returnable purchase orders from overseas sellers or returned-goods liquidation buyers where documentary security or supplier financing is required. LC disbursements follow UCP600 and SWIFT MT700 issuance; the issuing bank (e.g., ANZ, ASB, BNZ, or Westpac) will require a trade-finance facility or collateral coverage. For reverse-logistics refund disbursements to end consumers, the entity should use local payout rails—including direct credit to NZ bank accounts via Bulk Electronic Clearing System or international refunds via MT103 or an EMI payout API—always referencing the original transaction and avoiding cash-equivalent transfers in high-risk jurisdictions. Clearing-rail selection matrix: amounts up to NZD 50,000 and domestic beneficiaries: Local ACH; cross-border amounts up to USD 50,000: EMI/aggregator; any amount above USD 50,000 or requiring documentary records: SWIFT MT103; supplier requiring financing or large-batch trade settlement: Commercial LC. Payment purpose codes should accurately reflect underlying commercial activity: 'Payment for return logistics services', 'Refund for cancelled/rejected merchandise', 'Purchase of returned/graded goods', and 'Transportation and freight charges' (BoP codes 2000 for services, 1000 for goods).Deliverable FX forwards on NZD/USD, NZD/EUR, NZD/GBP, NZD/AUD, and NZD/CNH with licensed New Zealand registered banks (ANZ, ASB, BNZ, Westpac, and other authorized dealers), typically up to 12-month tenors without central-bank approval, used solely to hedge transactional currency exposures arising from reverse-logistics receivables and payables.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) on restricted or less-liquid cross-currency pairs (e.g., NZD vs emerging-market currencies) executed with international bank counterparties, cash-settled in a deliverable base currency, permissible where the underlying trade exposure is demonstrable and the counterparty accepts the CK_IC's corporate treasury mandate.Cross-currency swaps (FX swaps) between NZD and major tradable currencies (USD, EUR, GBP) for managing liquidity across accounts and neutralizing temporary mismatches between inbound receipts and outbound disbursements, limited to notional amounts equal to projected net exposure over the swap tenor.Vanilla FX options (call/put on NZD or foreign currency) purchased from Tier 1 banks for downside protection on material trade payables/receivables, including collar strategies, provided no speculative option-selling is undertaken and the strategy is documented in the board treasury policy.Foreign-currency deposit hedging: maintaining multi-currency accounts in the settlement currency to reduce the need for active hedging, combined with natural-offset netting of receivables and payables in the same currency—classified as a permissible non-derivative hedging practice.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium-to-High, subject to licensing and restricted-scope constraints. New Zealand does not currently operate a dedicated e-money institution licensing regime comparable to the UK FCA or EU EMD regimes. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Airwallex, Currenxie, Wise) may provide non-resident account services through licensed partners or through an authorised financial adviser / money service provider framework under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Airwallex is specifically viable: it holds a New Zealand money service provider registration and can offer multi-currency settlement, local NZD account details, and SWIFT-compatible global payment rails. However, an offshore EMI must not hold itself out as a New Zealand registered bank, and any credit-balance holding is typically not covered by the New Zealand statutory depositor compensation scheme (which covers registered banks only). Legal constraints also include: (i) strict customer due diligence and beneficial ownership disclosure; (ii) inability of pure fintech EMIs to issue or confirm documentary letters of credit in New Zealand—such capacity resides with registered banks; (iii) the CK_IC entity must verify that its corporate constitution and ultimate beneficial owners are compatible with the EMI's client acceptance policy; and (iv) any inbound NZD funding through an EMI must be routed through local clearing in compliance with the Reserve Bank's settlement system access rules. Overall, the EMI route is a strong secondary clearing rail for relatively low-value, high-volume reverse-logistics receipts and disbursements (target rails include Local ACH and Airwallex), but the primary capital-escrow and trade-finance relationship should be maintained with a Tier 1 commercial bank to preserve full LC/guarantee access.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under New Zealand's transfer pricing rules (sections GC 13 to GC 15B of the Income Tax Act 2007 and the transfer pricing guidelines), documentation is required for cross-border related-party arrangements. A Local File / Master File is mandatory where the multinational group has New Zealand revenue exceeding NZD 100 million (CbCR threshold) or where the group operates significant cross-border related party dealings; for other taxpayers, contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation must be retained for all cross-border related-party transactions above NZD 10 million (for services) and above NZD 1 million for other cross-border related-party dealings such as loans, intellectual property, and goods, to evidence compliance with the arm's length principle.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply the OECD Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) of the Gulf Cooperation Council; however, New Zealand's anti-avoidance rules require economic substance and commercial rationale for intercompany arrangements. The 'business facilitation' and general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) in section BG 1 and sections GA 1 and GB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 may apply to artificial or contrived cross-border arrangements that lack commercial substance. Thin capitalisation rules under sections FE 1-FE 32 also apply to non-resident owned companies, limiting interest deductions where debt-to-equity ratios exceed 60% (generally) or 75% for certain conduit and approved issues. The OECD Multilateral Instrument and New Zealand domestic provisions require the principal purpose test for treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under sections 143B and 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994; penalty of up to NZD 50,000 for a company for evasion or similar offence and, on indictment, imprisonment up to 5 years for individuals knowingly involved. Civil shortfall penalties up to 150% of the tax shortfall for evasion, 100% for abusive tax position, and 40% for lack of reasonable care, plus use-of-money interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers involved in or knowingly permitting tax evasion may be personally prosecuted (section 149A TAA), fined, and imprisoned; the corporate veil may be pierced under the Companies Act 1993 and common law where the company is used as a façade for fraud; directors may be subject to civil restitution claims by Inland Revenue and travel bans or asset freezing orders under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.
Shell Company and Artificial Arrangements

Statutory sanction: GAAR recast under section GB 1 and section BG 1; voidance of tax benefit, reassessment of arm's length profits, shortfall penalties of 100% for abusive tax position, and denial of treaty benefits under the principal purpose test; disclosure rules under sections 20 and 28 of the Tax Administration Act may require disclosure of avoidance arrangements with penalties of up to NZD 20,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who sanction or implement such artificial arrangements are jointly and severally liable for the company's shortfall penalties under section 149A TAA if they knew or ought reasonably to have known of the avoidance or evasion; directors may face disqualification under the Companies Act 1993 and criminal prosecution for participating in a dishonest tax avoidance scheme.
AML/CFT Breach and Structuring

Statutory sanction: Penalties under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009: civil penalties up to NZD 2 million for a body corporate per breach, and for serious or repeated breach, criminal liability with fines and potential imprisonment of up to 2 years for senior officers; transaction reporting under the Financial Transactions Reporting Act 1996 (repealed but relevant for historic) and reporting of suspicious transactions.

Director personal liability: AML/CFT supervisors and directors are individually liable where they failed to exercise due diligence in implementing the AML/CFT programme; the court may order civil penalties against the natural person (up to NZD 100,000 under the previous regime; higher under current penalties), and a bank or financial institution may terminate the merchant facility; directors may be banned from managing an entity under the Companies Act and subjected to pecuniary penalties.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: Adjustment to arm's length under sections GC 13-GC 15B; disallowance of excessive interest deductions under thin capitalisation rules (excess debt percentage > 60%); shortfall penalty of 40% for lack of reasonable care, 100% for abusive tax position, or 150% for evasion; late payment interest; potential disputes and binding rulings with IRD.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for the company's tax shortfall penalties if they knowingly promoted incorrect transfer pricing positions; personal liability under section 149A TAA and possible criminal liability under section 143A; travel restrictions may apply under the Tax Administration Act 1994 sections 176-179 (departure prohibitions) for tax debtors.
