Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Te Tari Taake / IRD), Wellington, New Zealand; secure online portal: myIR (myir.ird.govt.nz); supplementary company registration portal: New Zealand Companies Office (companies-register.companiesoffice.govt.nz) and New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) registry.

E-government filing portal

For a New Zealand-incorporated International Company (CK_IC): (i) On incorporation under the Companies Act 1993, the Registrar of Companies automatically issues a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and the company is data-matched to receive a statutory IRD number (income tax file number) directly from the IRD; (ii) if the entity is a foreign-incorporated IC migrating into New Zealand or a non-resident company, the responsible person must apply for an IRD number using the IRD's non-resident company application process (form IR594 or the online 'Apply for an IRD number' service within myIR), supplying the certificate of incorporation, registered office details, director and ultimate beneficial owner particulars, and a New Zealand address for service (c/o a tax agent or accounting firm); (iii) GST registration is a separate election made through myIR (form GST1A or the online 'Register for GST' service) upon exceeding the NZD 60,000 taxable-supplies threshold, or voluntarily to recover input tax on New Zealand acquisition costs; (iv) on approval, the IRD issues the GST registration date and filing frequency (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly); (v) a non-resident IC with no physical New Zealand presence must appoint a New Zealand-based agent for service of notices to satisfy GST collection and enforcement requirements under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Tax ID type & application path: Tax_Exempt_Zone (Non-Resident Controlled Offshore Company): A CK_IC incorporated in New Zealand is statutorily tax-resident under s YD 2 of the Income Tax Act 2007 unless its head office, centre of management, and control are all situated outside New Zealand. Where those territorial tests are satisfied, the IC is classified as a non-resident company taxable only on New Zealand-source income (ss YD 2(2) and YD 4), while foreign-source income — such as the offshore purchase, processing, and on-sale of recycled scrap copper wire — falls outside the New Zealand tax net. The entity therefore operates as a tax-neutral exempt-zone vehicle for cross-border circular-metals trading provided no New Zealand permanent establishment, fixed place of business, or local source nexus is created; any New Zealand-source income would be taxed at the entity level at the 28% corporate rate (no general pass-through entitlement) unless a Look-Through Company election is separately made. — For a New Zealand-incorporated International Company (CK_IC): (i) On incorporation under the Companies Act 1993, the Registrar of Companies automatically issues a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and the company is data-matched to receive a statutory IRD number (income tax file number) directly from the IRD; (ii) if the entity is a foreign-incorporated IC migrating into New Zealand or a non-resident company, the responsible person must apply for an IRD number using the IRD's non-resident company application process (form IR594 or the online 'Apply for an IRD number' service within myIR), supplying the certificate of incorporation, registered office details, director and ultimate beneficial owner particulars, and a New Zealand address for service (c/o a tax agent or accounting firm); (iii) GST registration is a separate election made through myIR (form GST1A or the online 'Register for GST' service) upon exceeding the NZD 60,000 taxable-supplies threshold, or voluntarily to recover input tax on New Zealand acquisition costs; (iv) on approval, the IRD issues the GST registration date and filing frequency (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly); (v) a non-resident IC with no physical New Zealand presence must appoint a New Zealand-based agent for service of notices to satisfy GST collection and enforcement requirements under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone (Non-Resident Controlled Offshore Company): A CK_IC incorporated in New Zealand is statutorily tax-resident under s YD 2 of the Income Tax Act 2007 unless its head office, centre of management, and control are all situated outside New Zealand. Where those territorial tests are satisfied, the IC is classified as a non-resident company taxable only on New Zealand-source income (ss YD 2(2) and YD 4), while foreign-source income — such as the offshore purchase, processing, and on-sale of recycled scrap copper wire — falls outside the New Zealand tax net. The entity therefore operates as a tax-neutral exempt-zone vehicle for cross-border circular-metals trading provided no New Zealand permanent establishment, fixed place of business, or local source nexus is created; any New Zealand-source income would be taxed at the entity level at the 28% corporate rate (no general pass-through entitlement) unless a Look-Through Company election is separately made.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box election akin to the U.S. regime. Statutory elections available to the IC include: (i) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under s HB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, conferring tax transparency for a closely held IC subject to the 28% corporate-rate cap and loss-limitation rules; (ii) qualifying company (QC) election (grandfathered regime; unavailable to newly incorporated companies); (iii) GST filing-frequency elections (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly) and the payment-basis election under s 19 of the GST Act 1985; (iv) hybrid and branch mismatch ordering elections under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018 to determine the priority of adjustments as between New Zealand and the counterpart jurisdiction; (v) transitional residence election under s YD 4 for a newly incorporated NZ company whose central management is moving offshore within 12 months; (vi) thin-capitalisation elections regarding the treatment of assets, debt, and the worldwide group for non-resident-owned entities; and (vii) an election under the financial arrangements rules to apply a notional interest method to deferred-payment export receivables.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period (actual turnover), or a reasonable expectation of exceeding NZD 60,000 in the following 12 months (s 51 of the GST Act 1985). Voluntary registration is available to non-resident businesses, including the international scrap trader, to recover input tax on New Zealand-sourced acquisition costs; separately, offshore suppliers of remote/digital services and low-value imported goods (LVI) supplied to New Zealand consumers are liable to register under the special offshore-supplier registration regime once their New Zealand supplies exceed NZD 60,000 per annum.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of exported goods applies under s 11(1)(a) of the GST Act 1985 where the CK_IC (as supplier) exports the recycled scrap copper wire from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply and retains sufficient evidence of export evidencing physical removal from New Zealand. Additionally, a supply of scrap to a non-resident purchaser is zero-rated under the 'exports by purchaser' limb where the contract is on f.o.b., c.i.f., or similar terms and the purchaser (or its agent) exports the goods within 28 days. Zero-rating also extends under s 11A to services performed wholly outside New Zealand for a non-resident recipient. In the recycled-scrap trade, the supplier may claim input tax on secondhand goods acquired from non-registered vendors under Part IV of the GST Act, provided the statutory record-keeping and export-evidence conditions are satisfied, enabling the exporter to claim a refund of embedded GST. The zero rate is denied if the goods are diverted, consumed, or further processed in New Zealand before export, or if the IRD determines the export is a contrived arrangement for refund leakage.

  • Original or certified copy of the Bill of Lading (B/L) or Sea Waybill showing the New Zealand port of loading, the vessel, and the foreign port of discharge for the cargo
  • Commercial invoice and detailed packing list describing the shredded non-ferrous scrap, net/gross weights, copper content, and consignee details
  • The IRD's 'Evidence of Export' schedule maintained under s 11(1)(a), including the shipping line's on-board date and the export manifest
  • Insurance certificate and freight forwarder's certificate (FIATA or equivalent) for c.i.f./c.i.p. contracts
  • Copy of the documentary letter of credit (L/C) or SWIFT MT103/TT remittance advice evidencing the inward payment terms and the 180-day collection cycle
  • Waste export authorisation or permits under the Waste Minimisation Act 2008 and Basel Convention pre-notification/consent documents where applicable
  • Port weighbridge / weighmaster certificates and container tally sheets for the shredded scrap quantity
  • Certificate of origin and, where required, the destination-country import licence for recycled copper material
  • Inward money transfer certificate (IMTC) issued by an authorised New Zealand bank where proceeds are repatriated through the New Zealand banking system

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or a-priori government invoice authorisation is imposed in New Zealand. The IRD operates a voluntary Peppol-based e-invoicing framework (e-invoicing.govt.nz), with mandatory e-invoicing only for suppliers transacting with New Zealand government agencies. All GST returns and supporting schedule data must be filed electronically via myIR or through IRD-approved gateway/API software; the IRD does not perform per-invoice clearance but employs post-return analytics, data-matching with the Customs Trade Single Window, and risk-based audit selection to validate zero-rating and input-tax claims.

The IRD applies strict audit scrutiny to export zero-rating claims, focusing on whether the contractual terms, physical movement, and documentary chain satisfy the 28-day export-evidence rule of s 11(1)(a). If the CK_IC fails to obtain or retain acceptable export evidence, or the goods are diverted to domestic consumption, the supply is recharacterised as taxable at 15% and the unpaid GST is clawed back with use-of-money interest (currently 8.36% per annum under s 120T of the Tax Administration Act 1994) and shortfall penalties: 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for an unacceptable tax position, and 100–150% for evasion or a dominant-purpose tax avoidance arrangement; late payment penalties accrue at 1% initially plus 4% per month for continued default. The standard reassessment period is four years, extending to seven years or indefinitely in cases of fraud or evasion. In the cash-for-scrap sector, a dedicated clawback risk exists under the secondhand-goods input-tax rules: if the IC claims input tax credits on purchases from unregistered collectors and the goods are not in fact exported, or the identity and locality of the supplier are unverified, the input tax is denied, penalties apply, and the directors and the IC's New Zealand agent may be personally liable for the unpaid GST under ss 170 and 171 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Additionally, the 180-day proceeds-collection cycle is audited in conjunction with transfer-pricing and financial-arrangements rules; overdue export receivables may attract imputed notional interest, and proceeds not repatriated to New Zealand may trigger controlled-foreign-company or permanent-establishment inquiries.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a full, free-floating, market-determined exchange rate regime with no exchange-control legislation and no prohibition on current or capital account convertibility. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account transactions, and profit repatriation of dividends, interest, royalties, and proceeds from divestment is legally unrestricted. In practice, the practical frictions on convertibility are limited to New Zealand bank AML/CFT verification, FIRD tax registration, the Non-Resident Withholding Tax or Approved Issuer Levy requirements on distributions, and the OECD common reporting framework for tax transparency. The RBNZ does not approve or block foreign exchange, but banks are obliged to report suspicious transactions and comply with sanctions screening. Transfer pricing documentation may be required by Inland Revenue where distributions or payments are made to non-resident related parties, but no central-bank convertibility hurdle exists.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand has no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules. Exporters are not required to convert foreign exchange earnings into NZD, and no prescribed timeline exists for bringing export receipts onshore. For recycled scrap copper wire exports, the exporter may hold proceeds in an approved foreign-currency account with a New Zealand registered bank, use those balances to settle offshore trade creditor obligations, or convert to NZD at market rate. The bank remains subject to the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 and must ensure the customs export entry, bills of lading, commercial invoices, and weight/treatment certificates are consistent to avoid enhanced-duty reporting. Balance-of-payments statistical data is collected by the RBNZ through bank reporting systems but does not create a repatriation obligation.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) – Te Pūtea Matua

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Relay Fit: High

Medium-to-Low. Non-bank fintechs and offshore electronic money institutions operating in New Zealand are not registered banks and cannot provide full trust/escrow or operating banking functions with the same settlement force as registered commercial banks. They may be licensed in New Zealand as financial service providers on the Financial Service Providers Register and supervised for AML/CFT purposes, but settlement is ultimately cleared through domestic bank-held funds. For a cross-border physical scrap copper trading operation, a fintech/EMI may be acceptable as a corridor collection tool receiving small trade payments, but the principal treasury structure should use a Tier 1 domestic bank to ensure LC issuance, SWIFT connectivity, RBNZ settlement, and full regulatory certainty. Offshore EMI access should be limited to segregated payment relay accounts and must never replace the primary operational bank account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity and shareholder liquidity should be routed through a New Zealand Tier 1 commercial bank using SWIFT MT103 wires from the parent or shareholder account. The purpose/payment narrative on the transfer must be explicit and consistent with the banking mandate, e.g. 'Equity Contribution to NZ IC – Discretionary Trust Funded', 'Shareholder Subordinated Loan', or 'Recycled Scrap Copper Trade Working Capital'. The bank will verify the source of the funds against the board resolution and UBO diligence files. The NZD route clears through the RBNZ high-value Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS); foreign-currency routes clear through correspondent banking relationships. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium-to-Low. Non-bank fintechs and offshore electronic money institutions operating in New Zealand are not registered banks and cannot provide full trust/escrow or operating banking functions with the same settlement force as registered commercial banks. They may be licensed in New Zealand as financial service providers on the Financial Service Providers Register and supervised for AML/CFT purposes, but settlement is ultimately cleared through domestic bank-held funds. For a cross-border physical scrap copper trading operation, a fintech/EMI may be acceptable as a corridor collection tool receiving small trade payments, but the principal treasury structure should use a Tier 1 domestic bank to ensure LC issuance, SWIFT connectivity, RBNZ settlement, and full regulatory certainty. Offshore EMI access should be limited to segregated payment relay accounts and must never replace the primary operational bank account.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% resulting from the New Zealand Income Tax Act 2007, Schedule 1, Part A, which applies to all resident companies; for the International Company operating under the Tax-Exempt Zone classification the effective headline rate on offshore-sourced distilled revenue is reduced to 0%, while New Zealand-sourced recurrent trading income is taxed at the full 28% rate.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The New Zealand statutory non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) rate on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders is 30%, reduced to 15% when the dividend is fully imputed and accompanied by a supplementary dividend; resident shareholders are subject to resident withholding tax (RWT) at 33% unless a valid IR13 declaration prescribes a lower rate; for International Companies structured in the Tax-Exempt Zone, no additional top-up tax applies when repatriating only the offshore exempt earnings.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZ$5,000,000 aggregate annual value of all cross-border related-party transactions triggers the mandatory preparation and retention of a Master File and Local File under the IR260 transfer pricing guidelines; the threshold is measured by combining purchases of shredded scrap copper, processing fees, commission payments and intercompany financing; when the aggregate does not exceed NZ$5 million the IC should nevertheless prepare a contemporaneous benchmark study evidencing the arm's length result if any single transaction exceeds NZ$1 million.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand applies an economic substance over legal form doctrine under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule contained in sections BG1 and GB1 of the Income Tax Act 2007; the Tax-Exempt Zone classification of the International Company is disregarded where the entity lacks a principal officer exercising independent judgment, a physical office retaining minutes and contract files, at least an annual board meeting controlling the metals trading risk, and real operational management over the consignment contracts; a bare shell interposed by a trusteeship without commercial substance will be recharacterised as a sham and the full New Zealand 28% corporate tax will be assessed on the entire recycled copper margin.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Financial Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 the corporate entity is liable to a criminal fine not exceeding NZ$250,000 and an evasion shortfall penalty of 150% of the tax evaded, plus use-of-money interest compounded daily; the filing of forged shipping documents also constitutes forgery under the Crimes Act 1961, carrying imprisonment of up to 10 years.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally and severally liable for the company's unpaid tax debts where false returns are filed; the courts may grant a statutory civil restitution order requiring each director to disgorge the unjust enrichment, issue a departure prohibition order preventing the director from leaving New Zealand until all penalties are settled, and impose imprisonment of up to 5 years for the principal bookkeeper's fraudulent evasion.
Shell Company and Assumed Economic Substance Avoidance

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner will reconstruct the full arrangement under the GAAR and assess the entire recycled copper margin as New Zealand-sourced business income; a shortfall penalty of 100% is imposed for the unacceptable tax position, increased to 150% where the taxpayer's behaviour involves gross carelessness; in addition, the High Court may issue an injunction to unwind the shell shareholding chain and a civil pecuniary penalty of up to NZ$100,000 for breaching the corporate disclosure obligations of the Financial Reporting Act 2013.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced under the Companies Act 1993 section 135 where directors allow the shell to trade recklessly; directors become personally liable for the astronomical assessed tax, are disqualified from managing companies for five years, and face a preservation order on their personal property pending the outcome of the civil penalty action.
Trade-Based Money Laundering and AML/CFT Breach

Statutory sanction: For the corporate entity, the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 imposes a civil penalty of up to NZ$2 million for each compliance failure and a criminal fine of up to NZ$500,000 where the failure is commercially motivated; the separate offence of money laundering under sections 243 and 246 of the Crimes Act 1961 carries a maximum penalty of 7 years imprisonment and a further NZ$1 million fine.

Director personal liability: Senior managers and directors that knowingly direct the trade-based money laundering are criminally liable as principals; the court may additionally order the forfeiture of all proceeds traceable to the false scrap transactions, issue a standing travel ban under the Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters Act 1992, and impose a 12-year period of disqualification from being a director under the Companies Act 1993.
Transfer Pricing Evasion and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The Inland Revenue Department will make a transfer pricing adjustment under section GB2 of the Income Tax Act 2007, recharacterising the sales prices to the international LME copper value and deem non-deductible the inflated cross-border financing expenses; a documentation negligence penalty equal to 20% of the tax adjustment is applied, rising to 150% for an arrangement with a dominant purpose of obtaining a tax advantage; the CbC report of the global group, if required, will be flagged for automatic exchange in all treaty jurisdictions.

Director personal liability: Directors who participated in approving the below-market sale are jointly and severally liable for the adjusted tax, interest and penalties under the director liability provision of section 34 of the Tax Administration Act 1994; in a flagrant moving of profits to a blacklisted jurisdiction, the High Court may order the director to reimburse the company for the entire lost tax benefit and to pay personal exemplary damages of NZ$25,000 for breach of the duty of good faith under section 131 of the Companies Act 1993; a prohibition order will remain in force until the full assessed amount is secured.
