Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a New Zealand incorporated limited company is a separate taxable entity and is subject to New Zealand corporate income tax at 28% on worldwide income if resident in New Zealand. Non-resident shareholders are not taxed on underlying company profits; distributions are instead subject to non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at applicable rates; no default pass-through treatment applies.

Available election forms

New Zealand has no US-style check-the-box election and no elective hybrid mismatch carve-out. A closely held NZ limited company may elect to be a Look-Through Company (LTC) under Subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, converting the entity to a flow-through basis for qualifying shareholders, provided the statutory eligibility conditions are satisfied (limited to five or fewer permitted shareholders and no corporate shareholders). This election is generally unavailable for a manufacturing company with non-resident corporate owners; the default remains entity-level taxation. The qualifying company (QC) election is closed to newly formed companies and is not available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of gross taxable supplies in any 12-month period (GST Act 1985, s 51); registration is mandatory once the threshold is exceeded or is likely to be exceeded, and voluntary registration is available for persons carrying on a taxable activity with lower turnover. For non-resident suppliers of remote or digital services, the same NZD 60,000 remote-services registration threshold applies under s 54B.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under s 11(1)(a) of the GST Act 1985, a supply of the electric cargo e-bikes is zero-rated if the goods are exported by the supplier as part of its taxable activity and are actually exported from New Zealand within 28 days (or any further period accepted by the Commissioner) after the time of supply. The supplier must be the exporter on the New Zealand customs export entry, the buyer must be resident outside New Zealand or otherwise outside New Zealand at the time of supply, and the goods must not have been used or consumed in New Zealand before departure. Zero-rating also applies to goods delivered to a carrier for direct shipment to an overseas destination and to certain services supplied to non-residents in connection with exported goods. The manufacturer must hold contemporaneous documentary evidence proving the movement of goods out of New Zealand to the overseas buyer; absent that evidence, the supply is taxable at 15%.

  • New Zealand Customs Service export entry (customs export declaration) lodged electronically through the Trade Single Window or approved service provider, showing the NZ manufacturer as exporter and the final foreign destination
  • Bill of Lading (sea freight) or Air Waybill (air freight) evidencing physical shipment from New Zealand to the overseas buyer
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or, in New Zealand banking practice, the bank-issued foreign currency receipt/credit advice confirming repatriation of the sales proceeds from the overseas buyer to the NZ manufacturer
  • Commercial invoice showing the cargo e-bike description, HS classification, quantity, sale value and overseas consignee
  • Packing list and carrier-issued proof of delivery or final shipment certificate, supported where requested by the NZ Customs export clearance report
  • Internal export records showing the time of supply, actual export date, shipment terms and insurance/freight documentation

No real-time tax clearance, compulsory e-invoicing, API fiscal stamping or anti-fraud digital stamping regime exists in New Zealand. Tax invoices must satisfy the prescribed particulars in s 24 of the GST Act 1985, and GST returns must be filed electronically using the prescribed GST101 return form through myIR. Voluntary e-invoicing is supported on the New Zealand PEPPOL BIS 3.0 e-invoicing network, but is not mandatory. Audit and anti-fraud integrity are supported by information sharing between Inland Revenue and the New Zealand Customs Service on export entries and border movements.

If the zero-rating claim is not substantiated, or the export documentation is false, forged, incomplete or does not evidence physical export within the statutory period, Inland Revenue may reopen the period, reverse the zero-rating, and assess GST output tax at 15% on the full value of the supply, plus use-of-money interest. The Tax Administration Act 1994 authorises shortfall penalties up to 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims may also expose the company and responsible directors to criminal prosecution, fines and imprisonment, and retrospective clawback for all affected GST periods.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a pure floating exchange-rate regime with no foreign exchange controls. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) does not require approval for the remittance of dividends, repayment of shareholder loans, or liquidation proceeds to foreign residents. Non-resident shareholders may receive profit distributions and capital returns freely, subject to the Non-resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) or approved issuer levy, and subject to the entity's own AML/CFT obligations. The Overseas Investment Act 2005 applies narrowly to certain land and sensitive business assets, not to ordinary operating capital of a manufacturing subsidiary, and does not restrict daily FX flows. There is no dual exchange rate, no central bank surrender obligation, and no limitation on holding foreign currency bank accounts. The actual friction consists mainly of bank-side compliance, including source-of-funds verification, purpose-of-payment descriptions, and sanctions screening, which are required under the AML/CFT Act 2009. Consequently, treasury planning can treat New Zealand as a high-convertibility, low-friction jurisdiction for both equity and debt capital movements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory surrender requirements in New Zealand. Exporters may retain 100% of proceeds in NZD or foreign currency accounts, and no minimum conversion ratio or statutory timeline applies. Receipts must still be reflected in financial statements for tax and statistical purposes, and larger cross-border flows must be supported by documentary evidence for AML/CFT compliance.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Revolut Business, or Airwallex have a limited but permissible role in the New Zealand payments landscape. They are not registered banks in New Zealand and cannot accept deposits from the public under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989; deposit-taking requires registration as a bank or licensing as a non-bank deposit taker under the Non-Bank Deposit Takers Act 2013. Offshore EMIs offering e-money accounts to New Zealand entities must comply with the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and the AML/CFT Act 2009 if they provide financial services in or into New Zealand. An offshore EMI account is not an acceptable substitute for a commercial bank account for letters of credit, capital escrow, or wholesale FX, but it can be used as an auxiliary low-value supplier payment rail. Overall feasibility rating: Medium for auxiliary payment corridors, Low as the primary treasury banking layer.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan funds should be transmitted by SWIFT MT103 from the remitting bank to a New Zealand clearing bank, referencing the exact legal name of the NZ Ltd and the NZ domestic bank account number, with a clear payment description such as 'share capital injection' or 'shareholder loan'. There are no ex-ante RBNZ exchange-control approvals required, but the receiving bank will assign a balance-of-payments classification such as direct-investment equity for statistical purposes. To satisfy capital verification requirements, the company must maintain a board resolution, an updated share register, a notarised statutory declaration confirming the source and purpose of funds, and evidence of the sender's bank account. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: mandatory where the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions equals or exceeds NZD 10 million in the income year. Master File: mandatory where the aggregate value equals or exceeds NZD 100 million in the income year. Country-by-Country Report: mandatory notification (and filing by the ultimate parent or the tax resident surrogate) where the multinational group's consolidated turnover equals or exceeds NZD 1.1 billion. Small-sized taxpayers with related-party transactions below NZD 1 million for a financial year with a total worldwide turnover under NZD 60 million are not subject to the contemporaneous documentation retention requirements, but they remain subject to transfer pricing adjustments and can be asked to produce documentation on audit. All thresholds are computed in New Zealand dollars (NZD); the documentation must be prepared in English, updated to the end of the income year, and retained for seven years.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a code of Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the mould of the United Arab Emirates or Caribbean jurisdictions, and there is no statutory minimum number of employees or physical premises. Substance is nevertheless required as a matter of tax law and anti-avoidance doctrine through six enforcement mechanisms: (i) statutory residency -- a company incorporated in New Zealand is a New Zealand tax resident (s YD 2, ITA 2007), so it cannot renounce residence by management-and-control movement without an approved migration and continuity ruling; (ii) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s BG 1) and the associated 'tax avoidance arrangement' reconstruction powers, under which an arrangement with no real commercial substance is void as against the Commissioner and recharacterised at full fair value with a shortfall penalty of 100% of the resulting tax shortfall; (iii) the BEPS-compliant transfer pricing regime (ss GC 13-GC 16) requiring the commercially rational allocation of profit relative to the actual business functions, assets contributed, and risks assumed by the New Zealand manufacturing entity; (iv) the thin-capitalisation regime (Part FE) applying to non-resident closely-held groups, restricting related-party debt to 60% debt-to-assets and prescribing a 'restricted transfer pricing' (deemed credit rating) approach to internal borrowings so that artificially loaded debt is economically neutralised; (v) the MLI Principal Purpose Test limiting treaty access where obtaining treaty benefit was one of the principal purposes of the structure; and (vi) the High-Value Dealer obligations under the AML/CFT Act for cash-intensive dealing, which, while not a tax substance rule, creates an independent record-keeping and beneficial-ownership verification requirement that the tax audit may discover. An NZ Ltd engaged in genuine manufacturing of urban electric cargo e-bikes -- with a staffed production facility, substantive assembly operations, quality control, warranty servicing, R&D personnel and risk-bearing revenue -- will readily satisfy any substance challenge; conversely, a letterbox holding company without operations will fail, and the IRD will reattribute profit, deny treaty benefits and impose penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent False Tax Returns (TAA 1994, ss 143A, 143B)

Statutory sanction: Conviction under s 143B: imprisonment for up to 5 years and/or a fine of up to NZD 50,000 per offence for any person; a body corporate is liable to fines of up to NZD 250,000; the Commissioner also imposes a civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax, and the limitation period for reassessment is extended indefinitely. In practice, the IRD conducts criminal referral to the Serious Fraud Office for falsification of books, and assets obtained from the offence are subject to restraint and forfeiture under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or cause the false return are personally exposed to prosecution as principals or parties to the evasion (aiding, abetting, counselling or procuring), with imprisonment up to 5 years and personal fines; on conviction a director is disqualified from managing a company for up to 5 years under s 383 Companies Act 1993. Inland Revenue may issue a Departure Prohibition Order under s 158 TAA restraining such a director from leaving New Zealand until all assessed tax, penalties and interest are paid, and the director will be jointly and severally liable for the tax shortfall where the liability was incurred by reckless trading within the meaning of s 135 Companies Act 1993.
Artificial Tax Avoidance and Abusive Tax Positions (GAAR, s BG 1 ITA 2007)

Statutory sanction: The arrangement is void as against the Commissioner; the IRD reconstructs the transaction at arm's-length consideration (including substituting a notional arm's-length interest rate on related-party debt), disallowance of deductions, and the shortfall penalty for an abusive tax position (100% of the tax shortfall) or, for an unacceptable tax position, 20%; interest and late payment penalties accrue from the original due date. The time bar that otherwise limits reassessment to four years does not apply to avoidance arrangements.

Director personal liability: Directors who designed or authorised the arrangement can be assessed personally for the company's tax shortfall in the context of insolvency under s 300 Companies Act 1993 (liability for reckless trading), and the IRD will name them in civil proceedings and seek contribution; criminal liability for 'evasion' arises where the arrangement is accompanied by concealment or false documentation. An interdiction order may be lodged against company assets to prevent profit-stripping distributions during the investigation.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Off-Market Intercompany Pricing

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue adjusts taxable income using the arm's-length principle under s GC 13; the taxpayer is denied deduction for unpaid offshore interest (s GC 14); the thin-capitalisation rules limit interest to a 60% debt-to-asset ratio or the worldwide group ratio and recharacterise excess interest as a dividend; shortfall penalties apply at 20% (unacceptable tax position), 40% (gross carelessness), or 100% (abusive tax position) of the reconstructed tax shortfall. Documentation is also a condition for mitigation: ILT/IRD practice requires that documentation be prepared before the tax return is filed to qualify for penalty relief on a 'reasonable care' defence.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign a return knowing that the transfer pricing position is unsupportable are personally exposed to the evasion offence; in a liquidation, the Commissioner can seek a personal liability order for the company's tax debt against directors who failed to maintain tax records or who allowed the company to trade while insolvent (s 135-136 Companies Act 1993), and may proceed against the directors' assets through civil proceedings. A travel ban remains available for unpaid assessments.
Profit Repatriation Without Withholding / Mischaracterised Dividends and Interest

Statutory sanction: The withheld NRWT not deducted is recovered from the payer, together with penalties equal to 100% of the unreported amount in aggravated cases; the payment is recharacterised as a dividend or royalty subject to gross-up; the interest deduction is denied; and use-of-money interest and late payment penalties are added. If the arrangement involved sham documentation, the directors face criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorised the payment knowing it was a taxable distribution become personally liable for the company's withholding obligation to the extent the company is unable to pay, and are jointly liable for any penalties; the departure prohibition order can be used as a collection tool, and criminal dishonesty charges can be pursued under the Crimes Act 1961 for obtaining a pecuniary advantage by deception.
Shell Company Structure, Poor Beneficial-Ownership Transparency and MLI/PPT Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Under the OECD MLI Principal Purpose Test, treaty benefits are denied and the 30% NRWT is restored; the GAAR voids the arrangement; shortfall penalties at the 100% 'abusive tax position' level apply; the company is subject to strike-off by the Registrar of Companies under s 318 Companies Act 1993; and the anti-money-laundering reporting obligation for beneficial owners exposes the structure to AML/CFT penalties of up to NZD 2 million for a body corporate for non-reporting.

Director personal liability: A director of a shell company who is a party to the sham may be personally liable for the tax shortfall under the universal anti-avoidance jurisprudence, banned from managing companies for up to 5 years, and restrained from travel under departure prohibition orders; where funds were laundered through the shell, the director faces money-laundering offences under s 243 Crimes Act 1961 carrying a maximum of 7 years' imprisonment.
AML/CFT High-Value Dealer and Cash Transaction Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, non-compliance with a regulation attracts civil penalty up to NZD 200,000 for a natural person and NZD 2 million for a body corporate; for reckless or deliberate contravention of a fundamental obligation, penalties are up to NZD 400,000 / NZD 4 million, and criminal liability of up to 2 years' imprisonment and NZD 300,000 fines applies; the Department of Internal Affairs may also censure and require independent audit of the compliance programme.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers are individually liable for a failure to supervise or a deliberate breach; the Court can make banning orders disqualifying them from acting as a director for a period the Court fixes; the underlying criminal proceeds can be restrained under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, and the AML regulator routinely refers tax evasion and money-laundering cases to the Financial Intelligence Unit and the Serious Fraud Office, resulting in travel-banned status and confiscation of personal assets.
