Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% applies to goods physically exported from New Zealand by or on behalf of the supplier, and to services supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand when the direct benefit of the services is enjoyed outside New Zealand. For the Speciality Tea Salon/Lounge, eligible zero-rated exports include loose-leaf tea, high-end tea ware and ceramic products sent to overseas customers, as well as related remote consulting or cataloguing services provided to foreign buyers. Domestic supplies of prepared tea beverages, food for immediate consumption in the lounge, and serving of tea with hospitality services are standard-rated at 15%; packaged retail supplies of loose-leaf tea for home consumption may qualify for zero-rating as basic food when not supplied as part of a meal, restaurant or takeaway supply. A GST-registered exporter may claim input tax credits attributable to zero-rated exports and may obtain repayment of any resulting credit balance through its GST return.

New Zealand has no real-time fiscal clearance, no mandatory digital stamping, and no API-based government pre-authorisation for GST invoices. E-invoicing is voluntary under the PEPPOL BIS 3.0 four-corner model recognised by Inland Revenue. All GST invoices must contain the statutory content, including supplier identity, IRD number, GST-inclusive amount and supply date, and must be retained in a readable electronic or paper format. GST returns are filed electronically through myIR; no invoice-level clearance code is required for a valid invoice.

Inland Revenue may disallow zero-rating and retrospectively reassert output tax at 15% if the export documentation is incomplete, the goods did not in fact leave New Zealand, or export proceeds were not received or repatriated within the 180-day compliance window. Reassessment runs from the original supply date and carries use-of-money interest. Shortfall penalties apply under the Tax Administration Act 1994 at 150% for evasion or a similar act, 100% for an abusive tax position, 40% for gross carelessness and 20% for an unacceptable tax position. The ordinary four-year time bar does not apply where fraud or evasion is established, and criminal prosecution may result in fines and imprisonment. False export entries are also separately penalisable under New Zealand customs legislation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) enjoys full convertibility across both current and capital account transactions under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's liberalised foreign exchange framework. There are no general exchange control approvals required for capital inflows or outflows, nor for the remittance of dividends, branch profits, or royalty payments. The External Investment Act and the Overseas Investment Act only impose screening requirements for certain large or sensitive asset acquisitions, not for routine operating capital or trade flows. A specialty tea salon undertaking normal commercial activities (lease, inventory, equipment) is not expected to encounter capital account hurdles, provided all tax liabilities, including non-resident withholding tax on dividends and interest, are duly settled.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no mandatory export proceeds surrender or conversion requirements. Exporters of tea, ceramics, or any other goods are free to receive foreign currency, retain it in foreign-currency bank accounts offshore or onshore, or convert it to NZD at commercial rates without a statutory timeline. The only obligation is accurate reporting of export income for income tax purposes and, where applicable, the OECD CRS and FATCA information reporting frameworks. No ratio of mandatory conversion exists.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand – Te Pūtea Matua

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High for ancillary payment processing, Low-Medium as a primary treasury account. Offshore digital EMTs (e-money institutions) are generally permitted to offer payment services in New Zealand only if registered as a Financial Service Provider (FSP) under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and enrolled with an approved dispute resolution scheme. Additionally, the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 requires a licensed AML/CFT reporting entity with supervision from the Department of Internal Affairs. Global EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, and OFX operate through New Zealand bank partnerships or hold FSP status; however, they typically do not provide formal 'Capital Escrow' capacity, and rely on pass-through accounts with local banks, codifying a risk of settlement delay. For treasury-grade liquidity management and the holding of capital reserves, a direct account with a tier-1 domestic commercial bank is strongly recommended; EMIs should be annexed only for low-value cross-border disbursements where speed and FX rate efficiency are critical.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The compliant routing for inbound equity injection or shareholder loans into a New Zealand operating company is a SWIFT MT103 (wire transfer) from the beneficial owner’s bank to the two designated accounts: (i) a NZD denominated capital escrow account for the FDI equity contribution, and (ii) an NZD operational account for intercompany loans / working capital. The remittance must clearly state the ISO 20022 structured purpose code and a supporting narrative. For equity injection, use the Economic Transaction Code (ETC) 1110 for inward direct investment (equity capital) and append the phrase 'Contribution to paid-in capital – CK_IC International Company'; for intercompany loans use ETC 1120. The inward remittance should be pre-advised to the relationship manager 48 business hours ahead. The bank will request the underlying corporate resolution, share allotment documentation, or loan agreement to complete the 'source of funds' verification, and to confirm the capital is not tied to foreign exchange restrictions. For amounts exceeding NZD 1 million, a certificate from a New Zealand chartered accountant on the non-manipulation of transfer pricing may be required.For high-value and supply-chain-sensitive trade settlements (e.g., bulk tea auctions or ceramic container imports), the preferred clearing rail is a SWIFT MT103 (for straight commercial payments) or a letter of credit (LC) issued under UCP600 terms (for deferred payment assurance). The treasury shall maintain a dual-track disbursement policy: for amounts under NZD 50,000, use SWIFT MT103 with immediate execution and verified beneficiary identifiers (BIC/IBAN); for amounts above NZD 50,000 or involving a new supplier, use an Irrevocable Confirmed Commercial LC. All outward transfers must be tagged with the appropriate transaction narrative (e.g., 'Trade Payment – Import of Tea Leaves – Inv. TSL-2024-1123') and the corresponding outward ETC codes. To reduce settlement risk, settlement shall be executed via the New Zealand Electronic Settlement System (ESAS) for same-day NZD clearing or via the correspondent bank’s SWIFT network for cross-currency. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: mandatory where aggregate cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZ$3 million in an income year (Inland Revenue Transfer Pricing Guidelines 2020; Income Tax Act 2007 s GC 13). Master File and Country-by-Country Report: mandatory where the multinational group has consolidated group revenue of NZ$1.12 billion or more. Detailed contemporaneous documentation is also required for any cross-border financing, royalty, or management fee arrangement with a related party regardless of size if it materially affects NZ taxable income.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) code (as in BVI/Cayman). However, economic substance is enforced through: (i) the general anti-avoidance rule (s BG 1/GB 1 Income Tax Act 2007), which voids any arrangement with a tax avoidance purpose lacking commercial reality; (ii) transfer pricing rules under s GC 13–GC 14; (iii) thin capitalisation rules under s FE 2–FE 18 for foreign-owned entities; and (iv) the Principal Purpose Test in NZ DTAs under the MLI. A 'Tax-Exempt Zone' CK_IC with no physical presence, staff, active decision-making, or real economic activity in its home jurisdiction that purports to hold the NZ tea salon's IP or finance will be re-characterised: Inland Revenue will deem the company to be NZ tax resident if its centre of management is in NZ (s YD 2(1)(c)) and assess its worldwide income at 28%; alternatively the arrangement will be treated as a sham with profits assessed to the actual NZ operators.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under s 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994: a fine of up to NZ$50,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years per offence. In addition, a civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the tax shortfall applies for evasion, plus full payment of unpaid tax and Use of Money Interest. Under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2009, asset confiscation orders may strip the value of the tea salon business.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns, direct the under-reporting, or who knowingly benefit from fraudulent invoicing are guilty as parties to the offence and face the same imprisonment and fines. Under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 372, directors are personally liable for unpaid PAYE/GST deductions and for any penalty arising from their own actions. Reckless trading under s 289 Companies Act 1993 exposes directors to personal civil liability for the company's tax debts. Travel restrictions are typically imposed as bail conditions (passport surrender, Customs departure bans) once criminal charges are laid; serious fraud prosecutions may be accompanied by a Proceeds of Crime restraint order freezing personal assets.
Aggressive Tax Avoidance / Promoter Schemes

Statutory sanction: The general anti-avoidance rule under s BG 1/GB 1 disregards the arrangement and reconstructs the income on the NZ operator; a shortfall penalty of 100% of the tax shortfall applies for an abusive tax position (s 141D). The promoter penalty regime (s 141E) imposes an additional civil penalty equal to the tax shortfall of the arrangement (up to NZ$50,000 minimum) on persons who promote, encourage, or assist in the avoidance arrangement. Late filing penalties and UOMI continue accruing until resolved.

Director personal liability: Directors who are promoters or who knowingly participate face personal liability for the promoter penalty and the corporate shortfall penalty; no corporate veil protection is available. If the arrangement is a sham, the court may pierce the veil and hold directors personally liable to Inland Revenue for the entire reconstructed tax shortfall. Civil restitution routes include s 320 Companies Act 1993 misconduct proceedings, director disqualification orders, and contribution orders for company debts. Criminal charges for aiding a tax evasion may also carry imprisonment and travel bans pending trial.
AML/CFT Non-Compliance (Cash-Intensive Business)

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, a reporting entity that willfully fails to comply is liable on conviction to a civil fine or criminal penalty of up to NZ$2,000,000 for a body corporate and NZ$200,000 for an individual. Additional fines per violation can be imposed for each day of continued non-compliance. The Financial Markets Authority and the NZ Police Financial Intelligence Unit may seize cash and bank balances that are the proceeds of crime, and the Serious Fraud Office may prosecute for money laundering under s 243/243A of the Crimes Act 1961, with imprisonment up to 7 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers hold personal responsibility for establishing an AML/CFT compliance programme. They are individually liable for penalties up to NZ$200,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years for knowingly permitting money laundering. Directors may be disqualified from managing any risk-assessed business and banned from operating merchant facilities (POS/credit card rails). In serious cases, restraint orders and travel interdiction orders are issued to prevent the director leaving New Zealand while financial crime investigations are ongoing.
Shell Company / Sham / No Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The New Zealand tax residence rules (s YD 2, Income Tax Act 2007) treat a company as resident if it is incorporated in New Zealand or its centre of management is in New Zealand. A sham arrangement is void from the outset; the tea salon's profits are reassessed against the New Zealand operators at the 28% rate, with shortfall penalties of 20%–150%. Failure to register and file results in default assessments under s 106 and non-filer penalties of 20% of the shortage. In addition, the CFC rules (s CQ 2, EX 1–EX 21) may attribute income of the CK_IC to any New Zealand resident shareholder. The 'Tax-Exempt Zone' status of the Cook Islands entity provides no shield; New Zealand source income is always taxable in New Zealand.

Director personal liability: Directors of the sham IC face personal liability under s 372(1) of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for unpaid PAYE and GST; under s 370, directors are jointly and severally liable for the company's tax shortfall where the company was used to evade tax. A court may order civil restitution and the repayment of any distributions taken from the sham structure. Director disqualification orders under the Companies Act 1993 s 299 are routine. Travel bans (passport surrender and departure prohibition) are commonly imposed where the director retains offshore assets and faces a tax debt exceeding NZ$10,000, or where criminal charges for fraud are laid. Imprisonment of up to 5 years may be imposed for tax evasion; the court may also order personal contribution orders before discharging the company from liquidation.
