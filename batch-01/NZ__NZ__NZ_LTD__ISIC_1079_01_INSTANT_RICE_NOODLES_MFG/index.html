Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue (Te Tari Taake) – New Zealand's national tax authority; online portal: myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz/myir)

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must apply for an IRD number (tax file number) and a GST number via the myIR portal or by submitting the relevant application forms (IR742 for non-resident companies, IR356 for GST registration). The application requires: (1) proof of incorporation in the home jurisdiction (e.g., certificate of incorporation), (2) details of the entity's New Zealand business activities, (3) a New Zealand physical or registered office address (or a tax agent's address), and (4) identification of directors and shareholders. For GST registration, the entity must demonstrate that it is carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand or intends to do so, and that its taxable supplies exceed or are expected to exceed the NZD 60,000 threshold. The IRD will issue an IRD number and a GST number upon successful verification. Additionally, for export activities, the entity must register with the New Zealand Customs Service to obtain a Customs Client Code (CCC) for lodging export declarations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to the supply of goods that are exported from New Zealand by the supplier or on the supplier's behalf, provided the goods are exported within 28 days of the supply date. The supplier must hold sufficient evidence of export, such as a customs export declaration, bill of lading, or air waybill. For services, zero-rating applies if the recipient is a non-resident and the services are supplied to that recipient outside New Zealand, subject to specific exceptions (e.g., services connected with land in NZ). For the manufacture of instant rice noodles, the export of finished goods qualifies for zero-rating if all statutory conditions are met.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry) – lodged via the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or equivalent bank statement showing receipt of foreign currency payment from the overseas buyer
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Proof of export within 28 days (e.g., customs clearance date stamp)

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime. New Zealand does not require electronic invoicing for GST purposes; however, the Peppol e-invoicing framework is available on a voluntary basis. Invoices must contain prescribed information (e.g., supplier name, GST number, date, description, amount) and be retained for at least 7 years. There is no API-based clearance requirement.

The Inland Revenue may audit export zero-rating claims. If the required documentation is not retained or the goods are not exported within the statutory timeframe, the supply becomes taxable at 15% GST, and the supplier is liable for the GST amount plus interest and penalties. Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, penalties for incorrect returns can be up to 150% of the tax shortfall, and criminal prosecution may apply for fraudulent claims. Additionally, the New Zealand Customs Service may impose penalties for incorrect export declarations under the Customs and Excise Act 2018.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised and open capital account with no exchange control regulations. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not restrict the purchase or sale of foreign currency for either current account transactions (trade in goods and services, income, and transfers) or capital account transactions (direct investment, portfolio investment, and loans). There are no surrender requirements for export proceeds, and exporters are free to retain, convert, or repatriate foreign currency earnings at their discretion. Profit remittance and dividend distributions to non-resident shareholders are not subject to any prior approval or quantitative limits, provided that the company has sufficient distributable reserves and meets standard tax withholding obligations (non-resident withholding tax, typically 15% under NZ domestic law, potentially reduced by an applicable double tax agreement). The NZD is one of the most actively traded currencies globally in the G10 space, and the market is deep and liquid, allowing efficient spot and forward hedging. Banks in New Zealand are required to report certain balance-of-payments and international investment position data to Statistics New Zealand, but these reporting obligations are statistical only and do not constitute approval or conversion hurdles. The absence of exchange controls means there is no centralised FX allocation, no import/export licensing for currency, and no restriction on the use of offshore banking channels, although anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing (AML/CFT) obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 impose verification and record-keeping requirements on all cross-border wire transfers and trade settlement transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds in New Zealand. Exporters are not required to surrender foreign currency receipts to any authority or to convert them into NZD within any period. All export receipts may be held in foreign currency accounts, utilised directly for offshore trade settlements, or converted to NZD at market rates as the entity determines.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility is Medium-High but constrained. New Zealand does not prohibit corporate entities from using offshore fintech EMIs for transaction processing, and there is no exchange control that forces the use of domestic bank accounts. However, the AML/CFT Act 2009 requires every financial institution that does business in New Zealand, including money transmitters and electronic money institutions, to be registered and compliant. An offshore EMI without a New Zealand money remittance registration cannot lawfully provide payment services to a New Zealand-domiciled entity on a continuous basis. Additionally, most Tier-1 domestic banks will not fund an account held at an unregistered EMI due to correspondent banking due diligence concerns. Practical viability is therefore limited to EMI rails that have obtained a New Zealand financial service provider (FSP) registration or that partner with a licensed New Zealand bank as an agent. For treasury purposes, a registered EMI can be used for small-value administrative expenses but is not acceptable as the primary settlement rail for trade finance or capital injection verification.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity or shareholder loan capital must be routed via an authorised New Zealand bank (or a registered financial institution) using the SWIFT MT103 message. The payment should be made from the investor's bank account in the investor's jurisdiction to the company's capital escrow account at a NZ Tier-1 bank. For tax and foreign direct investment statistical purposes, the bank will require a valid purpose code. Though New Zealand does not have an exchange control purpose code framework, banks do require a description field indicating 'Subscription for shares' or 'Shareholder loan'. Payment must be accompanied by a director's resolution approving the capital injection, the shareholders' subscription agreement, and a vesting/escrow agreement if the funds are held pending completion of business launch. Once the funds clear, the NZ bank will issue a statement that the company must retain for capital verification audits. Inward foreign investment into New Zealand is subject to the Overseas Investment Act 2005 only if the transaction involves the acquisition of sensitive land or significant business assets; a standard food manufacturing company with assets under the NZD 100 million threshold does not require consent from the Overseas Investment Office. For compliance with the Companies Act 1993, share allotment must be lodged with the Companies Office within 20 working days and the consideration recorded in the share register; the bank may request evidence of this filing before allowing a dividend distribution.Trade settlements for the procurement of raw rice, seasoning inputs, and packaging materials from overseas suppliers should be executed via the following rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 wire transfers for open-account or advanced payment arrangements up to USD 200,000 per transaction, reliant on a standard purchase order and commercial invoice; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit (LCs) issued by the NZ bank (SWIFT MT700) for larger shipments, with documents against payment (D/P) or documents against acceptance (D/A) attached to documentary collections (SWIFT MT400 series) as a cost-effective alternative; (3) for immediate domestic disbursements to freight and customs brokers, the standard NZ clearing rails (New Zealand Payments and Clearing System) apply. All outbound payments must be accompanied by the supporting trade documentation retained in the bank's transaction monitoring system, including the export invoice from the supplier, the international freight booking note, and the dealer contract. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File / Master File thresholds under New Zealand transfer pricing rules (Income Tax Act 2007, s GC 13-14 and Tax Administration Act 1994, s 78CA): Mandatory TP documentation is required for material cross-border related-party transactions. New Zealand does not apply a de minimis monetary threshold for documentation preparation as such, but the IR has an administrative focus on transactions above NZD 10 million in aggregate value for large multinationals (the 'disclosed international transactions' schedule requires detail above NZD 10 million benchmark). For Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting: New Zealand resident ultimate parent entities of multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of EUR 750 million or more (approximately NZD 1.3 billion) must file CbC report; Master File and Local File required for groups with revenue above the same EUR 750m threshold where New Zealand is the ultimate parent, and for New Zealand entities of foreign multinationals above that threshold (local filing obligations apply if the group has New Zealand revenue above NZD 10 million and cross-border related-party transactions above NZD 10 million).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply OECD-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as in the UAE/BVI; however, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s BG1 and s GA1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, and judicial doctrines of sham and agency, require that any offshore corporate structure maintains commercial substance. A New Zealand-resident manufacturing company (NZ Ltd) with actual factory operations in New Zealand, local payroll, factory lease, and production activities automatically satisfies substance requirements. If used solely as a shelf entity to hold IP or channel profits without real activity, Inland Revenue may reconstruct the transaction and assess at 28%, plus penalties up to 150% of tax shortfall (under Tax Administration Act 1994, ss 141A-141D) and impose non-deductible penalties for abusive tax positions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Under Tax Administration Act 1994, s 141E, tax evasion penalty is 150% of the resulting tax shortfall; criminal prosecution under s 143A can result in a fine of up to NZD 50,000 for each offence and/or imprisonment not exceeding 5 years. Courts may also award reparation and costs.

Director personal liability: Directors may be jointly and severally liable for tax shortfalls if they knew or ought to have known of tax avoidance and failed to take reasonable steps; directors can be banned from managing companies under the Companies Act 1993, s 383, and subject to civil recovery; serious fraud offences carry imprisonment up to 7 years under the Crimes Act 1961, s 228.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: IRD has power to adjust taxable income under s GC 13-14 and re-characterise transactions; tax shortfall penalty of 20% for unacceptable tax position (with tax avoidance dominant purpose), 40% for gross carelessness, or 100% for abusive tax position; CbC non-filing penalties up to NZD 20,000 per failure. Additional interest accrues.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve false transfer pricing documentation may be personally liable for penalties as 'controllers' under s 141J if they aided, abetted, or incited the failure; prohibitions on leaving New Zealand can be enforced under the Tax Administration Act s 157 for unpaid tax, and director disqualification under Companies Act 1993 applies for persistent tax defaults.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Anti-money laundering (AML) violation under Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 can attract civil penalties up to NZD 2 million for corporations and NZD 300,000 for individuals; tax reconstruction can apply, and a company may be struck off the Companies Register for failure to comply with statutory obligations (Companies Act 1993, s 318).

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company may be personally liable for company debts incurred while trading recklessly (Companies Act 1993, s 135); criminal liability for money laundering under Crimes Act 1961 s 243 carries up to 7 years imprisonment; travel bans and freezing orders available under Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.
Failure to File Returns and Withholding Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Late filing penalty of NZD 250 + NZD 50 per day (max NZD 2,500); late payment penalties escalating to 10% after 12 months; criminal prosecution for continued non-compliance (s 143A); GST non-compliance attracts penalties of 20% on shortfall plus monthly interest.

Director personal liability: Directors have a statutory obligation to ensure company complies with tax obligations; a director can be personally liable to the Commissioner for penalties and interest under the Tax Administration Act 1994, s 165 (for GST and PAYE) – no proof of dishonesty required; travel bans (s 157) and seizure of personal assets can be enforced.
Imputation Credit Abuse / Dividend Stripping

Statutory sanction: Penalties under s 141D (abusive tax position) of 100% of shortfall; NRWT shortfall penalties up to 20%; interest; potential cancellation of imputation credit accounts (s ME 947), and reinstatement of imputation credits denied.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorise the dividend with knowledge of imputation debit imbalance may be personally liable for the company's NRWT liabilities to the extent of distributions made within 12 months of insolvency; claw-back under Companies Act 1993 s 292 (avoidance of transactions).
