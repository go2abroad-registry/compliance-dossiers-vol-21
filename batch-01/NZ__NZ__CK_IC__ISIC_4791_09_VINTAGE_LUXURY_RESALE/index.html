Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand; primary self-service portal: myIR at https://www.ird.govt.nz

        
E-government filing portal
A foreign-incorporated International Company (IC) that is not otherwise required to register under the Companies Act 1993 must apply to the IRD for an IRD number and, where applicable, a separate GST registration number. The standard procedure is: (1) determine whether the IC has a New Zealand fixed establishment or derives New Zealand-sourced income; (2) submit IR596 (Company and Other Entity IRD Number Application for non-resident companies) together with certified certificate of incorporation, memorandum/articles, board resolution appointing a New Zealand agent for service (if applicable), and proof of overseas registered office and directors; (3) concurrently register for GST under Part 8 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 if the NZD 60,000 compulsory registration threshold is met or expected to be met, or elect voluntary registration below the threshold; (4) receive the 8-9 digit IRD number and, if registered, a 9-digit GST number; (5) enrol on myIR for electronic filing and provide New Zealand bank account details to receive any GST refunds; (6) obtain a Customs client code from the New Zealand Customs Service for physical export and import of goods. A non-resident IC with no New Zealand permanent establishment and no New Zealand-sourced assessable income is generally not required to file New Zealand income tax returns, but remains subject to GST registration obligations on taxable activities in New Zealand and to relevant indirect tax obligations on cross-border transactions.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Tax_Exempt_Zone

        
Available election forms
New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box pass-through election for companies. The IC is an entity-level taxpayer under the Income Tax Act 2007 only to the extent it derives New Zealand-sourced income through a fixed establishment or from New Zealand land, natural resources, or certain services; a tax-exempt-zone IC with no New Zealand-source taxable income is effectively outside the New Zealand income tax net. Available statutory mechanisms include: (a) voluntary GST registration under s 51(3) of the GST Act 1985 even when annual turnover is below NZD 60,000, allowing recovery of input tax on costs including acquisition of secondhand goods; (b) election of GST filing frequency (1-month, 2-month, or 6-monthly) at registration; (c) election of GST accounting basis (invoice/hybrid basis vs payments basis); (d) for non-resident suppliers of remote services, election to use the flat-rate credit scheme under s 84A of the GST Act 1985, where the non-resident supplier may claim a deemed input tax credit equal to 6/23 of the GST-inclusive consideration for specified remote/digital supplies to New Zealand consumers; and (e) hybrid mismatch limitation rules operate automatically under Subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007, with no elective opt-in; the IC cannot elect into or out of the hybrid rules except by structuring financing instruments to avoid hybrid outcomes.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
15%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
NZD 60,000 - compulsory registration for any taxable activity carried on in New Zealand when total annual taxable supplies exceed or are reasonably expected to exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period; voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold. A separate NZD 60,000 threshold applies to non-resident suppliers of remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers; non-resident suppliers making such supplies are required to register and return GST unless they elect to utilise the flat-rate credit scheme where eligible.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
Zero-rating (0% GST) applies to supplies of goods physically exported from New Zealand by the supplier or an agent, provided the supplier holds sufficient evidence that the goods were exported within 28 days of the time of supply (s 11(1)(a), s 11(2)(a) of the GST Act 1985). For the online vintage luxury resale business, eligibility requires: (a) the goods are sold under a contract by which ownership passes to the overseas buyer; (b) the goods are delivered to an address outside New Zealand and physically removed from New Zealand within the 28-day statutory window; (c) the supplier retains the export documentation bundle; and (d) the supply, if the buyer is a New Zealand resident, is not supplied to the buyer in New Zealand. Where the IC acquires secondhand luxury goods from non-registered persons (e.g., private consignors), it may claim a secondhand goods input tax credit under s 20(3) of the GST Act 1985 equal to 3/23 of the purchase price, and the subsequent export sale is zero-rated, resulting in a net refund position. Services directly connected with exported goods (e.g., international freight, authentication and repair services supplied to non-residents) may also be zero-rated under s 11A, provided the services are supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply and are not supplied in connection with land or moveable property in New Zealand.

New Zealand imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, digital tax stamping, or API-based pre-clearance mandate. e-Invoicing is voluntary and operates on the Peppol network, administered in New Zealand under the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment's e-Invoicing programme; B2B e-invoices exchanged over Peppol are recognised for GST purposes. GST returns, GST adjustments, and GST refund claims are electronically filed through myIR. Paper or electronic tax invoices must satisfy the content requirements of ss 24-24D of the GST Act 1985, including the words 'GST invoice', supplier identity and GST number, invoice date, description and quantity of goods, consideration, and where zero-rated, the reason (e.g., 'export' or 'zero-rated under s 11'). For exported goods, the supplier must retain export evidence showing goods have left New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply; the IRD may require presentation of this evidence in an audit.

      
The IRD may audit zero-rated export supplies and claw back input tax credits where the supplier fails to substantiate physical export within the 28-day period or where documentation is incomplete, fraudulent, or fabricated. Consequences include: (a) assessment of GST output tax at 15% on the full sale consideration, with use-of-money interest accruing from the original due date; (b) civil shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for failure to take reasonable care or unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or similar conduct; (c) late filing penalties and prosecution under s 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for evasion (imprisonment up to 5 years and fines up to NZD 50,000 per offence); (d) prosecution under the Crimes Act 1961 and Customs and Excise Act 2018 for fraud, false statements, or procuring export clearance by deception; and (e) retrospective cancellation of the IC's GST registration and denial of secondhand goods input tax credits where the export chain is broken. While New Zealand has no fixed statutory FX repatriation deadline, market-standard documentary practice expects export proceeds to be repatriated within 180 days; the IRD and NZ Customs may examine the inward remittance trail as corroborative evidence that a genuine export sale occurred, and deviation from the 180-day benchmark increases audit risk.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

      
Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

    

    

      
Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised and freely floating currency regime, with all exchange controls abolished in 1984 under the Exchange Control Act Repeal. Both current account transactions (trade settlements, commissions, platform fees, interest, consignor payouts) and capital account transactions (equity injections, shareholder loans, dividend repatriation, liquidation proceeds) are fully convertible into NZD or any foreign currency without prior central bank approval, quantitative limits, deposit taxes, or repatriation restrictions. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is a freely traded currency settled on the wholesale interbank market via the Continuous Linked Settlement (CLS) system. Practical compliance obligations are limited to (i) the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) reporting duties, which require banks to verify purpose and source of funds for cross-border wires above NZD 10,000 or of an unusual nature; and (ii) the Overseas Investment Act 2005, which is relevant only if an offshore investor acquires sensitive land or fishing quota, neither of which applies to an online consignment retail business. Profit or dividend remittance from a New Zealand-resident subsidiary would attract non-resident withholding tax at treaty-reduced or statutory rates, but in this structure the International Company is tax-exempt in its home jurisdiction and is not tax-resident in New Zealand; therefore, settlement funds held by the IC are not subject to New Zealand source taxation on conversion or repatriation.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
New Zealand imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirements. There is no mandated timeline for converting foreign currency export or trade receipts into NZD, no minimum conversion ratio, and no requirement to channel export earnings through any designated local account. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not require exporters to hedge their earnings or hold a percentage of receipts in NZD. Commercial banks may contractually require a borrower utilising trade finance facilities to repatriate sale proceeds to its NZ settlement account as a covenant of the facility, but such requirements are commercial credit conditions rather than regulatory mandates. Consequently, the CK International Company may retain USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, or CNY resale proceeds in foreign-currency accounts, convert them at market rate, or apply them to foreign supplier payables without any surrender obligation or timing constraint.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Stripe
            Fit: High
          

          
Feasibility rating: MEDIUM for ancillary collection and FX conversion; LOW as the primary or sole settlement rail for capital escrow obligations. Legal constraints: under the AML/CFT Act 2009, any entity providing money remittance or digital wallet services into or out of New Zealand must be registered as a reporting entity with the Department of Internal Affairs (DIA), unless it operates under a registered bank exemption. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Airwallex, Wise Platform, Currencycloud, Payoneer) can lawfully service a Cook Islands International Company only if the EMI (a) holds a New Zealand registered bank license (none currently do), or (b) forms an agency/trust arrangement with a locally licensed bank that performs the acceptance of deposits and NZD clearing, while the EMI provides only the interface and conversion. Most offshore EMIs will decline a zero-tax offshore International Company engaged in luxury consignment resale because (i) the beneficial ownership typically sits behind an asset-protection trust, (ii) the business model involves omnibus escrow settlements with third-party beneficial interest, and (iii) the IC lacks a New Zealand Companies Office registration. If the IC completes registration as an overseas company under section 340 of the Companies Act 1993 and appoints a New Zealand-resident agent, a niche EMI may approve onboarding but will cap monthly settlement volumes and require daily source-of-funds reporting. Overall, an EMI can be used for margin collection via card acquirers (Stripe) but not for the custody of consignor proceeds.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: Contemporaneous New Zealand transfer pricing documentation (Master File and Local File) is required where the multinational group has annual global turnover of NZ$100 million or more and the NZ-resident entity (or NZ PE of a non-resident) enters into cross-border related-party transactions (including inventory purchases and management fees) exceeding NZ$10 million in an income year. The Inland Revenue International Questionnaire (IR1061) is the standard disclosure mechanism for such transactions. Transactions below these thresholds remain subject to the arm's length standard under s GC 13 and require contemporaneous evidence upon request.

      
Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations. Substance nevertheless controls the tax outcome through: (i) tax residence of a company under s YD 2 (incorporation in NZ or centre of management in NZ); (ii) the permanent establishment threshold under ss YD 4 and YD 5; (iii) the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) in s BG 1; (iv) the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018, which denies benefits to related-party financing and platform structures lacking sufficient people, functions, assets, and risks; and (v) the Multilateral Instrument (MLI) Principal Purpose Test for treaty access. An IC that merely holds title to vintage luxury inventory while all sales, marketing, customer support, and risk functions are performed in New Zealand will be found to have a dependent agent PE of the foreign principal, attributing taxable profit to New Zealand.

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Returns – Tax Administration Act 1994 ss 143A-143B
        
Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction: for an individual, imprisonment up to 5 years and/or a fine up to NZ$50,000; for a company, a fine up to NZ$50,000. Civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax plus Use-of-Money Interest; the time bar for reassessment is extended indefinitely where evasion exists; the Crown may also seek forfeiture of proceeds of crime under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

        
Director personal liability: Directors and shadow directors are prosecuted as principals for the company's false return. Personal civil restitution may be ordered for the tax shortfall; director disqualification up to 5 years under s 382 Companies Act 1993; travel bans are available where fugitive or flight risk is shown in an insolvency examination or arrest warrant procedure under the Insolvency Act 2006; imprisonment up to 5 years is personal.

      

      
      

        Sham / Shell Company & General Anti-Avoidance – s BG 1 Income Tax Act 2007
        
Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may reconstruct the arrangement under the GAAR: ignore the IC as a sham intermediary, attribute the full resale margin to the New Zealand principal/settlor, and assess tax at 28% plus an abusive tax position shortfall penalty of 100% and UOMI. The IC's local registered office/agent may be prosecuted for facilitating a sham; deregistration and striking off under the Companies Act 1993 may follow.

        
Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced for fraud or sham: directors are jointly and severally liable for the avoided tax as parties to the sham. Personal civil restitution orders, disqualification from managing companies, and, in liquidation, examination orders under s 261 Companies Act 1993 may compel the director's attendance, production of records, and surrender of passport; non-compliance leads to arrest. Criminal conspiracy charges may result in imprisonment up to 5 years.

      

      
      

        AML/CFT Non-Compliance – Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009
        
Statutory sanction: Civil penalty up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate per contravention; criminal penalty for an individual: fine up to NZ$5 million and/or imprisonment up to 2 years; corporate fine up to NZ$5 million; enforcement includes remediation orders, suspension or conditions on business registration, and forfeiture of proceeds of crime under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

        
Director personal liability: Directors, senior managers, and compliance officers are 'designated persons' under the AML/CFT Act. They may be personally liable for civil penalties where they aided, abetted, or failed to supervise an employee's contravention (s 100); criminal liability attaches where they knowingly permit money laundering. The court may prohibit a person from holding a directorship in any reporting entity; travel bans may be imposed in criminal proceedings when the director poses a flight risk; imprisonment up to 2 years is a personal sanction.

      

      
      

        Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalisation Non-Observance – Income Tax Act 2007 ss GC 13, GC 15
        
Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment to the arm's length price under s GC 13; shortfall penalty of 20% for an unacceptable tax position escalating to 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive position, and 150% for evasion; UOMI on the underpayment; mandatory disclosure via IR1061; possible NRWT recapture on adjusted cross-border payments.

        
Director personal liability: Directors who approved the non-arm's length pricing and signed the return may be assessed personally for failure to exercise due diligence; civil restitution of the shortfall is recoverable as a Crown debt. In insolvency, the liquidator may sue directors for reckless or negligent trading (ss 135 and 137 Companies Act 1993) for causing the company to incur an uneconomic tax liability; deliberate manipulation constitutes evasion, leading to imprisonment up to 5 years.

      

      
      

        Profit Repatriation and Withholding Tax Defaults – Income Tax Act 2007 ss NG 1, RF 8
        
Statutory sanction: The company, as agent, is personally liable for the un-withheld NRWT under s RF 8, together with the initial 1% late payment penalty, monthly 4% increments, and UOMI; shortfall penalties up to 150% apply for deliberate failure to withhold; unpaid NRWT constitutes a Crown debt ranking ahead of unsecured creditors in liquidation; late filing penalties for NRWT returns apply per return.

        
Director personal liability: Under s 151 TAA, directors and former directors of a company that fails to pay withholding tax (including NRWT) after receiving a 30-day demand may be personally liable for the entire unpaid amount, without limitation, where they have failed to procure payment or place the company in liquidation; personal civil restitution, director disqualification, and, in cases of fraudulent diversion of withheld tax, imprisonment up to 5 years and travel bans in extradition proceedings.
