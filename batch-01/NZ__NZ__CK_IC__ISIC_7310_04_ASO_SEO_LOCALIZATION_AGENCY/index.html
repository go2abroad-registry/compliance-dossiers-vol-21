Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office (NZCO), a business unit within the Ministry of Business, Innovation, and Employment (MBIE), acting through the Registrar of Companies.

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (New Zealand): sections 10–16 (application for registration and Registrar powers), section 20 and Schedule 1 (constitution of company), sections 21–24 (name requirements), sections 30–35 (issue of shares and share capital), sections 40–42 (share certificates and register of members), section 110 (registered office), sections 151–156 (appointment of directors and officers), section 244 (annual return obligations); supplemented by the Companies (Fees) Regulations 2016 and the Financial Markets Supervisory Act 2013 in respect of corporate service provider oversight.

Formation / constitutional filings

Form 6 – Application for Registration of a Company (Companies Act 1993), filed electronically through the Companies Register and New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) online portal, together with the Company Constitution and executed director and shareholder consents.

Incorporation processing time

⚡ 22 business days (SOP sum)

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is required by the Companies Act 1993. An International Company formed as a company limited by shares may be incorporated with an issued capital of one ordinary share at NZD 1.00 or any nominal value stated in the constitution. No bank escrow account, capital deposit, or paid-up capital verification is mandated by the Registrar of Companies for this entity class.

Funding & unlock path: Fire Safety Clearance: The leased registered office must hold a current Building Warrant of Fitness issued by the territorial authority under the Building Act 2004, including a Fire and Emergency New Zealand (FENZ) approved evacuation scheme where the premises exceed prescribed occupancy thresholds, together with annual fire-safety system inspection certificates. Structural Clearance: A structural load compliance inspection report or building certificate confirming the premises are fit for general office use must be retained. Occupational Clearance: WorkSafe New Zealand clearance under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, comprising a written workplace health and safety policy, hazard identification register, first-aid provisions, worker participation system, and emergency response protocols; for remote or home-based workers, a completed home-workspace self-assessment must be archived.

Paid-in capital rule: No minimum paid-up capital is required by the Companies Act 1993. An International Company formed as a company limited by shares may be incorporated with an issued capital of one ordinary share at NZD 1.00 or any nominal value stated in the constitution. No bank escrow account, capital deposit, or paid-up capital verification is mandated by the Registrar of Companies for this entity class.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD / Te Tari Taake), Wellington, New Zealand; taxpayer digital portal: myIR (Secure Online Services via www.ird.govt.nz); business identity registry: New Zealand Companies Office and New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) register.

E-government filing portal

Step-by-step procedure for a foreign-owned International Company (IC) to obtain a New Zealand IRD number and GST registration: (1) Register the company with the New Zealand Companies Office and obtain an NZBN; (2) Apply for an IRD number through the non-resident company application process (Form IR742 or myIR online application), providing certificate of incorporation, registered office details, director particulars, and, if required, a New Zealand agent address; (3) Register for GST via myIR or Form IR356, electing a filing frequency (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly) and declaring the date the taxable activity commenced; (4) Where the IC is a non-resident supplier of remote or digital services to New Zealand consumers, use the dedicated non-resident GST registration pathway under section 54B of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (5) Receive the 8-9 digit IRD number and confirmation notice; the IRD number serves as the single statutory tax identification number for income tax, GST, PAYE, and FBT (New Zealand does not maintain a separate VAT ID).

Tax ID type & application path: Tax_Exempt_Zone — non-resident territorial scope: the International Company is subject to New Zealand income tax only on New Zealand-source income (sections BD 1 and YA 1, Income Tax Act 2007); foreign-sourced revenue derived from App Store Optimization (ASO), multilingual SEO, and localization services is outside the New Zealand tax net absent a New Zealand permanent establishment (PE); no entity-level taxation applies to offshore income; any deemed New Zealand-source income is taxed on a net basis at the 28% corporate rate, with no New Zealand capital gains tax on offshore assets. — Step-by-step procedure for a foreign-owned International Company (IC) to obtain a New Zealand IRD number and GST registration: (1) Register the company with the New Zealand Companies Office and obtain an NZBN; (2) Apply for an IRD number through the non-resident company application process (Form IR742 or myIR online application), providing certificate of incorporation, registered office details, director particulars, and, if required, a New Zealand agent address; (3) Register for GST via myIR or Form IR356, electing a filing frequency (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly) and declaring the date the taxable activity commenced; (4) Where the IC is a non-resident supplier of remote or digital services to New Zealand consumers, use the dedicated non-resident GST registration pathway under section 54B of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (5) Receive the 8-9 digit IRD number and confirmation notice; the IRD number serves as the single statutory tax identification number for income tax, GST, PAYE, and FBT (New Zealand does not maintain a separate VAT ID).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone — non-resident territorial scope: the International Company is subject to New Zealand income tax only on New Zealand-source income (sections BD 1 and YA 1, Income Tax Act 2007); foreign-sourced revenue derived from App Store Optimization (ASO), multilingual SEO, and localization services is outside the New Zealand tax net absent a New Zealand permanent establishment (PE); no entity-level taxation applies to offshore income; any deemed New Zealand-source income is taxed on a net basis at the 28% corporate rate, with no New Zealand capital gains tax on offshore assets.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not apply a US-style check-the-box classification regime; statutory elections available to a foreign-owned International Company include: (a) GST grouping election under section 55 of the GST Act 1985, allowing eligible companies to be treated as a single GST group; (b) consolidated group election under subpart FM of the Income Tax Act 2007, permitting wholly-owned resident group companies to file as one tax entity; (c) hybrid mismatch elections under the Taxation (Neutralising Arising Mismatches) Act 2018, including deemed deduction and offset elections to neutralize hybrid financial instrument outcomes; (d) group loss offset election under subpart IC of the Income Tax Act 2007, enabling intra-group loss transfers; (e) branch-equivalent election under the controlled foreign company (CFC) attribution rules in subpart EX of the Income Tax Act 2007, electing to tax CFC income on a branch-equivalent basis; (f) non-resident GST registration election under section 54B of the GST Act 1985, simplifying registration for non-resident suppliers of remote digital services. No pass-through or entity-level check-the-box elections exist in New Zealand.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period (section 51, Goods and Services Tax Act 1985); non-resident suppliers of remote services, including digital marketing, ASO, SEO, and localization services, must register for GST once the aggregate value of supplies to New Zealand consumers exceeds NZD 60,000; voluntary registration is permitted under section 51(3) for persons carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand, including non-residents supplying goods from outside New Zealand.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Services, including ASO, SEO, and localization services, supplied to a non-resident person who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply are zero-rated under section 11A(1)(k) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, provided the services are not physically performed in New Zealand; remote or digital services supplied to non-resident businesses on a B2B basis are zero-rated under section 11A(1)(m); goods are zero-rated under section 11 where the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand within 28 days of supply and holds documentary evidence of export. The supplier must verify the non-resident status of the recipient and maintain records sufficient to demonstrate the offshore nexus; where zero-rating is claimed incorrectly, output tax at 15% is payable together with consequential penalties and interest.

  • Customs Export Declaration or New Zealand Customs electronic cargo movement record evidencing physical export of goods
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing transport of goods out of New Zealand
  • Inward Foreign Exchange Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds in NZD or foreign currency
  • Executed service agreement, Statement of Work, and delivery evidence substantiating the supply of zero-rated remote services
  • Recipient non-residency declaration, Tax Residence Certificate, or zero-rated export invoice containing the required statutory particulars

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, tax-authority digital stamping, or continuous transaction control (CTC) regime applies in New Zealand. Taxpayers may issue standard electronic invoices; voluntary PEPPOL-BIS 3.0 e-invoicing interoperability is supported through the New Zealand E-Invoice network for B2B transactions, but no transactional API clearance obligation exists. GST returns are filed electronically via myIR, and IRD APIs permit XML-based GST return submission and status queries. Taxpayers must retain complete electronic and physical records sufficient to substantiate zero-rated exports; inadequate record-keeping attracts the general anti-avoidance and penalty provisions of the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Inland Revenue may audit zero-rated export claims with heightened scrutiny of digital service exporters, applying a substance-over-form analysis to determine whether services were physically performed in New Zealand or supplied to a genuine non-resident. Incorrect zero-rating results in retrospective reassessment and clawback of the full 15% GST, plus use-of-money interest and shortfall penalties under Part 4A of the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and 100% for an abusive tax position. Fraudulent zero-rating or false export documentation constitutes tax evasion under section 143A and knowingly providing false information under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, carrying criminal conviction, substantial fines, and imprisonment for individuals; the standard 4-year reassessment limitation period is extended to 8 years in cases of fraud or non-disclosure, permitting retrospective clawback across additional tax periods.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

NZD is freely convertible. New Zealand maintains no exchange-control law that restricts current-account or capital-account transactions, foreign-currency account balances, or settlement of bona fide trade invoices. Profit distribution, dividends, loan repayments, interest, royalties, and liquidation proceeds may be remitted offshore upon satisfaction of New Zealand tax withholding and reporting requirements (NRWT/RLWT) but without RBNZ approval. The only material approvals are the Overseas Investment Act 2005 screening rules for foreign acquisitions of sensitive land, fishing quota, or significant business assets above the relevant thresholds; a cross-border digital marketing/localization agency transacting operational share capital and service invoices is not within those screening categories unless it is acquiring sensitive New Zealand assets or a substantial New Zealand business.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. New Zealand does not impose mandatory surrender, repatriation, conversion, or declaration deadlines for export proceeds. An exporter of app-store optimization and multilingual organic search services can lawfully invoice in USD, EUR, or any agreed currency, retain collections in a New Zealand foreign-currency account, and convert at a time of its choosing. Banks will apply standard CDD/AML checks and record the international transaction for statistical BOP reporting, but no RBNZ approval or conversion ratio applies.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ / Te Pūtea Matua) under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021; AML/CFT supervisory powers are also shared with the Financial Markets Authority and the Department of Internal Affairs, but RBNZ is the central bank for FX and prudential policy.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

High for day-to-day low-value disbursements and sub-contractor payments; Medium as a full treasury replacement. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise (Wise B2B) and Payoneer may settle low-value international invoices rapidly and integrate into the intended payment rails. Legal constraints: they are not New Zealand registered banks or licensed deposit-takers; they hold client money under safeguarding/trust arrangements rather than accepting deposits, must comply with the AML/CFT Act 2009 and Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 if operating into New Zealand, and may impose stricter UBO/substance checks on tax-exempt offshore International Companies. They should be used only for small/medium operational flows; high-value settlements, LC transactions, and strategic FX hedging should be routed through a Tier 1 bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections should be remitted by wire from the overseas shareholder's account to the IC's New Zealand bank account using SWIFT MT103, with the beneficiary stated as the IC and remittance information referencing 'Equity subscription / share capital contribution'. New Zealand banks do not use a prescribed national purpose-code table, but the customer must provide a commercial declaration in the bank's remittance form; a common safe description is 'Equity contribution for share subscription under Companies Act 1993'. For ordinary agency capitalization below the Overseas Investment Act screening thresholds and with no sensitive assets, no RBNZ or Government approval is required. The bank will perform CDD and may place the funds on temporary hold pending verification of the subscription agreement and source of funds; directors should pre-submit the corporate file and board resolution before the wire is dispatched. Large injections above NZD 100 million or acquisitions of sensitive New Zealand assets require separate Overseas Investment Act screening; this is generally irrelevant for a bootstrap or mid-cap digital agency.Cross-border vendor payments, contractor settlements, and profit distributions should be cleared through the SWIFT network using MT103 for outright client/vendor payments, MT202/MT202 COV for correspondent-bank transfers, and MT700/MT707 for commercial letters of credit and amendments. NZD leg settlement between local banks is cleared through RBNZ-operated ESAS/NZClear; USD, EUR, or GBP legs are settled via the bank's overseas correspondent network. For low-value disbursements to independent service providers and SEO localization freelancers, Wise B2B or Payoneer local-currency rails can be used as ancillary channels, provided the recipient's virtual account is in the IC's name and the transfer is matched to a signed work order. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: High for day-to-day low-value disbursements and sub-contractor payments; Medium as a full treasury replacement. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise (Wise B2B) and Payoneer may settle low-value international invoices rapidly and integrate into the intended payment rails. Legal constraints: they are not New Zealand registered banks or licensed deposit-takers; they hold client money under safeguarding/trust arrangements rather than accepting deposits, must comply with the AML/CFT Act 2009 and Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 if operating into New Zealand, and may impose stricter UBO/substance checks on tax-exempt offshore International Companies. They should be used only for small/medium operational flows; high-value settlements, LC transactions, and strategic FX hedging should be routed through a Tier 1 bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not prescribe a bright-line NZD threshold triggering mandatory contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation. However, the Inland Revenue may require TP documentation when aggregate cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZD 2 million per annum per related party. Large multinational groups with global revenue exceeding EUR 750 million (approximately NZD 1.3 billion) are subject to country-by-country reporting (CbCR), master file, and local file obligations if they are New Zealand tax residents or have a New Zealand presence.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) akin to the Cayman Islands or BVI regime. Instead, the tax law applies a general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, plus specific 'PE' and 'sufficient substance' principles embedded in tax treaty case law. To benefit from DTA protection (e.g., reduced withholding rates), the New Zealand resident payer must ensure the payee is the legal and beneficial owner of the income and has substantive business activity in its claimed residence jurisdiction. A shell company without physical office, staff, or real commercial operations may be denied treaty benefits and be re-characterised as not treaty-eligible.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994. Maximum fine of NZD 50,000 for a company; for individuals, a fine up to NZD 50,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years. Civil shortfall penalties typically 150% of the tax shortfall attributable to evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for the company's unpaid taxes under section 76 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for GST and PAYE. For income tax, directors may be personally liable under the 'quasi-security' provisions if the company's scheme of arrangement has a purpose of avoiding tax. Courts may also pierce the corporate veil for fraudulent conduct, enabling recovery of penalties from directors and imposing travel bans and director prohibition under the Companies Act 1993.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Application of the GAAR (section BG 1) to strike down the arrangement, reconstruction of the actual taxpayer, a shortfall penalty of up to 100% of the avoided tax (100% for abusive tax position), and denial of DTA relief. Fines under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 may also apply to non-compliant trust or company service providers.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company may be personally assessed for the resultant tax shortfall if they knowingly created or participated in an arrangement with a dominant purpose of tax avoidance. They may face civil prohibition orders and debtor travel restrictions where the company owes outstanding tax liabilities.
Anti-Money Laundering / Terrorist Financing (AML/CFT)

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, reporting entities (including payment service providers and financial advisers) must enforce a risk-based AML programme. Non-compliance may lead to civil penalty orders of up to NZD 200,000 per contravention for individuals and up to NZD 2 million for companies. Criminal fines of up to NZD 50,000 and imprisonment up to one year apply to wilful non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers may incur personal civil and criminal liability if they knowingly fail to implement adequate AML/CFT policies, permit suspicious transactions, or obstruct Inland Revenue or FIU investigations. Travel ban orders may be issued pending investigation of money-laundering predicates.
Unlawful Profit Repatriation / NRWT Evasion

Statutory sanction: Section 28 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 requires deduction of NRWT. Failure to deduct results in a withholding penalty of 10% of the full amount withheld per due date, plus late payment penalties (1% daily up to 10% maximum), and a shortfall penalty of 20% to 150%. The company remains fully liable for the gross NRWT amount plus penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who caused or permitted payment, or signed a false declaration that NRWT was deducted, may be jointly and severally liable for the unpaid NRWT, and may face an unpaid-recovery interdiction order (travel ban) preventing departure from New Zealand until the tax is paid.
