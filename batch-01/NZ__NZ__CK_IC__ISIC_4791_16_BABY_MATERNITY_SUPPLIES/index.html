E-government filing portal

A foreign person establishing a New Zealand corporate vehicle must first incorporate a company with the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993, obtaining a unique NZBN (New Zealand Business Number) issued by the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE). Upon incorporation, the company must apply to Inland Revenue for a tax file number (IRD number) by completing Form IR596 (Registration of a Company) via myIR or through an approved tax agent. The application must include the certificate of incorporation, NZBN, director and shareholder details, registered office address, intended business activity (e.g., ISIC 4791 – online retail sale of maternity and infant care commodities), and the expected annual turnover for GST purposes. A non-resident company that is not required to have a New Zealand tax residence may still be required to register for GST and income tax if it carries on a taxable business activity in New Zealand. The IRD number is ordinarily issued within 5–10 business days, and separate GST registration is automatically triggered upon application unless the entity expects taxable supplies below the registration threshold. Foreign directors must comply with the requirement to maintain a New Zealand resident director, and all foreign-incorporated entities carrying on business in New Zealand must register as an overseas company with the Companies Office.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (New Zealand-resident company subject to corporate income tax at 28% on worldwide income, subject to imputation; the specific International Company structure presents a Territorial_Scope / Tax_Exempt_Zone profile under which income derived from non-New Zealand-sourced offshore e-commerce sales is exempt from New Zealand income tax, while New Zealand-sourced income remains taxable at 28%. Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime, but provides the following statutory elections and treatment options relevant to this entity: (1) voluntary GST registration at any turnover level under section 51(3) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (2) GST grouping election for 100% related companies under section 55 of the GST Act, permitting a single GST return for the group; (3) GST cash basis or invoice basis election under sections 19A–19D, relevant for e-commerce payment timing (cash basis is available when taxable supplies do not exceed NZD 2 million per annum); (4) an explicit hybrid mismatch regime under Part FM of the Income Tax Act 2007 that automatically neutralises inconsistent tax treatment of instruments or entities without an elective check-the-box filing; (5) thin capitalisation ratio elections for non-resident-owned groups under sections FE 13–FE 18; (6) an election to apply the foreign income exemption rules for certain foreign equity interests; and (7) an election for the company to receive a qualifying company election (QC) historically, though this is no longer available for new companies. For the International Company, the relevant territorial exemption is mandatory rather than elective where the income is not attributable to a New Zealand permanent establishment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar is fully convertible on both current account items (goods, services, royalties, licensing fees, shipping income, and dividend remittances) and capital account items (foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, intercompany loans, guarantees, and derivatives). No prior central bank approval, currency license, or surrender obligation applies to convert NZD into foreign currency or vice versa. Profit repatriation to non-resident shareholders is unrestricted from an FX perspective, subject only to standard non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at applicable treaty-limited rates on dividends, interest, and royalties, as well as normal AML/CFT verification of the remittance's source and purpose. Registered banks must ensure that every cross-border payment is consistent with the customer's declared business profile and sanctions screening, but they do not administer convertibility approvals. The OIO may, however, require screening approval before an offshore investor can complete a transaction involving sensitive New Zealand assets, and non-compliance with OIO conditions can trigger remediation or divestment orders; this is a substantive investment-admission rule, not an exchange-control limitation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no prescribed repatriation timeline for export receipts. Exporters and online sellers of maternity and infant care commodities (ISIC 4791) may legally retain foreign currency earnings in offshore multi-currency accounts, choose their own conversion timing, and hold USD, CNY, EUR, or AUD working capital outside New Zealand. Registered banks will nevertheless apply standard AML/CFT customer due diligence and may request supporting trade documentation—such as commercial invoices, shipment tracking references, customs declarations, and payment gateway settlement reports—for large or frequent foreign currency receipts, to satisfy the bank's transaction monitoring filters and sanctions screening. No Reserve Bank of New Zealand notification, conversion certificate, or repatriation undertaking is required; any 'export surrender' language appearing in financing agreements is a private contractual covenant designed for bank cash-flow control, not a statutory mandate.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), established under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 (RBNZ Act), serving as the central bank, monetary policy authority, prudential regulator of registered banks, and the anti-money laundering supervisor of banks and non-bank deposit takers under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act). Since the abolition of the Exchange Control Act 1964 in 1984 and New Zealand's acceptance of IMF Article VIII obligations, the Reserve Bank maintains no exchange control approval regime for current or capital account transactions; the foreign exchange market is fully liberalised, and the NZD floats freely. The Overseas Investment Act 2005, administered by the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) within Toitū Te Whenua Land Information New Zealand, is the sole material cross-border capital filtering mechanism for foreign investments involving sensitive land, significant business assets, or fishing quota, but it is not an FX control statute.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. New Zealand does not maintain a bespoke electronic money institution (EMI) licensing category analogous to the EU; non-bank payment and money-transfer providers must register on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must become an AML/CFT reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009. Offshore digital EMIs offering multi-currency wallets or virtual IBAN-like accounts are therefore not passportable into New Zealand; they may serve the entity only by partnering with a New Zealand registered bank, a licensed non-bank deposit taker, or a locally registered money remitter acting as the settlement agent. Global gateways such as Stripe and PayPal operate in New Zealand through local partner-acquiring arrangements and remit merchant settlements in NZD into a New Zealand bank account, which remains the binding constraint for this International Company; a Cayman-style International Company without an NZ bank account will face significant friction in receiving Stripe/PayPal payouts. Legal constraints include the absence of a standalone EMI passport, the mandatory requirement of a locally incorporated reporting entity or appointed AML/CFT compliance officer, the practical insistence of NZ banks that settlement accounts be held by the legal entity with substantive CDD documentation, and sanctions/export-control screening on cross-border payment corridors.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant capital-injection routing is executed through a standard SWIFT MT103 wire from the offshore shareholder's bank to the New Zealand bank's nominated beneficiary account, using the domestic account format accepted by the NZ bank (typically the BN/Branch-Branch suffix account number, e.g., 01-XXXX-XXXXXXX-XX) together with the bank's BIC, the exact legal entity name of the beneficiary, and a remittance narrative in English stating 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION / EQUITY SUBSCRIPTION — CK_IC INTERNATIONAL COMPANY — [Share Certificate/Resolution Reference]'. New Zealand does not administer a statutory FX purpose-code system; however, the receiving bank's transaction-monitoring filters will expect the narrative to be economically consistent with the entity's registered business objective (online sales of maternity and infant care commodities). For transactions that may trigger Overseas Investment Act 2005 screening—for example, if the capital is to be deployed into sensitive land or significant business assets (with the indexed threshold typically in the NZ$100 million-plus range, subject to current gazetted amounts)—the investor must obtain and produce an OIO consent or exemption reference before settlement. The receiving bank will verify the remitter's identity and conduct source-of-funds due diligence, and may require the following documentary bundle: (1) certified copy of the board resolution authorising the capital contribution; (2) shareholders' register extract evidencing the allotment; (3) source-of-wealth statements for the subscribing shareholder; (4) OIO decision letter or a legal opinion confirming non-notifiable status; and (5) a signed funds-receipt declaration for the company's accounting records. Merchant settlement inflows from Stripe and PayPal are received into the NZ operational account via the gateways' local NZ settlement partner banks, in NZD, providing the natural liquidity pool for these outbound disbursements. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. New Zealand does not maintain a bespoke electronic money institution (EMI) licensing category analogous to the EU; non-bank payment and money-transfer providers must register on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must become an AML/CFT reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under New Zealand's transfer pricing rules (Subpart GC and Sections GC 1 to GC 16 of the Income Tax Act 2007, current as amended in 2024 following the OECD BEPS Actions 8-10), a tax return filing entity is required to prepare and retain transfer pricing documentation if the relevant cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZ$1,000,000 in aggregate for the income year for goods and services transactions, and NZ$1,000,000 for loans or other advances; however, where no documentation is prepared, IRD may impose a transfer pricing shortfall penalty under Section 141EE of the Tax Administration Act 1994, with a no-documentation penalty of up to NZ$100,000 per year, and a transfer pricing adjustment will be made; note also that New Zealand has adopted the OECD's Master File and Local File requirements for multinational groups with turnover above EUR 750 million (approximately NZ$1.3 billion), and the Country-by-Country Report threshold applies to groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million (approximately NZ$1.3 billion), with the IRD requiring the CbC report to be filed by the ultimate parent entity or a surrogate parent entity resident in New Zealand; the Cook Islands International Company itself is not subject to Cook Islands transfer pricing documentation legislation, but if it is a non-resident counterparty to New Zealand cross-border related-party transactions, the New Zealand entity or permanent establishment must document and benchmark the arm's length price, and for a Cook Islands IC that is nominally tax-exempt, IRD will look through to the economic substance and beneficial ownership of the profits, and apply the transfer pricing rules to any New Zealand-related party payment.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand applies a general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under Section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and the associated reconstruction/schoenherr provisions under Section GA 1/GB 1, under which any arrangement lacking a bona fide commercial purpose and having a tax avoidance purpose may be voided and reconstructed, especially where a Cook Islands International Company is inserted to divert taxable profits; the Cook Islands has enacted Economic Substance Regulations under the International Companies (Economic Substance) Act 2020, as amended, requiring International Companies and Limited Partnerships carrying on a 'relevant activity' (including distribution and service centre businesses) to demonstrate substantial economic presence in the Cook Islands in proportion to the relevant activity; the required substance indicators for a distribution and service centre business include: (i) physical presence in the Cook Islands such as an office or premises, (ii) at least one full-time employee or other personnel physically present in the Cook Islands who manages the relevant activity, (iii) a minimum of one board meeting held in the Cook Islands with the minutes recording the strategic decisions, and (iv) a level of operating expenditure in the Cook Islands proportionate to the level of the relevant activity; where a Cook Islands International Company fails the economic substance test, it is liable to an economic substance penalty under the Cook Islands Economic Substance Act of up to NZ$500,000 or a sum equal to the tax advantage, in addition to potential enforcement action by the Cook Islands Financial Intelligence Unit under the AML/CFT framework; New Zealand's tax authorities will also deny treaty benefits and apply the full New Zealand corporate tax rate of 28% to the income attributed to the entity if it is managed and controlled from New Zealand and lacks substance in the Cook Islands.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers are liable for civil penalty actions under Section 99 of the New Zealand AML/CFT Act for each failure to comply with an AML/CFT obligation, up to NZ$200,000; under the Cook Islands AML/CFT Act, directors can personally be fined for failing to report suspicious transactions where they have actual knowledge, and the Cook Islands Financial Intelligence Unit can apply to the High Court for a pecuniary penalty order; if the business is used to launder proceeds of tax evasion or defrauding the Crown, directors may be prosecuted under the Crimes Act 1961 for money laundering (imprisonment up to 7 years) and for participating in an organised criminal group (imprisonment up to 10 years).
Cross-Border E-Commerce Consumer and Data Protection Non-Compliance (Digital Red Flag)

Statutory sanction: IRD may register the offshore seller for New Zealand GST compulsorily and assess GST for the past seven years within the IRD's time bar, with late payment penalties, and use-of-money interest; a 20% shortfall penalty may apply for lack of reasonable care; regulated product safety breaches under the Fair Trading Act attract a fine of up to NZ$600,000 per offence for a company, and under the Consumer Guarantees Act, civil liability to refund, repair, or replace products and to pay consequential damages; a Privacy Act breach can result in proceedings before the Human Rights Review Tribunal with damages awarded up to NZ$350,000 and USTR/UCC compliance issues concerning cross-border data transfer; failure to appoint a New Zealand agent or representative under the relevant data/GST registration rules may expose directors to service of process and personal liability for GST debts in cases of deliberate avoidance.

Director personal liability: For GST, directors are not generally personally liable unless the company is in liquidation and has not paid GST, in which case the Commissioner may issue a director penalty notice under the Tax Administration Act 1994 or the Companies Act 1993, making directors personally liable for the unpaid GST amount; directors who knowingly make false GST returns face personal fines under Section 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994 of up to NZ$75,000; for Fair Trading Act offences, directors can be convicted as parties to the offence and fined up to NZ$200,000; for privacy breaches, the Privacy Commissioner may name directors in public reports but generally the company bears liability, although directors who are knowingly concerned in the breach can be personally liable under the civil penalty provisions; cross-border travel bans may be sought where directors accumulate large tax debts and are resident overseas but travel to New Zealand.
Transfer Pricing Compliance Failure (New Zealand-IRD Cross-Border Pricing)

Statutory sanction: IRD may apply a transfer pricing adjustment substituting arm's length consideration for the full period for which the assessment is open; a shortfall penalty of 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and up to 100% for an arrangement with a dominant purpose of tax avoidance, or 150% for evasion; a separate no-documentation penalty of NZ$100,000 per year may be imposed even if no tax shortfall results; the adjustment could result in additional New Zealand income tax of 28% and ancillary tax on non-filing; in egregious cases, a criminal tax evasion prosecution may be brought.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign or cause to be signed tax returns that contain false or misleading transfer pricing statements may be prosecuted for tax evasion under Section 143 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, with fines and imprisonment up to five years; they may be jointly and severally liable for the company's transfer pricing shortfall penalties if the company is unable to pay and the directors directed or sanctioned the pricing policies; in an egregious individual case, the Commissioner may apply orders against directors' assets, and directors may be subject to director-banning orders and arrest warrants for failing to respond to Commissioner's examination powers under Sections 17 and 17A of the Tax Administration Act (penalties for non-compliance of NZ$2,000 per question, and up to 10 years of imprisonment for knowingly providing false or misleading information under Section 143H for an officer of a body corporate).
