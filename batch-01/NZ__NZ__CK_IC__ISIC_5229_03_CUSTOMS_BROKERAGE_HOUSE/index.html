Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by the Companies Act 1993; the International Company may be validly incorporated and carry on business with a nominal share capital (e.g., 1,000 Ordinary Shares at NZD 0.01 each, totalling NZD 10.00). There is no mandatory escrow account, capital deposit, or capital verification requirement under New Zealand law. However, as a condition precedent to the customs broker licence, the New Zealand Customs Service will require the shareholder to procure a security bond or bank guarantee in the amount prescribed by the Chief Executive (commonly NZD 20,000 for a licensed customs brokerage house) pursuant to Part 7 of the Customs and Excise Act 2018; this instrument operates as the functional financial deposit for licensing purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a 'check-the-box' classification regime. Relevant elections and statutory mechanisms include: (i) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007 (limited to close companies with 5 or fewer counting owners who are individuals or trustees); (ii) Consolidated group election under subpart FM (80% common ownership) allowing a group of NZ-resident companies to file a single tax return; (iii) GST grouping election under section 55 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (iv) Hybrid and branch mismatch elections under subpart FH (BEPS measures) to align NZ tax treatment with hybrid transfer pricing outcomes; (v) Non-resident company election to compute and file NZ branch accounts and claim deductible expenditure against NZ-source income; and (vi) the conduit election under the NRWT rules to reduce non-resident withholding tax on outbound dividends derived from underlying foreign income. For a customs brokerage house, an election to zero-rate offshore transport-related services may be made under the GST Act if statutory documentation requirements are satisfied.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of GST applies to: (i) goods physically exported from New Zealand by the supplier within 28 days of the time of supply (GST Act 1985, s.11(1)(a)–11(1)(c)); (ii) services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed and where the services are supplied for the non-resident's business carried on outside New Zealand (s.11A); and (iii) goods entered into a bonded warehouse for export or transported offshore under customs bond. A licensed customs brokerage house may zero-rate its fees when the immediate recipient is a non-resident located outside New Zealand and the brokerage services directly relate to export logistics, customs clearance of offshore cargo, or documentation for goods to be exported from New Zealand. The zero-rating of services is subject to the 'directly in connection with land or goods in New Zealand' exception, so pure export facilitation services must be distinguished from services that benefit a person in New Zealand. There is no statutory 180-day foreign exchange repatriation deadline under New Zealand law; the 180-day parameter in this dossier is an internal compliance benchmark adopted by the brokerage model.

  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (ocean or air) evidencing physical export of the goods from New Zealand
  • Commercial invoice and packing list

No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping or continuous transaction control (CTC) mandate is imposed by Inland Revenue for GST. E-invoicing is voluntary in New Zealand and operates on the Peppol interoperability framework (managed by Inland Revenue) for B2B electronic invoice exchange. However, all customs declarations — import and export — must be lodged electronically through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window (TSW); customs brokers must use the Automated Broker Interface or direct-to-TSW integration and each export declaration receives a Unique Consignment Reference (UCR). Peppol e-invoicing does not replace the statutory GST return filing obligations under the GST Act.

Inland Revenue can redetermine GST liability and recover any incorrectly claimed zero-rating as GST debt (s.79, GST Act 1985), with use-of-money interest accruing from the original due date. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply: up to 20% for lack of reasonable care, up to 40% for gross carelessness, up to 75% for an abusive tax position, and up to 150% for evasion or fraudulent intent. Criminal prosecution may be brought under s.143A (imprisonment up to 5 years) and s.143B for aiding and abetting evasion. NZ Customs separately imposes penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 for false or misleading export declarations (up to NZD 20,000 per offence), forfeiture of goods, and suspension or revocation of a Customs Broker Licence under s.283 for professional misconduct. Inland Revenue and NZ Customs operate a Joint Border Management System (JBMS) and use risk-profiling algorithms to scrutinise zero-rated export claims, including cross-matching export declarations with foreign exchange receipts and GST returns.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a freely floating exchange-rate regime with full convertibility on both current and capital accounts. There is no central-bank approval for trade-related current payments, customs brokerage commissions, dividend remittances, interest payments, or equity injections. The only outward-investment screening relevant to a non-resident owner is under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 for sensitive land and strategic assets, which is not engaged by the ordinary operation of a licensed customs brokerage house. Profit and capital repatriation to the Cook Islands International Company parent is unrestricted by FX controls but remains subject to New Zealand non-resident withholding tax under the Income Tax Act 2007 (statutory default 30% for dividends and interest, reducible only where a recognised double-tax agreement supplies a lower rate; the New Zealand-Cook Islands relationship rests on administrative cooperation and information exchange, not a comprehensive dividend-rate treaty).

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio exists for export receipts or export-related service fees. The surrender-and-repatriation machinery of the former exchange-control regime was dismantled in 1984 and is not in force; a customs brokerage house in New Zealand may retain international service fees and duty-fund replenishments in any convertible currency, net them against overseas disbursements, or convert at market rates without Reserve Bank consent. The sole compliance overlay is transactional: reporting for balance-of-payments statistics by the settlement bank and originator/beneficiary identity verification of international wire transfers under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, with prescribed transaction-reporting obligations triggered for cash-equivalent transactions of NZD 10,000 or more.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), Wellington. Exchange-control powers originate from the Exchange Control Act 1953, which is currently dormant following the 1984 liberalisation of the New Zealand financial system; the Governor-General may re-activate such controls only by Order in Council on national-interest grounds. In ordinary commercial practice, the Reserve Bank performs no transaction-level FX approval function; its active role is confined to monetary policy, prudential regulation of registered banks under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 2013, balance-of-payments statistics, and supervision of real-time gross settlement through the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Low-Medium, with material legal constraints. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, and Ebury are usable only as supplementary payment-rail facilitators, not as settlement banks or custody platforms for a licensed customs brokerage. An EMI accepting deposits or executing payments in New Zealand must either hold a registered-bank licence under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 2013 or, if a non-bank deposit taker, a licence under the Non-bank Deposit Takers Act 2013; the fallback model is registration as a financial service provider on the FSP register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and compliance as a reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009, with settlement effected through a locally registered partner bank. Holding client duty deposits or brokerage fee flows in an unlicensed EMI would breach the customs broker's fiduciary agency duties to importers and would not satisfy the New Zealand Customs Service security requirements governing deferred duty arrangements.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Low-Medium, with material legal constraints. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, and Ebury are usable only as supplementary payment-rail facilitators, not as settlement banks or custody platforms for a licensed customs brokerage. An EMI accepting deposits or executing payments in New Zealand must either hold a registered-bank licence under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 2013 or, if a non-bank deposit taker, a licence under the Non-bank Deposit Takers Act 2013; the fallback model is registration as a financial service provider on the FSP register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and compliance as a reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009, with settlement effected through a locally registered partner bank. Holding client duty deposits or brokerage fee flows in an unlicensed EMI would breach the customs broker's fiduciary agency duties to importers and would not satisfy the New Zealand Customs Service security requirements governing deferred duty arrangements.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends paid by a New Zealand company to non-resident shareholders; no general exemption applies for an International Company. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Master File and Local File will be required when the New Zealand customs brokerage entity has aggregate related-party cross-border transactions exceeding NZD 10 million in an income year and the multinational group has consolidated turnover above NZD 40 million. Country-by-Country Reporting threshold: multinational group with consolidated group revenue of at least NZD 1.127 billion must file CbC reports with IRD.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply the Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) by name, but Inland Revenue applies a substance and control analysis: a non-resident-owned International Company purporting to be tax-exempt must demonstrate genuine economic presence in New Zealand, including real premises, operational employees, bank accounts, board functions, and the ability to make day-to-day customs brokerage decisions. Without such substance, IRD will treat the entity as having a permanent establishment in New Zealand, assess income at 28%, and may invoke anti-avoidance provisions to re-characterise the structure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Anti-Money Laundering / AML-CFT Failure

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties up to NZD 2 million per reporting entity and pecuniary penalties for officers; regulatory sanctions include cancellation or suspension of the AML/CFT registration, public censure, and criminal fines in aggravated cases. Customs brokerage falls within the reporting-entity net and failure to register is itself a criminal offence.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers are treated as officers and may be prosecuted personally for failing to ensure compliance; individuals face fines and imprisonment of up to two years, court-ordered disqualification, civil restitution, and travel bans where there is a material flight risk or risk of leaving New Zealand to avoid enforcement.
Customs Fraud and Duty Evasion

Statutory sanction: Customs and Excise Act offences: goods forfeiture; monetary penalties up to three times the duty evaded; criminal fines of up to NZD 1 million or five times the duty value, whichever is higher; imprisonment for a term up to five years; revocation of customs broker authorisations.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, participate in, or conceal false customs declarations are primary offenders rather than protected corporate agents; they face joint and several civil restitution for the duty shortfall, personal criminal conviction, imprisonment for up to five years, and New Zealand courts may issue travel bans or border alerts against departing principals.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: IRD will adjust the taxable income using the arm's-length principle, apply shortfall penalties of between 20% and 150% of the tax shortfall, charge UOMI interest, and may impose disclosure-related penalties for failure to produce contemporaneous documentation. Fraud factors increase penalties to the maximum level.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false English-language transfer pricing policies or issue invoices that divert profits from New Zealand PE are exposed to personal liability as knowing participants in a transaction structured to defeat tax; civil recovery may extend to personal bank accounts, and serious evasion gives rise to criminal penalties and director disqualification.
NRWT Withholding Evasion

Statutory sanction: The New Zealand company is personally liable for the un-withheld NRWT at the statutory rate (30% dividends, 15% interest/royalty); shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the unpaid tax can be imposed, plus UOMI interest; criminal prosecution is available where the avoidance was deliberate.

Director personal liability: Limited liability does not shield a director who knowingly causes the company to omit withholding; civil recovery can be enforced against a director as an aider and abettor of an arrangement whose dominant purpose is to defeat an NRWT obligation, with personal criminal liability resulting in imprisonment of up to five years and travel restrictions pending prosecution.
Shell Company and Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: IRD treats the entity as a resident company and PE, weighs all facts under the general anti-avoidance rule, re-characterises the Monday transactions, taxes the attributed profit at 28%, adds shortfall penalties of up to 150%, and may confiscate assets through the Proceeds of Crime Act for money laundering involved in the sham.

Director personal liability: Directors of both the New Zealand IC and the foreign principal can be held as parties to the avoidance scheme; the corporate veil can be pierced for sham arrangements, leaving personal assets open to civil claims; criminal conviction carries imprisonment for up to five years, director disqualification, and court-imposed travel bans.
