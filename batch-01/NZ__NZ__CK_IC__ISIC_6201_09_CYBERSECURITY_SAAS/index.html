Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand, with the secure online portal 'myIR' at myIR.ird.govt.nz (primary URL: www.ird.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

A foreign-incorporated entity such as the International Company must apply to the IRD for a non-resident IRD number (New Zealand tax file number) before engaging in any economic activity with New Zealand nexus. For non-resident companies, the standard pathway is submission of IR742 (Non-Resident Company Tax Registration Application) through myIR or postal lodgement, supported by certified constitutional documents, registered office address, particulars of directors/shareholders, and, if applicable, notice under section 42 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. If the entity will make or intend to make taxable supplies in New Zealand, it must simultaneously apply for GST registration using form IR356 or online via myIR (registration code 'GST'). The IRD issues the resident and GST numbers upon verification; the combined IRD 8/9-digit identification number serves as the legal taxpayer reference for income tax, GST, PAYE, FBT and other administered taxes. No minimum capital or physical presence is required for an International Company, but a registered New Zealand address for service or an IRD-accredited tax agent is recommended to maintain compliance.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a conventional 'check-the-box' classification regime analogous to US Form 8832; however, because the International Company falls under the statutory Tax_Exempt_Zone model, its offshore-derived income and foreign-source revenue are treated as exempt from New Zealand entity tax without requiring an annual ruling. Elections that remain available to the entity under the Income Tax Act 2007 are: (i) election for Look-Through Company (LTC) status under subpart HG for close-held qualifying companies (if ownership is within five natural persons, not applicable where the sole shareholder is a corporate/oceania trust); (ii) election for branch equivalent tax treatment of permanent establishment income under section 91AAB; and (iii) filing a hybrid mismatch election/notice under the BEPS anti-hybrid rules (sections FH 1 to FH 11) to neutralise double non-taxation outcomes. Because the entity is financially transparent in its offshore exempt zone, the use of hybrid instruments must be documented in the CFC/offshore disclosure annex to the annual income tax return.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

0.15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (compulsory registration threshold for annual taxable supplies in New Zealand; for non-resident suppliers of remote/digital services and low-value goods the compulsory threshold is likewise NZD 60,000 derived from New Zealand consumers, with no elective registration below this threshold except under the 'platform economy' grouping rules); the simplified 'offshore supplier registration' regime (GST Act 1985 ss. 51A-51D) applies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 s 11 and s 11A, a zero-rated (0% GST) export supply is available to: (1) goods actually exported by the supplier - the supplier must ensure the goods leave New Zealand within 28 days (unless movement is a continuous journey) and hold prescribed evidence of export including a customs export entry, bill of lading/airway bill, or certificate of posting; (2) supplies of services provided to a non-resident customer who is outside New Zealand when the services are performed, where the contractual arrangement concludes with a non-resident and the intellectual property or benefit of the service is not consumed in New Zealand (zero-rating applies to remote services such as cybersecurity vulnerability scanning, cloud penetration testing and compliance SaaS); (3) supplies of international transport (if relevant); and (4) supplies of goods and services purchased by a person for the purpose of creating, maintaining or extending a taxable activity where the supply is of a going concern. The supplier must retain a truthful statement from the non-resident customer confirming their overseas status (common in B2B services) and, for SaaS where the customer receives communications/access from an overseas IP address, a permanent record of delivery. If part of the supply is performed in New Zealand or benefits a person in New Zealand, the portion must be attributed to taxable (15%) supplies; time-of-use apportionment is generally permitted.

New Zealand does not impose mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls (CTC), or digital tax stamping for e-invoices. The national standard is the voluntary PEPPOL BIS Billing interoperability (Peppol network) aligned with the digital invoicing guidelines from GS1 NZ and the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE). A registered GST supplier must issue a proper 'tax invoice' within 28 days after each taxable supply providing: the words 'tax invoice', supplier name and IRD number, GST number, transaction date, description and quantity, consideration, and GST amount; and for buyer-created tax invoices under section 24(5). GST returns (IR372) are due one calendar month after the end of the GST period (generally two-monthly, but monthly if turnover exceeds NZD 24 million or for customs/import-export intensive entities); there is no API-required clearance in the IRD system for each invoice, but large taxpayers should maintain machine-readable records (XBRL/SAF-T) for compliance. Additionally, when exporting goods, customs clearance is performed through the New Zealand Customs Trade Single Window (TSW) before physical exports, and export entries are matched to the GST return to verify zero-rating.

If the IRD identifies insufficient export evidence or a false zero-rating claim, the claimed zero-rating is retrospectively clawed back by treating the supply as charged with GST at 15%, along with use-of-money interest from the filing date. The Tax Administration Act 1994 imposes civil penalties for 'not taking reasonable care' (20% of the shortfall), 'unacceptable tax position' (20%), or 'gross carelessness' (40%); cases of deliberate evasion attract penalties of 100% to 150% of the tax avoided. For fraudulent export zero-rating, criminal prosecution under section 143B (evasion) may result in imprisonment (up to 5 years) and a fine up to NZD 50,000; strict export documentation is required for each export declaration and GST refund; the Customs and Excise Act 2018 also imposes fines for false customs declarations and forges cross-agency audit with the IRD and NZ Customs. The standard reassessment period is 4 years from the end of the return period, but for fraudulent evasion or wilful misrepresentation, the Commissioner of Inland Revenue has no reassessment time-bar and may claw back tax indefinitely.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible under both current-account and capital-account principles. New Zealand maintains no exchange-control legislation, no central-bank permit regime for cross-border transfers, and no restriction on the repatriation of profit, dividends, interest, royalties, or loan principal. The only frictions are fiscal and informational: non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at 15% on NZ-source dividends, interest, and royalties (potentially reduced under an applicable double-tax agreement), the requirement to obtain an IRD number for account-opening and withholding purposes, and prescribed transaction reporting (PTR) for payments exceeding NZD 10,000 under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Because the entity is classified as a Tax_Exempt_Zone International Company (ELF_CUSTOM_CK_IC), non-NZ-source income is not subject to New Zealand income tax, and remittance of such earnings is administratively frictionless once the bank's client-taxonomy profile is established.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. New Zealand abolished its exchange-control regime in 1984, and there is no statutory timeline for the repatriation of export or services receipts, no mandatory conversion ratio into NZD, and no obligation to surrender foreign currency to the Reserve Bank. Exporters and SaaS service providers may retain receivables indefinitely in any currency. The only residual requirement is statistical and anti-money-laundering related: cross-border transactions exceeding NZD 10,000 are reported by the receiving bank to the Reserve Bank through the International Transactions Reporting System (ITRS) and to the DIA through prescribed transaction reports; no approval or permission is required.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand – Te Pūtea Matua (central bank and prudential supervisor); responsibility for foreign-exchange controls is not centralized, with residual foreign-investment screening administered by the Overseas Investment Office under Land Information New Zealand (LINZ) pursuant to the Overseas Investment Act 2005. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and the Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) jointly complete the regulatory perimeter for payment services and anti-money-laundering oversight.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Offshore digital electronic-money institutions and payment corridors (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Currencycloud, and Luxembourg/UK EMIs) are viable as secondary collection and disbursement rails for the cybersecurity SaaS treasury, rated Medium-to-High for payment-speed efficiency, but they are legally incapable of serving as the primary settlement bank in New Zealand. They are not registered banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989, cannot take deposits from the New Zealand public, and must settle through a New Zealand-licensed bank. Any provider rendering money-remittance or money-or-value-transfer services into or within New Zealand must comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 under DIA supervision and may require registration as a financial service provider under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008. For treasury risk, the blueprint mandates that all EMI balances remain below NZD 1,000,000 equivalent and are swept daily to a registered tier-1 bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity and shareholder-loan injections must be executed via SWIFT MT103 (single payment) or MT202 (bank-to-bank cover) to the International Company's tier-1 NZ operational account. The routing protocol is: (1) originating shareholder bank instructs the transfer using the NZ beneficiary bank's SWIFT BIC and the beneficiary account number (New Zealand uses BBAN format with no IBAN); (2) the payment reference must state 'Share Capital Contribution – Companies Act 1993' or 'Shareholder Loan Agreement dated [date]' to avoid misclassification; (3) the receiving bank performs beneficial-ownership verification against the 25% threshold and screens the remitter against UN/NZ sanctions lists; (4) any credit above NZD 10,000 triggers a prescribed transaction report and ITRS statistical capture; (5) the account-holding bank must hold on file the signed board resolution authorizing the capital injection, the updated share register or drawdown notice, and an executed loan agreement containing commercial terms. No Reserve Bank of New Zealand approval or exchange-control consent is required; however, if the non-resident investment would result in ownership of 'significant business assets' exceeding the statutory threshold under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 (currently NZD 369 million for most non-sensitive holdings) or any sensitive land or fishing quota, prior consent from the Overseas Investment Office is mandatory before settlement.For recurring SaaS vendor payments, subcontractor royalties, and infrastructure procurement, the clearing rails are: (a) SWIFT MT103 for individual cross-border payments, delivered through the bank's e-banking file-transfer channel with ISO 20022 pain.001 batch instructions for mass runs; (b) Commercial Letters of Credit (ICC UCP 600) issued via SWIFT MT700 series for capital hardware or infrastructure purchases, with settlement in USD, EUR, or NZD through the SWIFT network; (c) domestic NZD clearing via the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) operated by Payments NZ; (d) high-value same-day NZD settlement via the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) operated by the Reserve Bank; (e) USD clearing via CHIPS or Fedwire; (f) EUR clearing via SEPA/TARGET2; and (g) FX trade settlement for hedging instruments through CLS where both currencies are CLS-eligible. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% (Income Tax Act 2007, Schedule 1, Part A, standard corporate rate)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends: 30% statutory maximum rate; 0% for fully imputed dividends paid to New Zealand-resident shareholders; treaty relief reduces the rate for qualifying non-residents to 15% (or 5% where the beneficial owner holds 10% or more of the voting power under the New Zealand-Australia, New Zealand-Singapore and New Zealand-China double tax agreements).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZ$10 million aggregate cross-border related-party transactions in an income year trigger mandatory written transfer pricing documentation comprising the OECD-aligned Master File and Local File; Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) applies to multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue equal to or exceeding NZ$1.3 billion. Thin-capitalisation rules restrict related-party debt for non-resident controlled companies to a 60% debt-to-equity ratio (non-banking entities).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not replicate the Gulf Cooperation Council-style Economic Substance Regulations; however, Inland Revenue applies the general anti-avoidance rule (section BG 1 and GA 1, Income Tax Act 2007), the statutory residence tests (incorporation, head office, centre of management under section YC 2) and the economic substance analysis embedded in the permanent establishment article of each double tax agreement. A Tax-Exempt Zone International Company must demonstrate real commercial substance in New Zealand, including active board decisions, physical presence, employees and substantive contractual arrangements. Absent such substance, the structure may be disregarded, treaty benefits denied under the principal purpose test, and profits re-attributed to the true economic owner.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (section 143B, Tax Administration Act 1994)

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction: fine not exceeding NZ$50,000 for an individual or NZ$250,000 for a body corporate; imprisonment up to 5 years. Civil shortfall penalties up to 150% of the evaded tax under section 141D (egregious case), plus Use of Money Interest accruing on the underpayment, in addition to late filing and late payment penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers knowingly concerned in the evasion are personally liable for the company's unpaid tax under section 149A; criminal conviction carries imprisonment up to 5 years; the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 authorises travel bans, asset restraint and forfeiture orders against the proceeds of tax evasion.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may adjust taxable income to the arm's length result and deny interest deductions on excess debt (section FE 4, Income Tax Act 2007); shortfall penalty up to 40% where the tax shortfall is not substantial, or up to 100% where the shortfall is substantial or the taxpayer failed to exercise reasonable care (sections 141A-141B); Use of Money Interest applies from the original assessment date.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or file tax returns containing transfer pricing adjustments without reasonable care may be personally assessed for 50% of the unpaid tax penalty under section 149B(2)(b); the Companies Act 1993 permits director disqualification where insolvency results from excessive related-party debt or reckless trading.
Shell Company and Deficient Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The general anti-avoidance rule (section BG 1, Income Tax Act 2007) voids the tax advantage and permits reconstruction of the arrangement; Inland Revenue may assess the income in the hands of the true economic owner or attribute it to a New Zealand permanent establishment; treaty benefits are clawed back under the principal purpose test; civil shortfall penalties and UOMI apply to the reassessed amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be served with a directors' penalty notice for unpaid company tax under section 149A, restrained from leaving New Zealand under debt collection procedures, and disqualified for up to 5 years for insolvent trading or failing to maintain proper accounting records under the Companies Act 1993.
Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty under section 89 of the AML/CFT Act 2009 of up to NZ$2 million for a company and NZ$300,000 for an individual for each contravention; criminal penalties for wilful contravention include fines and imprisonment up to 2 years; mandatory suspension or termination of merchant banking and payment processing agreements with Stripe and New Zealand clearing banks.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers are personally liable for civil penalties where they were knowingly concerned in the contravention; convictions for money laundering trigger asset forfeiture, restriction orders and travel bans under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009; the Financial Markets Authority may prohibit individuals from managing regulated reporting entities.
False and Misleading Tax Declarations and NRWT Evasion

Statutory sanction: Strict liability offence under section 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994: NZ$10,000 penalty per false statement; evasion penalty under section 143B up to 150% of the unpaid NRWT; the unpaid NRWT remains a debt of the company as withholding agent; Inland Revenue may issue a charging order over the company's New Zealand assets and recover the tax personally from directors under section 149A.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal restitution orders, travel bans preventing departure from or re-entry into New Zealand, and imprisonment of up to 5 years for wilfully causing false declarations; under the Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters Act 1992, foreign prosecuting authorities may extradite directors for cross-border tax fraud and related money laundering offences.
