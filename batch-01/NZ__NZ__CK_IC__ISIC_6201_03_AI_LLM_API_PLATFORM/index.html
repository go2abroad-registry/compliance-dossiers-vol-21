Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Taake), Government of New Zealand; online compliance portal: myIR at https://www.ird.govt.nz; border customs validation performed by the New Zealand Customs Service at https://www.customs.govt.nz.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign-owned International Company (IC) seeking New Zealand tax identity: (1) Determine tax residency - an IC incorporated outside New Zealand is non-resident for income tax purposes unless it has a fixed establishment in New Zealand; an IC incorporated in New Zealand is resident unless the application of a double tax agreement tie-breaker or a finding that central management and control is exercised offshore establishes otherwise. (2) Apply to the Inland Revenue Department for an IRD number by submitting Form IR596 (Application for an IRD Number by a Non-Resident Company), together with the certified certificate of incorporation, constitution, register of directors and shareholders, and identity documentation for all directors and holders of 25 percent or more of the shares. (3) If the IC will make taxable supplies in New Zealand in excess of the NZD 60,000 registration threshold, or elects voluntary registration, register for GST by filing Form GST 101A (Non-Resident GST Registration) through myIR, designating a New Zealand agent for service of notices if required by Inland Revenue. (4) If the IC engages New Zealand-resident personnel, register as an employer and obtain an employer IRD number for PAYE purposes. (5) Finalize the registration by linking the entity to myIR, securing the IRD confirmation letter and GST registration certificate; IRD generally issues the IRD number within 10 to 15 working days and the GST certificate within 7 working days of a complete application.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

Available statutory elections and designations under New Zealand tax law: (1) Look-through company (LTC) election under Subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, rendering the IC fiscally transparent with income and losses flowing through to shareholders; (2) Consolidated group election under Subpart IC of the Income Tax Act 2007 for groups of companies with 66 percent or more common New Zealand resident ownership; (3) Exemption from income tax for a non-resident foreign IC that has no fixed establishment in New Zealand and derives no New Zealand-source income, consistently with the Tax_Exempt_Zone classification; (4) Hybrid mismatch elections and special reporting under Subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007, including the elective branch equivalent rule under section FH 7 and the optional tiering of hybrid instruments; (5) Dividend exemption elections under section CW 9 for fully-taxed or specifically-exempt non-resident dividends; (6) A binding tax position ruling under section 149A of the Tax Administration Act 1994 confirming the IC exempt non-resident status and GST registration position, being the principal elective mechanism to render the Tax_Exempt_Zone classification administratively conclusive in New Zealand.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies made in or imported into New Zealand in any 12-month period; the threshold applies equally to resident suppliers, non-resident suppliers of remote services, and operators of electronic marketplaces supplying digital services to New Zealand resident consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 is available to an IC supplying generative AI and LLM inference API services where: (a) the services are supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time of performance under section 11(2); (b) the services qualify as remote services and are not physically performed in New Zealand under sections 11A and 11B; (c) the supply does not relate to land, goods, or services physically enjoyed in New Zealand; and (d) contemporaneous commercial evidence, being executed contracts, invoices, offshore tax identification or KYC records, IP-address and server-access logs, usage metering reports, and foreign currency remittance records, demonstrates the offshore chain. Physical goods exported from New Zealand, such as edge-computing appliances, are zero-rated under section 11(1) upon exportation, supported by a Customs export declaration, bill of lading or airwaybill, and inward remittance evidence. A non-resident IC supplying exclusively to non-resident customers is outside the New Zealand GST system entirely and is not required to register; registration is triggered only if it supplies remote services to New Zealand resident consumers exceeding the NZD 60,000 threshold. A GST refund of input tax is available to a registered person on zero-rated or exported supplies; refunds to non-resident registered persons are paid to the designated New Zealand bank account established at registration.

  • Customs Export Declaration (electronic declaration via ExDOC/Tradepoint or approved New Zealand Customs Service export entry) for physical exports
  • Bill of Lading or Airwaybill evidencing physical departure of goods from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or the corresponding bank SWIFT MT103/MT950 credit advice evidencing receipt of export consideration from the offshore buyer
  • Commercial invoice and executed service agreement specifying the offshore recipient, the nature of the AI/LLM API services, and the zero-rated or out-of-scope GST treatment
  • Non-residence evidence of the recipient (foreign tax identification number, tax residence certificate, official company registry extract, or equivalent KYC documentation)
  • Technology delivery and utilization records (API access logs, server and IP-address jurisdiction records, usage metering and billing outputs) substantiating that the supply was performed and consumed outside New Zealand

New Zealand operates no mandatory electronic invoicing or real-time fiscal clearance regime. E-invoicing is voluntary under the international Peppol framework administered by the Peppol Authority New Zealand; there are no continuous transaction controls, no API-based fiscal stamping, and no requirement for digital clearance certificates. GST returns are self-assessed and filed through myIR on a one-month, two-month, or six-month basis, with GST due on the 28th day following each taxable period. The Inland Revenue Department and the New Zealand Customs Service share electronic trade data, and zero-rated or out-of-scope export transactions remain subject to document-based audit verification rather than pre-clearance approval.

Inland Revenue audits export zero-rating claims with concentrated scrutiny on the genuineness of offshore provision and on traceable foreign exchange repatriation. Where a zero-rating claim is reversed, the IC is assessed for the full 15 percent GST on the disputed supplies, plus use-of-money interest at the prescribed quarterly rate and shortfall penalties under Part 9 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, being 20 percent for an unacceptable tax position, 40 percent for gross carelessness, and 100 percent for fraudulent intent. Wrongly refunded or reclaimed GST is recoverable by the Commissioner under section 26 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, with assessments generally limited to four years under section 108 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 but unlimited in cases of fraud. Failure to repatriate export proceeds within the 180-day window, or reliance upon irreconcilable or missing customs documentation such as absent bills of lading, customs export declarations, or inward remittance certificates, automatically exposes input credits claimed on export-related expenditure to clawback, triggers penalty loading, and may constitute grounds for criminal prosecution carrying fines of up to NZD 50,000 and imprisonment under the Tax Administration Act 1994 and the Crimes Act 1961.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. No exchange-control approval is required for the conversion of NZD into foreign currency, the crediting of foreign currency export revenues, the repatriation of share capital, or the distribution of profits. Profit remittance is achieved without material impediment; the only residual friction is the operation of the non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) regime under the Income Tax Act 2007 and the requirement under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) that commercial banks verify the source and destination of outward payments. For a tax-exempt international company whose operations are limited to the cross-border supply of tokenised AI inference services, the convertibility profile is unrestricted, subject to standard AML/CFT documentary evidence. No capital gains tax is imposed on share dispositions by non-residents, and there is no branch remittance tax or dividend-control licence.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory obligation to repatriate, surrender, or convert export proceeds. Any legal or natural person may retain 100% of hard-currency receipts in domiciled foreign-currency accounts, and may selectively convert into NZD at commercial rates. There is no prescribed timeline within which outward or inward trade settlements must be cleared. The only mandatory reporting arises in respect of balance-of-payments statistics (via reserve bank data collection and aggregated banking statistics) and tax compliance; the AI API platform must file annual income tax returns and charge goods and services tax (GST) at 0% for exported digital services, and should maintain transfer-pricing documentation for related-party platform usage fees.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand – Te Pūtea Matua (RBNZ), acting jointly with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for derivative and financial-market licensing. New Zealand maintains no dedicated exchange-control administration; the openness of the New Zealand capital account is a statutory feature of the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the unilateral commitment under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement, which obliges New Zealand to refrain from imposing restrictions on current international payments.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Regulatory viability: Medium-to-High, but with structural limitations. New Zealand licensed money remitters and non-bank financial institutions (operating under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and regulated under the AML/CFT Act 2009) may provide multi-currency transaction accounts to companies, including foreign-owned tax-exempt international companies, yet they do not hold a banking licence and therefore cannot offer deposit-taking facilities, overdraft commitments, or direct membership of the RBNZ's Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS). Offshore electronic-money institutions domiciled in the EU or UK do not automatically passport into New Zealand; an NZ Financial Service Provider (FSP) registration, NZ-based AML/CFT compliance officer, and FSPR membership are mandatory prerequisites. For USDC-heavy treasury flows, an NZ-registered Virtual Asset Service Provider (VASP) conversion layer is legally mandatory; direct crypto-custodial banking at Tier 1 banks is unavailable in New Zealand, though the platform may convert USDC to NZD via regulated digital-asset exchanges such as Independent Reserve NZ Limited or Easy Crypto Limited, followed by normal SWIFT or local NZD settlement into the Tier 1 bank accounts.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound institutional share capital subscriptions will be routed as telegraphic transfers using SWIFT MT103 for customer-originated payments and MT202 for interbank reimbursement, with the messaging narrative populated using ISO 20022 purpose code 'CAPT' (Payment of Securities-Related Fees) or 'COSP' (Capital Payments such as contributions to corporate capital). The remittance field should cite 'Share subscription – capital injection under subscription agreement dated [DD Month YYYY] – Beneficiary Account [NZ bank account number]'. Value is settled in NZD through the RBNZ-operated ESAS real-time gross settlement system, with same-day availability for large-value payments. Non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on fully imputed dividends is 0% for residents of treaty jurisdictions (including Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, Singapore, and China) where an approved resident withholding tax (RWT) rate of 0% applies to New Zealand resident shareholders. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Inland Revenue's transfer pricing rules (Part 4A of the Income Tax Act 2007) require cross-border related-party transactions to be conducted at arm's length. There is no statutory monetary threshold for preparing Local/Master File documentation, but Inland Revenue's operational statement and administrative guidelines require contemporaneous documentation for transactions exceeding NZ$3 million in aggregate annual related-party dealings, or NZ$1 million for any single category of related-party transaction involving AI/ML services or intangible-backed payments. A multinational group with total NZ turnover exceeding a prescribed threshold (commonly justified by reference to the OECD Action 13 framework, as adopted in Inland Revenue guidance) must keep a Master File and a Local File available on request. For the AI/LLM API platform, the material intercompany flows include SaaS-like fees, API usage fees, GPU cloud service charges, and software royalty payments; these must be documented using a contemporaneous OECD-aligned transfer pricing study, including a functional analysis and economic benchmarking.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime in the manner of the UAE; however, New Zealand tax law and general anti-avoidance rules (Part 5A of the Tax Administration Act 1994 and the statutory General Anti-Avoidance Rule in section BG 1 and GB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007) require that an entity claiming New Zealand tax benefits have real economic substance. A non-resident company in the structure must demonstrate actual business purpose, substantive decision-making, staff, assets, and risks adequate to its functions. The use of a Cook Islands International Company (CK_IC) in this jurisdiction analysis implies a regional structure where the IC is tax resident in the Cook Islands under its own tax law, not in New Zealand; however, if central management and control is exercised from New Zealand, the IRD may assert New Zealand tax residence and apply the full corporate tax regime. Under the New Zealand general anti-avoidance rules, a series of transactions designed to shift profits out of New Zealand without changing the economic position of the group is void against the Commissioner, potentially triggering reconstructing adjustments and penalties up to 100% of the shortfall, plus interest.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Avoidance

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalties under section 141E of the Tax Administration Act 1994: 50% of the tax shortfall for abusive tax avoidance, increasing to 100% if the dominant purpose is tax evasion or fraud. In addition, late payment penalties, use-of-money interest from the original due date, and criminal prosecution under section 143 or 144 may result in fines up to NZ$150,000 (25 times the tax shortfall for evasion) and imprisonment terms up to 5 years in the case of wilful evasion or aiding and abetting tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors of a New Zealand-registered or New Zealand-managed IC may be personally liable in equity for any tax unpaid by the company if the company's business has been carried on recklessly or fraudulently (section 135 and 136 of the Companies Act 1993, and section 115 of the Tax Administration Act 1994). The Commissioner can issue a director penalty notice (section 166) requiring payment of unpaid PAYE, GST, RWT, NRWT or other withholding taxes. Directors may face an order for civil recovery, a travel ban under the High Court's inherent jurisdiction where assets are dissipated, and personal disqualification. Criminal prosecution may also be brought against 'persons concerned with the management' of the company, including a shadow director or offshore shareholder director who participates in the decision to defraud.
Artificial Shell Company and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The IRD may under the general anti-avoidance rule apply the reconstruction rules in section GA 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 to treat the arrangement as void against the Commissioner, with the tax consequences determined as if the avoidance scheme had not occurred. This may lead to a 50% abusive tax position shortfall penalty, a 100% evasion penalty if intentional, and the disallowance of all expenditure attributable to the sham. In addition, the '100% entity' is treated as New Zealand resident under the dual-residence tie-breaks in an applicable DTA; the company can be assessed on its worldwide income, and any intercompany royalties paid offshore are disallowed as a deduction if the underlying IP ownership does not reflect the economic substance.

Director personal liability: Directors of the shell company, including offshore nominee directors who act without questioning the commercial reality, may be found to have breached their fiduciary duties under the Companies Act 1993. They may be jointly and severally liable to pay the company's unpaid tax (section 115 TAA), be prohibited from managing a company for up to 5 years under section 382 of the Companies Act 1993, and be subject to asset freezing orders and travel bans while an investigation is pending.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalisation Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may make a transfer pricing adjustment under Part 4A of the Income Tax Act 2007 and section GC 12, recalculating the taxable income at arm's length. The resulting tax shortfall is subject to a 20% shortfall penalty for taking an unacceptable tax position, or 50% for an abusive tax position with dominant tax avoidance purpose. Late payment penalties and UOMI apply. The thin capitalisation rules (section FE 4) disallow interest deductions to the extent that the New Zealand group's related-party debt exceeds 60% of its net assets, and the financial arrangement rules may deny deductions on hybrid mismatches involving convertible notes or excessive non-resident related-party debt.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve a tax return with an incorrect transfer pricing position may be jointly and severally liable for the resulting shortfall to the extent they caused or permitted the misstatement. Under section 141EB TAA, a taxpayer is liable for a 100% penalty for suppressing a document, or for knowingly providing false information to the tax agent. Directors may also be charged with an offence under section 143A for evading the collection or payment of tax, carrying penalties up to NZ$150,000 for an individual and imprisonment for up to 5 years.
AML/CTF and Financial Crime Violation in Payment Rails

Statutory sanction: The Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 applies to financial institutions, including those dealing in virtual assets. A reporting entity that fails to conduct CDD, develop a compliance programme, or report suspicious transactions faces civil penalties of up to NZ$200,000 for each contravention and NZ$10 million for repeated serious contraventions. Individuals who knowingly launder the proceeds of criminal activity are liable under the Crimes Act 1961 (sections 243-245) to imprisonment up to 7 years. Failure to report a suspicious transaction attracts penalties; in the tax context, undeclared USDC-to-fiat conversions are taxable as income or capital gains depending on the holding, and omission may trigger the evasion penalties and criminal liability described above.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers may be prosecuted under the AML/CFT Act where the failure results from their personal conduct or neglect; penalties include imprisonment of up to 12 months for a knowingly false statement to the FIU, and unlimited compensation orders. Directors of an IC who personally move USDC into a New Zealand bank account, or who control the API payment wallet, may be personally liable for AML penalty fines if the business operates without an adequate compliance programme. Travel bans can be imposed under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, and directors' offshore assets may be restrained.
