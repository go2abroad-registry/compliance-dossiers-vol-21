Competent corporate registry

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (Public Act 1993 No 105), ss 12-14 (incorporation, certificate and effect), ss 19-24 (company name), ss 25-34 (constitution), ss 38-46 (share issues and no-par-value shares), ss 83-92 (share register and statutory records), ss 87-90 (directors' register), ss 128-151 (board of directors, duties, consents and residential requirements), s 189 (registered office); Companies Act 1993 Regulations 1994; New Zealand Business Number Act 2016; Tax Administration Act 1994; Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime. Available statutory elections: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under sections HA 4-HA 7 of the Income Tax Act 2007, available to close companies with five or fewer owners, allowing fiscal transparency for domestic tax purposes; (2) Active Income Exemption (AIE) election for controlled foreign company (CFC) active business income under sections DN 2 and EX 20B, exempting qualifying active income from attribution; (3) branch equivalent tax (BET) elections harmonising foreign branch tax treatment; (4) hybrid and branch mismatch elections under Part FM (inserted by the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018), permitting alignment of New Zealand treatment for hybrid financial instruments, hybrid payers, and reverse hybrids; (5) GST cash-basis election and monthly filing frequency elections for working-capital optimisation; and (6) consolidated grouping and loss offset elections (section IC 5) for wholly-owned corporate groups.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies (including remote services) in a rolling 12-month period, pursuant to section 51 and section 54(1A) of the GST Act 1985; voluntary registration permitted below this threshold; the NZD 60,000 threshold applies equally to non-resident suppliers of remote services to New Zealand consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Section 11(1) of the GST Act 1985 zero-rates services supplied to a non-resident recipient outside New Zealand at the time of supply, unless the services are supplied in connection with land or moveable goods situated in New Zealand or are supplied to a non-resident physically present in New Zealand. Section 11A additionally zero-rates 'remote services' supplied to non-resident businesses (B2B) irrespective of place of consumption. The offshore BPO call-centre service constitutes a zero-rated supply when delivered under a cross-border contract to an offshore principal; the supplier must evidence the non-resident identity of the counterparty, the offshore place of acceptance, and the absence of New Zealand nexus of the recipient.

  • Customs Export Declaration (via New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window; CUSDEC number)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (airfreight)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT MT103/MT202 evidencing FX repatriation of the export proceeds
  • Executed Service Agreement / Master Services Agreement and Client Purchase Order confirming the non-resident recipient and offshore place of supply
  • Zero-rated GST invoice and bank/Wise B2B payment ledger confirmation

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping exists in New Zealand. E-invoicing is voluntary, operating on the PEPPOL BIS 3.0 network through certified service providers (CSPs) and the NZBN; invoices are not pre-cleared by Inland Revenue. GST returns must be filed electronically via myIR or accredited software, and Inland Revenue operates a digital-first compliance model with payday PAYE reporting; no API clearance step is triggered at invoice issuance, but an auditable invoice trail must be preserved under section 24G of the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Inland Revenue may reassess a zero-rated supply within four years of the date of the GST assessment, extended to eight years for fraud or wilful misrepresentation (section 108, Tax Administration Act 1994). Where zero-rating is denied, the entire GST becomes payable, input credits are clawed back with use-of-money interest, and shortfall penalties apply under section 141: 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for an unacceptable tax position, 50% for an abusive tax position, and 100% (or 150% in deterrent cases) for evasion. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims also expose directors and the tax agent to criminal prosecution, fines up to NZD 50,000, and disqualification orders.

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised, free-floating exchange-rate regime with no exchange controls. The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is freely convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions, and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand imposes no restrictions on the purchase, sale, or holding of foreign currency. Profit remittance and capital repatriation by a non-resident-owned international company (IC) are unrestricted, subject to three practical hurdles: (i) the standard non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) of 30% on dividends, interest and royalties, reducible under an applicable double-tax agreement (commonly 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for substantial corporate shareholders); (ii) the bank's obligation to file International Transaction Reporting (ITR) with the Reserve Bank for reportable cross-border instructions, which is purely statistical and not a consent gate; and (iii) sufficient AML/CFT documentation of the beneficial owner and the source of funds at the receiving bank. The GST Act 1985 zero-rates exported services supplied to a non-resident recipient situated outside New Zealand, so an offshore BPO receiving fees from overseas clients will normally hold zero-rated GST status rather than being subject to domestic 15% GST output tax. There are no negative carry, exchange-holding, or approval requirements applicable to invoicing in USD, EUR, GBP, AUD, or NZD.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. New Zealand law and RBNZ prudential regulations impose no statutory export-proceeds surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation timeline for export receipts or service-export proceeds, including BPO call-center revenue. The IC may retain 100% of its foreign-currency earnings in any designated offshore account or in a foreign-currency account at a New Zealand registered bank without RBNZ approval. The only compliance duty is the Reporting Entity's automated International Transaction Reporting (ITR) to the Reserve Bank for individual transactions above NZD 50,000 and the IC's own income- and transfer-pricing disclosure under the Tax Administration Act 1994. A remote BPO that invoices clients abroad and pays costs, salaries, and fees from a foreign-currency operational account is outside any surrender regime.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), established under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and today governed principally by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021, which regulates all registered banks in New Zealand. For AML/CFT purposes, the Reserve Bank is the supervisor for registered banks, the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) supervises non-bank financial product providers and financial advisers, and the Department of Internal Affairs supervises money remitters and other non-bank financial institutions, all under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. The IC is also subject to the Overseas Investment Act 2015 only if it acquires sensitive land or significant business assets above the statutory threshold (NZD 100 million for most non-Government investors), which a remote BPO call-center vehicle does not trigger.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: HIGH for auxiliary settlement rails but MEDIUM-LOW as the sole or primary treasury account. Wise Business (Wise Payments NZ Limited, supervised by the Department of Internal Affairs as a money remitter under the AML/CFT Act 2009) and Airwallex (Airwallex (NZ) Limited, also DIA-registered; Airwallex Pty Ltd holds an Australian AFSL) can provide virtual multi-currency accounts with US ABA routing, UK sort code, EU IBAN, and local receiving numbers in 30+ corridors, enabling low-cost, interbank-rate FX and fast cross-border payments in the exact target rails (Wise_B2B and Wire_Transfer). Legal constraints: (i) an EMI is not a 'registered bank' under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021 and cannot take deposits from the public; all customer funds are pooled into a segregated trust/omnibus account held at a partner Tier-1 bank, creating a credit and operational risk on the EMI itself; (ii) an EMI cannot issue an MT760 standby letter of credit, performance bond, or bank guarantee, which may be required by enterprise BPO clients; (iii) counterparties and the IC's own auditors may require a regulated-bank SWIFT MT103 trail for capital injection, invoice settlement, and profit distribution, which an EMI virtual account cannot fully replace; (iv) the EMI's own KYC and sanctions screening remains independently triggered by NZ law but the EMI will generally require a pre-funded balance from a primary regulated bank. Recommended architecture: ANZ or BNZ as the bank of record for capital, escrow, treasury, and compliance; Wise Business and/or Airwallex as the secondary high-frequency disbursement rail for agent payroll and small-vendor payments.

Compliant protocol for the IC's inbound equity: (1) Open the primary operational account and a nominee share-capital ledger at ANZ, BNZ, or ASB. (2) The UBO or foreign parent initiates a SWIFT MT103 (or ISO 20022 pacs.008 where the local corridor supports it) to the IC's account with Field 70 'Remittance Information' clearly stating 'EQUITY INJECTION / SHARE SUBSCRIPTION / [IC NAME]'. (4) Verification bundle filed with the bank at or before arrival of funds: board resolution authorising the share issue; share-subscription agreement; updated share register evidencing allotment; CRS/FATCA self-certifications; and the source-of-funds affidavit. (5) The bank releases funds immediately once the payment descriptor matches; no RBNZ approval is required for amounts below the significant business assets consent threshold (NZD 100 million). To avoid EDD, the remitting account should be the UBO's personal or parent-company account at a top-100 global bank, never an unregulated money remitter or an opaque third party. (C) New Zealand domestic payments -- NZD payroll and local operating expenses clear via Payments NZ's Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) or the ISO 20022-based real-time rail; a zero-balancing account structure should be set up with the Tier-1 bank to sweep surplus to the primary treasury account. (D) Wise Business / Airwallex -- for high-frequency, low-value disbursements (offshore agent payroll in PHP, IDR, LKR, ZAR, or USD), the IC tops up the EMI balance via SWIFT from the primary bank, then initiates local ACH or Faster Payments; every beneficiary must be pre-screened through the EMI's sanctions engine. (E) Profit repatriation -- an MT103 referencing 'DIVIDEND DISTRIBUTION / [IC NAME]' from the NZ bank to the parent/UBO account abroad, with the bank applying NRWT at the applicable DTA rate after the IC supplies Form IR67 and the foreign tax-residency certificate. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: HIGH for auxiliary settlement rails but MEDIUM-LOW as the sole or primary treasury account. Wise Business (Wise Payments NZ Limited, supervised by the Department of Internal Affairs as a money remitter under the AML/CFT Act 2009) and Airwallex (Airwallex (NZ) Limited, also DIA-registered; Airwallex Pty Ltd holds an Australian AFSL) can provide virtual multi-currency accounts with US ABA routing, UK sort code, EU IBAN, and local receiving numbers in 30+ corridors, enabling low-cost, interbank-rate FX and fast cross-border payments in the exact target rails (Wise_B2B and Wire_Transfer). Legal constraints: (i) an EMI is not a 'registered bank' under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021 and cannot take deposits from the public; all customer funds are pooled into a segregated trust/omnibus account held at a partner Tier-1 bank, creating a credit and operational risk on the EMI itself; (ii) an EMI cannot issue an MT760 standby letter of credit, performance bond, or bank guarantee, which may be required by enterprise BPO clients; (iii) counterparties and the IC's own auditors may require a regulated-bank SWIFT MT103 trail for capital injection, invoice settlement, and profit distribution, which an EMI virtual account cannot fully replace; (iv) the EMI's own KYC and sanctions screening remains independently triggered by NZ law but the EMI will generally require a pre-funded balance from a primary regulated bank. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand's transfer pricing rules under Subpart GC and the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines apply to cross-border related-party transactions. The master file (MTF) and local file (LTF) documentation thresholds are NZ$10 million for the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions for the income year, excluding dividend payments. Where the threshold is exceeded, the company must prepare and maintain a master file and a local file in accordance with Inland Revenue's Transfer Pricing Guidelines, and submit a TP disclosure statement (IR1061) with the income tax return. Documentation must be contemporaneous and demonstrate arm's length pricing. For BPO/call-centre services, the analysis must benchmark the service fee against comparable uncontrolled service providers, considering functions, assets, and risks of the New Zealand entity versus the related offshore service provider.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has not enacted standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) equivalent to the UAE or Bermuda models. However, the economic substance doctrine is applied through several statutory channels: (1) the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) in section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, which voids tax avoidance arrangements lacking commercial substance; (2) New Zealand's controlled foreign company (CFC) rules and foreign investment fund (FIF) rules, which may attribute income from low-tax offshore entities; (3) the requirement under many DTAs that treaty benefits require the recipient to be the beneficial owner, subject to the Principle Purpose Test (PPT) under the OECD Multilateral Instrument (MLI); and (4) Inland Revenue's ability to treat an entity as having a permanent establishment in New Zealand if it has substantive New Zealand-based activities. A Cook Islands International Company or other offshore IC must therefore demonstrate genuine non-resident substance, including substantive decision-making outside New Zealand, non-resident directors, physical office presence outside New Zealand, and arm's-length payments, to avoid re-characterisation of its income as New Zealand-sourced or as a sham.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: A tax evasion conviction under the Tax Administration Act 1994 section 143B carries a maximum fine of NZ$50,000 for an individual, or NZ$250,000 for a company, for each offence. The court may also order payment of the tax shortfall plus penalties and interest. Shortfall penalties for gross carelessness or intentional disregard can range from 20% to 150% of the tax shortfall. For 2024-2025, the use-of-money interest rate on underpayments is 10.94% per annum.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the company's tax liabilities via the director tax liability provisions in the Tax Administration Act 1994 if tax is not paid within 10 working days of a demand and the company has no reasonable prospect of paying. The Commissioner may seek a court order for recovery against directors personally. Criminal liability may extend to directors and officers who aided, abetted, or were knowingly concerned in the evasion, exposing them to fines of up to NZ$50,000 and potential imprisonment for evasion offences. Under s 143B, a person who knowingly assists in evading tax is liable to imprisonment for a term not exceeding 5 years for tax evasion (punishable on conviction on indictment), along with fines. Additionally, a director may be personally liable for civil penalties under the Companies Act 1993 and be prohibited from managing companies if found to have acted fraudulently or recklessly.
Shell Company / Artificial Arrangement Lacking Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The arrangement may be voided under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s BG 1 and s GA 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007). Inland Revenue may reconstruct the arrangement, assess tax as if the arrangement had not been entered into, impose a shortfall penalty of up to 100% of the resulting tax shortfall for dominant purpose of tax avoidance, and charge interest from the date the original tax liability arose. The company may also be liable for a late payment penalty and receive a court order requiring dissolution or deregistration. If the shell is used to evade tax, criminal fines up to NZ$250,000 apply.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company who caused the company to enter into an arrangement for a dominant tax avoidance purpose may be personally liable for the company's tax shortfall under the director penalty notice regime. Where the arrangement is a sham at common law, the corporate veil will be pierced and directors and controlling shareholders will be personally liable for the tax owed plus penalties. Under the Companies Act 1993, directors who act dishonestly in relation to the company may be ordered to pay compensation, and a court may disqualify them from being a director for up to 5 years or more for persistent contraventions.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Anti-Terrorism Financing (CTF) Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the AML/CTF Act 2009, an offence committed by a body corporate for most strict liability offences carries a maximum fine of NZ$2 million per offence, and calculation errors of suspicious transaction reports can trigger separate penalties. For serious and knowing money laundering offences under the Crimes Act 1961 section 243, an individual is liable to imprisonment for a term not exceeding 7 years, and a body corporate is liable to a fine not exceeding NZ$1 million. Failure to report suspicious transactions can also attract civil liability and compliance costs plus penalties under the Financial Markets Authority or Department of Internal Revenue enforcement actions.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers who knowingly authorised, permitted, or failed to take reasonable steps to prevent the company's AML/CTF breaches can be prosecuted individually. Under the AML/CTF Act, a director may be fined up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate offence if the offence is attributable to the director's wilful neglect. Money laundering convictions can result in imprisonment of the individual director for up to 7 years. A travel ban may be imposed by court order when a custodial sentence is anticipated for non-resident directors. Directors may also be subject to civil asset forfeiture under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may adjust the taxable income under the arm's length principle, applying the transfer pricing rules in Subpart GC and section GD 13. Transfer pricing shortfall penalties apply under section 141FB of the Tax Administration Act 1994. A 100% shortfall penalty applies where the taxpayer's gross transfer pricing adjustment exceeds NZ$2.5 million and the taxpayer had both a 'dominant purpose' of avoiding tax and failed to retain adequate documentation. Lower penalties of 20% or 40% may apply for not meeting the 'acceptable level of care' or 'unacceptable tax position' thresholds. Interest applies to the resulting tax shortfall from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Where the transfer pricing adjustment causes tax shortfall that remains unpaid, the director penalty notice provisions in Part 11 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 allow the Commissioner to hold directors personally liable for the unpaid tax. Directors who knowingly signed a false or misleading income tax return containing TP adjustments may face evasion penalties and personal fines up to NZ$50,000, and imprisonment up to 5 years for evasion. Directors cannot rely on the in-house CFO's or external accountant's negligence where they failed to exercise reasonable diligence in overseeing the tax compliance function.
Misclassification of New Zealand Permanent Establishment

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue will assess the company as having a New Zealand permanent establishment, attributing profits based on the OECD authorised OECD approach. The company is liable to pay income tax at 28% on the attributable New Zealand-source profits, plus use-of-money interest from the original due date and shortfall penalties for an unacceptable tax position (20%) or gross carelessness (40%) if the PE was knowingly concealed. Late filing penalties also apply for the unfiled IR4.

Director personal liability: Directors who make or assist in making a false declaration that no PE exists when they know or ought to know of the New Zealand substantive activity can be personally fined under tax evasion provisions and be liable for the company's unpaid tax through director penalty notices. Overseas directors may receive travel bans preventing them from leaving New Zealand if criminal proceedings are commenced, and may be subject to surrender of passports under the Bail Act 2000 in cases involving tax evasion charges.
Failure to Collect and Remit Non-Resident Withholding Tax on Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The company is liable as the agent of the non-resident for the withholding tax and must pay the unpaid NRWT plus penalties. The late payment penalty applies immediately to the unpaid NRWT amount, plus use-of-money interest. Shortfall penalties for failing to withhold where required are 20% of the tax shortfall if the position was not 'acceptable', and up to 150% for evasion. If the company fails to remit the withheld tax, the Commissioner may recover the amount as a debt due from the company; directors can be made personally liable.

Director personal liability: Directors who fail to ensure NRWT is withheld and remitted within the statutory time frames are exposed to the director penalty regime in section 56 and Part 11 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, under which personal liability is triggered if the NRWT is unpaid for more than 10 working days after the Commissioner's demand. Directors who knowingly enter into a dividend-stripping arrangement to avoid NRWT may be charged with tax evasion, leading to fines and imprisonment of up to 5 years.
Reportable Tax Arrangement and Disclosure Failure

Statutory sanction: The promoter and participants of a reportable tax arrangement that is later voided by the Commissioner face shortfall penalties of up to 100% of the tax shortfall, plus possible civil penalties of up to NZ$200,000 for promoters under section 141ED. The arrangement may be cancelled and reconstructed. Inland Revenue may also apply the specific penalties regime under s 141EA for failure to disclose, with fines of up to NZ$2,000 plus 100% of any tax shortfall.

Director personal liability: Directors of the IC who promoted, designed, or implemented the arrangement may be treated as 'promoters' and personally liable for civil penalties of up to NZ$200,000. Where the arrangement is an artificial loss scheme, the promoter may also be barred from managing a company and personal criminal liability may arise under the Crimes Act 1961 for false accounting or fraud, carrying up to 7 years imprisonment.
