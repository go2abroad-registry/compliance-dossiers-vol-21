Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department of New Zealand (IRD) — online portal: Inland Revenue 'myIR' (https://www.ird.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity without a New Zealand fixed establishment that derives New Zealand-source income or is required to register for New Zealand GST must apply to the Inland Revenue for an IRD number using the prescribed application forms (e.g., IR742 for non-resident individuals/companies, or the applicable entity registration form). The application must be lodged with certified constitutional documents, proof of foreign registration, director/beneficial owner identity documentation, and a New Zealand contact address or agent appointment. Upon approval, the IRD issues an 8- or 9-digit IRD number. For GST registration, use the myIR portal or Form IR356; a non-resident may register for GST if it carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand or if it makes remote services supplies to New Zealand consumers unless a reverse-charge or registration-threshold exception applies.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

No general check-the-box election exists under New Zealand income tax law. However, an eligible company may elect into the Look-Through Company (LTC) regime (IR61 form and shareholder consents) so that income and losses are treated as transparent for New Zealand tax purposes, subject to the LTC eligibility rules and the requirement that New Zealand-resident shareholders collectively own at least 20% (or, if certain cross-border exceptions apply, the Commissioner may accept the election with appropriate conditions). Additionally, hybrid mismatch rules under Subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007 may require a person to make an election to align tax treatment of a foreign hybrid entity or to disapply a mismatch outcome; a New Zealand branch of a foreign company may also elect to use the branch equivalent tax accounts method. A fully foreign-owned International Company with no New Zealand substantive presence will generally be treated as non-resident and not subject to New Zealand income tax on non-New-Zealand-source income; the Tax-Exempt Zone classification reflects the absence of a New Zealand permanent establishment and the statutory exemption of certain offshore income.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period (or a reasonable expectation of exceeding NZD 60,000 in the next 12 months); non-resident suppliers of remote services to New Zealand consumers have a nexus based on the value of supplies to New Zealand consumers and may be required to register under the GST (Remote Services) rules regardless of the NZD 60,000 threshold unless the supplier is already registered or uses the reverse-charge regime under the MOSS-like simplified registration, subject to the specific rules in section 54C and schedule 3B of the GST Act.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under the GST Act 1985 to exports of goods supplied to a recipient outside New Zealand if the goods are exported from New Zealand within 28 days of the supply, to a supply of goods that is a final export, to certain international transportation and freight services, and to services supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, provided the services are not supplied to a New Zealand resident and are not supplied in connection with land in New Zealand. The 'zero-rating of services' also extends to remote services supplied to non-resident recipients if the services are not performed in New Zealand or the recipient is not in New Zealand at the time of supply. A person must hold sufficient written evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient and the export occurrence; the zero-rating does not apply to services that are physically performed in New Zealand for a non-resident's private use or enjoyment in a manner that gives the non-resident a direct benefit in New Zealand.

  • New Zealand Customs Export Declaration (Electronic Customs Export Declaration via the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window)
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Evidence of shipment and export within the statutory 28-day window (e.g., cargo movement records, port/terminal receipts)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank evidence of foreign currency payment for the exported goods
  • Supplier's GST tax invoice showing the zero-rating basis
  • A copy of the recipient's non-resident identity evidence (e.g., foreign VAT registration certificate, certificate of incorporation, or passport/address evidence)

New Zealand does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory e-invoicing clearance system. Inland Revenue accepts electronic invoices, but there is no API-based stamping or digital fiscal seal obligation for GST. Businesses meeting the turnover threshold must issue tax invoices showing the GST number, supplier and recipient details, description, date, and GST-inclusive/exclusive amounts; buyers may use the buyer-created tax invoice process where permitted. A voluntary electronic invoicing framework based on the Peppol standard exists for public sector procurement, but it is not a statutory GST clearance mandate.

The Commissioner of Inland Revenue may audit export zero-rating claims and require production of all export documentation and remittance evidence. If a taxpayer incorrectly zero-rates a supply or does not retain the required evidence, the supply may be recharacterized as subject to GST at 15%, resulting in a retrospective output tax adjustment, use-of-money interest, and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994. Shortfall penalties can range from 20% for lack of reasonable care to 150% for evasion or similar acts, plus a criminal penalty of up to NZD 50,000 for a company (and potentially higher fines for individuals) for knowingly providing false or misleading information. Goods exported but not remitted/repatriated within the contractual terms without valid commercial reasons may also signal an artificial arrangement and trigger anti-avoidance provisions, including the general anti-avoidance rule in section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and the associated reconstruction powers of the Commissioner.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand is an IMF Article VIII economy; the NZD is fully convertible in both current-account and capital-account transactions, and there are no exchange controls, no approval requirements, and no quantitative limits on inbound or outbound movements of funds. Capital contributions, shareholder loans, dividends, royalties, and service fees may be remitted freely in any convertible currency, subject only to standard banking AML/CFT due diligence and, where applicable, New Zealand withholding taxes on NZ-source income (notably 15% non-resident withholding tax on dividends and interest absent a treaty or exemption, with no NZ-Cook Islands double tax agreement currently in force). For the no-code website builder operating through Stripe Billing and Paddle, settlement proceeds in USD, EUR, or NZD may be swept into the IC's bank account without prior reserve bank consent; conversion to NZD is available at the interbank market rate less the bank's standard FX spread.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender, conversion, or repatriation timeline for export receipts, SaaS subscription proceeds, or any other form of foreign currency income. Exporters and digital service providers may retain foreign currency balances indefinitely, sell them at prevailing market exchange rates at any time, or denominate invoices in any freely convertible currency. Stripe and Paddle settlement statements should be reconciled monthly to the operating bank account, and any open foreign currency positions should be managed under the entity's documented treasury policy to avoid unintended FX exposure.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) as the apex monetary authority and prudential supervisor in New Zealand; for the associated Cook Islands International Company (CK_IC), the Cook Islands Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) administers licensing, AML/CFT supervision, and financial institution regulation within the Cook Islands, while the Reserve Bank of New Zealand acts as the effective central bank for the Cook Islands because the Cook Islands uses the New Zealand dollar (NZD) and maintains no independent central bank.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, and Currencycloud are viable as complementary cross-border payment and disbursement rails, but they cannot replace a Tier-1 New Zealand bank account because Stripe Billing and Paddle settle to a licensed bank account held by the payment aggregator, ultimately remitting to a board-designated operating account rather than to an unlicensed EMI wallet. Legal constraints: any EMI providing money or financial services in New Zealand must be registered on the Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008 and must implement an AML/CFT programme under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009; offshore EMIs without an NZ FSP registration or without a licensed partner bank may not lawfully solicit or maintain NZ payment services. Recommended treasury design: maintain Wise or Airwallex as automated sweep accounts for recurring supplier payments below NZD 10,000 and for same-day cross-border reconciliation, while the primary liquidity spine remains a Tier-1 NZ bank account; all EMI balances should be capped at NZD 50,000 and drained daily to minimize unsecured counterparty risk.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire protocol: remit via SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for high-value transfers) from the overseas beneficial owner's or shareholder's bank to the NZ Tier-1 operating account, using the harmonised payment purpose code for equity contribution or capital injection (e.g., the bank-specific New Zealand code for capital account transfers, or narrative text 'Share capital contribution per board resolution dated [date]'). The board resolution, Cook Islands corporate extract, and beneficial ownership declaration must be uploaded to the bank's compliance portal before or immediately after the transfer. No Reserve Bank of New Zealand approval is required; funds are typically credited on the same or next business day after automatic AML screening and destination-country validation. Inbound injections above NZD 100,000 may trigger a source-of-funds questionnaire; third-party remitters are not permitted unless supported by a written loan or assignment agreement, and structured or cash-based transfers must be avoided entirely.Clearing rails, in priority order: (1) SWIFT MT103 direct transfers for supplier invoices, referencing the commercial invoice number and the applicable purpose code, with FX rate lock executed through the bank's online treasury platform; (2) bank-issued documentary commercial letters of credit (sight and usance) under UCP 600 for procurement of agriculture/food commodities from APAC counterparties, supported by invoice, bill of lading, packing list, and inspection certificate; (3) New Zealand domestic clearings via the RBNZ Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) and Payments NZ direct credit facilities for salaries, GST payments, and domestic invoices; (4) licensed fintech EMI sweeps via Wise or Airwallex for recurring cross-border disbursements below NZD 10,000, with fallback to SWIFT for any rejected batch; (5) multi-currency netting through the bank's global treasury platform to minimise conversion costs. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: MEDIUM. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, Payoneer, and Currencycloud are viable as complementary cross-border payment and disbursement rails, but they cannot replace a Tier-1 New Zealand bank account because Stripe Billing and Paddle settle to a licensed bank account held by the payment aggregator, ultimately remitting to a board-designated operating account rather than to an unlicensed EMI wallet. Legal constraints: any EMI providing money or financial services in New Zealand must be registered on the Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008 and must implement an AML/CFT programme under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009; offshore EMIs without an NZ FSP registration or without a licensed partner bank may not lawfully solicit or maintain NZ payment services. Recommended treasury design: maintain Wise or Airwallex as automated sweep accounts for recurring supplier payments below NZD 10,000 and for same-day cross-border reconciliation, while the primary liquidity spine remains a Tier-1 NZ bank account; all EMI balances should be capped at NZD 50,000 and drained daily to minimize unsecured counterparty risk.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) rate on dividends paid by a New Zealand company to a non-resident shareholder is 15% (Income Tax Act 2007, section 891A). Where a resident company attaches imputation credits, the total tax on a fully imputed dividend is 28%, but the non-resident is still subject to 15% NRWT on the gross dividend; the imputation credits do not reduce the NRWT exposure, but a supplementary dividend regime can fund additional taxation for certain treaty residents. There is no separate dividend distribution tax beyond these rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand's transfer pricing rules under Subpart GC of the Income Tax Act 2007 incorporate the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines. There is no statutory fixed threshold requiring a formal Local File/Master File. Instead, taxpayers must prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation where the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions exceeds NZ$1 million for transactions involving goods or services, or NZ$500,000 for financial transactions. Inland Revenue's investigations practice expects a robust TP policy where the volume of related-party dealings is material, and the documentation should include a functional analysis, benchmarking search, and arm's length pricing model. For a low-code SaaS platform generating recurring subscription revenue, transactions with offshore affiliates (e.g., software development, marketing, IP royalty fees) must be benchmarked using accepted methods.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a separate 'Economic Substance Regulations' statute comparable to UAE/Cayman ESR. However, the general anti-avoidance rule in section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and the 'business continuity' and 'source/ordinary income' doctrines require economic substance. For a NZ International Company owned by non-residents, the tax residence test applies the 'centre of management and control' test: if board decisions and day-to-day executive control are exercised in New Zealand, the company is tax-resident and liable at 28% on worldwide income. Thin capitalisation rules under Part FE apply to non-resident-owned companies with New Zealand assets, limiting debt deductions. Inland Revenue can also apply the permanent establishment (PE) concept to attribute New Zealand-sourced business profits, especially where the no-code website builder sells to NZ customers via a local agent or server infrastructure. Substance documentation (physical office, local employees, board meetings) is critical for non-resident ICs to avoid being re-characterised as a NZ-resident company.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Returns

Statutory sanction: Under sections 143A and 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, tax evasion is a criminal offence: imprisonment for up to five years and a fine of up to NZ$50,000 (first conviction) or NZ$100,000 (subsequent conviction). In addition, shortfall penalties of 20%–150% of the tax shortfall may be imposed (sections 141A–141I TAA). Civil pecuniary penalties of up to NZ$10,000 may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted for aiding and abetting tax evasion. Where the company fails to deduct or remit NRWT or PAYE/income tax, directors can become personally liable under section 31B TAA for unpaid tax and penalties. Courts may impose travel bans, asset restraint orders, and disqualification orders under the Companies Act 1993.
Shell Company and Incorrect Tax Residence

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may treat the company as a New Zealand-resident company, assess 28% corporate tax on worldwide income, and apply shortfall penalties for lack of reasonable care (up to 40%) or an abusive tax position (up to 150%). The anti-avoidance rule in s BG 1 can void the arrangement. Failure to maintain a local agent or registered office may lead to deregistration under the Companies Act.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly make false residence declarations or breach the Companies Act face personal liability for unpaid taxes. Directors who fail to exercise proper control can be sued for reckless trading and be personally liable for the company's debts. Travel bans and disqualification orders are available in civil penalty proceedings.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Under Subpart GC and OECD TP Guidelines, Inland Revenue may re-assess taxable income at an arm's length profit and apply shortfall penalties of 20%–150% depending on culpability. For failing to keep and maintain sufficient documentation, a specific penalty may be imposed under section 141D TAA (penalty equal to 50% of the resulting tax shortfall if the taxpayer took an unacceptable tax position). Thin capitalisation adjustment causes disallowance of excessive interest deductions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false TP declarations or fail to ensure the company meets its documentation obligations may be personally liable for shortfall penalties if they are found to have participated in an arrangement with dominant purpose of tax avoidance. Director civil liability applies under company law for insolvent trading.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax Evasion on Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The paying company is liable to NRWT at the statutory rate (15% dividends/royalties/interest generally). Late payment penalties (1%+4% initial, plus up to 10% monthly), UOMI, and shortfall penalties apply. Inland Revenue may recover any unreported amount from the directors. Breach of the PAYE/NRWT remittance rules is also a criminal offence under s 144 TAA punishable by imprisonment up to 6 months.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for unremitted NRWT under section 31B TAA when the company has an outstanding deduction obligation and the director fails to take reasonable steps to ensure compliance. Director disqualification and travel bans may be imposed.
AML/CFT Compliance Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, civil penalties for non-compliance range up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate and NZ$200,000 for an individual director/officer. Criminal pecuniary penalties for wilful contravention may reach NZ$5 million for a body corporate and NZ$300,000 for an individual, plus imprisonment up to 2 years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may be personally liable for civil penalties and criminal prosecution if they knowingly allow AML/CFT failures. Personal asset forfeiture or travel bans may be ordered by the High Court. A director can be disqualified under the Companies Act if involved in serious AML/CFT misconduct.
False Statutory Filings to Companies Office

Statutory sanction: Under the Companies Act 1993, knowingly filing false information is an offence punishable by a fine up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment for up to five years (s 377). Failure to maintain a registered office or agent may cause the Registrar to initiate deregistration, resulting in a dissolved company without the ability to sue or own assets. A false declaration to an AML/CFT regulator also triggers penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false statutory declarations are personally liable for fines and imprisonment. If the company trades while insolvent and the directors are engaging in fraudulent conduct, personal liability up to the full amount of debts can arise under sections 135, 136 and 588G Companies Act analogues (s 135 reckless trading, s 136 incurring obligations without reasonable belief of solvency).
