Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office — a business unit of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE), operating the New Zealand Companies Register and the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) portal at https://www.companiesoffice.govt.nz.

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (Public Act 1993 No 105), as amended by the Companies Amendment Act 2020–2023; applicable provisions include ss 9–14 (constitution), s 20 (name requirements), ss 38–40 (registration of companies), s 39 (application for registration), s 40 (registration and certificate of incorporation), ss 42–47 (allotment and issue of shares), s 60 (registered office), ss 83–84 (register of shares), s 91 (board resolutions in lieu of meeting), ss 128–130 (appointment of directors), and ss 189–199 (directors' duties). Subsidiary instruments: Companies (Fees) Regulations 2016, Companies (Directors and Registered Offices) Regulations 1993, and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 for registered agent and secretarial services.

Formation / constitutional filings

Form NZCN1 — 'Application for Registration of a Company Limited by Shares' submitted electronically under s 39 of the Companies Act 1993, together with the company constitution, subscriber consents, director consents, registered office details, and the prescribed filing fee on the Companies Office Online portal.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed for an International Company (IC) incorporated as a company limited by shares under the Companies Act 1993. Each subscriber must take at least one share (s 39(2)(a)); shares may be issued partly or fully paid (ss 42–45). No escrow account, capital deposit, or bank verification is required at incorporation. Shares may be allotted for cash or non-cash consideration (s 47), including property and goodwill, at fair value as approved by board resolution. Where the company holds client monies for secretarial and registered-agent services, such client funds must be maintained in separately designated trust or client accounts in accordance with the AML/CFT Act 2009 and the Lawyers and Conveyancers Act 2006 (if applicable), but this does not constitute a paid-up capital escrow requirement.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification: annual Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) under the Building Act 2004, including means of escape, smoke detection, fire extinguisher inspection by a chartered fire safety engineer, and emergency lighting verification by Fire and Emergency New Zealand (FENZ). Structural load clearance: a building consent memorandum from the territorial authority confirming the floor load-bearing capacity (minimum 3.5 kPa uniformly distributed for office records and archive vaults). Workplace clearance: WorkSafe New Zealand health-and-safety induction, first-aid registers, safe work method statements for manual handling, and a civil-defence emergency plan (earthquake-prone building status, safe-location signage, and evacuation assembly point) — all required before personnel are physically present for client services.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed for an International Company (IC) incorporated as a company limited by shares under the Companies Act 1993. Each subscriber must take at least one share (s 39(2)(a)); shares may be issued partly or fully paid (ss 42–45). No escrow account, capital deposit, or bank verification is required at incorporation. Shares may be allotted for cash or non-cash consideration (s 47), including property and goodwill, at fair value as approved by board resolution. Where the company holds client monies for secretarial and registered-agent services, such client funds must be maintained in separately designated trust or client accounts in accordance with the AML/CFT Act 2009 and the Lawyers and Conveyancers Act 2006 (if applicable), but this does not constitute a paid-up capital escrow requirement.

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue (Te Tari Taake) — the Inland Revenue Department of New Zealand. Digital portal: myIR, accessible at https://www.ird.govt.nz (secure sign-in at https://myir.ird.govt.nz); business registry interface via the NZBN (New Zealand Business Number) Register at https://www.nzbn.govt.nz.

E-government filing portal

A foreign company, including a Cook Islands International Company (CK_IC) operating a registered agent and offshore secretarial office with a New Zealand presence, must first obtain an NZBN (optional for non-resident companies) and then apply to Inland Revenue for a single IRD number which functions as the company tax identification number for income tax, GST, and PAYE. Application is made by filing form IR742 (Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application) — or, where the company is registered on the New Zealand Companies Register, the IRD number is issued automatically through the NZBN registration event — accompanied by (i) a certified copy of the certificate of incorporation issued by the Registrar of International Companies in the Cook Islands (or equivalent foreign company registry), (ii) the constitutive documents (Articles/Charter and Memorandum of Association), (iii) a list of directors, shareholders and the registered office address, (iv) a completed IR596 Certificate of Identification or equivalent evidence for the beneficial owners, and (v) details of the New Zealand appointed agent/service address. Where the CK_IC activities create a taxable activity in New Zealand (e.g., fees earned for secretarial services physically performed in New Zealand), a separate GST registration must be completed online via myIR or on form IR356 (GST registration). The IRD number is issued within 8–12 working days for paper applications and is required before any New Zealand bank account, payroll, or Customs client code can be operationalized. If the CK_IC merely uses a licensed New Zealand registered agent as an outsourced service provider and itself has no New Zealand taxable activity, the CK_IC is not required to register for NZ tax; the New Zealand agent must separately register for GST and IRD number in its own name.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Territorial_Scope

Available election forms

New Zealand has no check-the-box election. A non-resident CK_IC is taxed solely on New Zealand-sourced income on a territorial basis (Income Tax Act 2007, Schedule 6 and s YD 4), with no tax on foreign offshore secretarial revenue unless central management and control is exercised in New Zealand. The Cook Islands International Companies Act tax exemption (0% on offshore income) does not extend to New Zealand-sourced income. Relevant elections and regimes include: (i) GST group registration (s 55, GST Act 1985) permitting consolidation of resident group companies; (ii) GST cash-basis or payment-basis accounting elections (ss 19C and 19D); (iii) hybrid and branch mismatch rules in Part FH, requiring a New Zealand deduction-taker to eliminate double non-taxation where the CK_IC payer is tax-exempt (an electable adjusting amount methodology applies under the Financial Arrangements Rules in s FH 6-FH 8); (iv) foreign currency elections for financial arrangements under s EW 14; and (v) the limited look-through company (LTC) election is unavailable because the CK_IC is not a New Zealand-resident qualifying company. Non-resident companies receiving only New Zealand interest or dividends may elect for approved issuer levy (AIL) status under ss 32M-32R of the Stamp and Cheque Duties Act 1971 (non-applicable to service fees).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of annual taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) made in the course of a New Zealand taxable activity within any 12-month period. The same NZD 60,000 threshold applies to non-resident suppliers of remote services (including digital, professional, registered-agent, and secretarial services) where supplies are made to New Zealand-resident recipients; once the threshold is exceeded on a trailing 12-month look-back, registration is compulsory, with a prospectively effective date and no retrospective liability.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: 0% GST applies under s 11 of the GST Act 1985 where the supplier ships goods out of New Zealand and exports them (or causes their export) within 28 days of the time of supply; where goods are delivered to a port or airport in New Zealand for the purpose of being shipped, the export must be completed within 28 days of the delivery date, and the supplier must hold satisfactory evidence of export. Services: 0% GST applies under s 11A to services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, unless the services are supplied directly in connection with (i) land in New Zealand; (ii) movable personal property situated in New Zealand (except goods exported or for repair/processing); or (iii) physical services performed in New Zealand for the direct benefit of an individual present in New Zealand. Registered-agent and offshore corporate secretarial services supplied to Cook Islands International Companies (CK_IC) whose directors, registered office, and central management sit outside New Zealand qualify for zero-rating only where the services are performed outside New Zealand; secretarial work physically performed by staff in New Zealand is standard-rated unless it falls within the s 11A(1)(e) remote-services carve-out for a non-resident recipient. The taxpayer must obtain and retain: CK_IC certificate of incorporation and tax-exempt certificate, copy of the service agreement indicating offshore performance, confirmation of non-resident status (registered office in Rarotonga/Aitutaki; non-resident director declarations), and evidence that the CK_IC derives no New Zealand-source income.

  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from New Zealand (for goods)

No real-time fiscal clearance, government API pre-clearance, or digital stamping regime applies. New Zealand operates a self-assessment e-invoicing model under the PEPPOL (Pan-European Public Procurement OnLine) framework, PEPPOL BIS Billing 3.0; e-invoicing is voluntary for commerce, but all central government agencies must receive e-invoices through an accredited PEPPOL Access Point and the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE) maintains the NZ e-Invoicing infrastructure. GST returns are filed via myIR on monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly cycles; payments are due in full by the return filing date. Invoices issued under s 24 of the GST Act 1985 must contain the words tax invoice, the supplier's name, IRD/GST number, date of issue, description, quantity and price. Records, including the PEPPOL e-invoice XML files and any digital clearance logs, must be retained for 7 years (TAA 1994, s 51).

Inland Revenue audits zero-rating claims with particular focus on related-party cross-border services, sham offices, and round-tripping structures involving tax-exempt Cook Islands ICs. Where zero-rating is incorrectly claimed, the supplier is liable for the uncollected output GST, denial of corresponding input credits, use-of-money interest at the prescribed quarterly underpayment rate, and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for failing to take reasonable care (s 141B), 20% for an unacceptable tax position (s 141C), 40% for gross carelessness (s 141D), 100% for an abusive tax position (s 141E), and 150% for evasion (s 141EB). The general anti-avoidance rule (s BG1 Income Tax Act 2007; s 76 GST Act 1985) permits the Commissioner to reconstruct the arrangement retrospectively, clawing back refunded GST with up to 4 years' retrospectivity for non-disclosure. Criminal prosecution under s 143A (evasion) carries a fine up to NZD 200,000 for a body corporate and/or up to 5 years' imprisonment for individuals; fraudulent GST refund claims may also be prosecuted under s 228 of the Crimes Act 1961 (up to 7 years' imprisonment). New Zealand has no statutory export-proceeds repatriation deadline; however, the 180-day period is used by Inland Revenue as a commercial benchmark — export invoices remaining unsettled beyond 180 days without explanation are treated as not genuinely exported and the zero-rating is clawed back. For associated-party offshore service fees, failure to maintain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (ss GC1-GC14, OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines) triggers a documentation penalty of NZD 20,000 (first request) and NZD 50,000 (each subsequent request) in addition to any transfer pricing adjustment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully convertible currency under an open capital account. Current account items, capital transactions, profit repatriation, dividends, liquidation proceeds, and service contract payments are freely transferable in any currency without prior central bank approval. Non-residents may hold New Zealand dollar and foreign-currency accounts with registered banks. The Overseas Investment Act 2005 may apply only to certain 'sensitive' land and significant business assets acquisitions; ordinary treasury transactions for a registered agent/corporate secretarial services entity are not restricted. There are no profit remittance hurdles beyond standard taxation and CRS/FATCA self-certification compliance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. Service providers and exporters may retain receipts in any currency, hold foreign-currency accounts, and remit or convert NZD freely in accordance with commercial requirements. The Reserve Bank imposes no obligation to convert export service earnings into New Zealand dollars.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua); foreign exchange administration is fully liberalised, with no separate exchange control department requiring general approvals. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) regulates financial service providers and conduct.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility is Medium for ancillary treasury operations, but Low as a substitute for a licensed bank relationship. New Zealand-incorporated financial service providers may be registered under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must be supervised under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Non-bank payment service providers may offer virtual accounts, payment initiation, and SWIFT-style disbursements but cannot take deposits, do not have Payment NZ clearing membership, and are not authorised as banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021. Offshore EMIs face significant legal constraints: they are generally not licensed deposit-takers in New Zealand and must rely on New Zealand partner banks for local clearing; many cannot provide NZD Direct Debit/Credit via the Payments NZ bulk electronic clearing system or real-time ESAS settlement. A registered agent should establish a primary banking relationship with a tier-1 bank and use EMIs only for multi-currency receivables or disbursement convenience.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections to the New Zealand International Company are routed by SWIFT MT103 from the overseas shareholder or lender. No exchange control approval is required. The remittance must clearly state the business purpose code (e.g., 'Equity Capital' or 'Intercompany Loan') and reference the New Zealand Companies Office company number. The receiving bank will require a certified copy of the share subscription resolution or intercompany loan agreement, a completed source of funds declaration, and CRS/FATCA self-certification forms. Funds must be received in the entity's NZD or foreign-currency operational account; for client trust money, a segregated client account must be used. The bank will verify that the remitter account name matches the shareholder/UBO documentation.Outbound disbursements are cleared using: (i) Payments NZ's bulk electronic clearing system (BECS) for NZD domestic and trans-Tasman periodic payments; (ii) the Reserve Bank's Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) for high-value real-time gross settlement; (iii) SWIFT MT103 for single cross-border wire transfers; and (iv) documentary letters of credit (SWIFT MT700/MT707) for international trade settlements governed by UCP 600. The entity's bank may issue a commercial LC for the settlement of goods/services on behalf of clients where trade finance facilities are established. All cross-border payments require a valid purpose code and may trigger correspondent bank due diligence; payments to sanctioned or high-risk jurisdictions will be blocked. Trade disbursements must be supported by invoices, contracts, and the bank's AML/CFT screening outputs.Deliverable FX forward contracts (NZD/USD, NZD/EUR, NZD/GBP, NZD/AUD, NZD/JPY) offered by New Zealand registered banks for hedging committed fee income or disbursement expenses.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) on restricted currencies that cannot be settled deliverable onshore, executed with a licensed bank or authorised financial product provider.Cross-currency swaps (FX swaps and plain-vanilla cross-currency interest rate swaps) for aligning FX risk on medium-term liquidity and matching the currency of fee receivables.European-style FX options (simple puts/calls) to hedge forecast foreign-currency cash flows, provided they are not used for speculative trading.Foreign-currency accounts (multi-currency accounts) as natural treasury hedging mechanisms allowing fee receipts and disbursements in the same currency without conversion.Plain-vanilla interest rate swaps or forward rate agreements to hedge NZD interest rate risk on any bank credit facilities (non-speculative).

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility is Medium for ancillary treasury operations, but Low as a substitute for a licensed bank relationship. New Zealand-incorporated financial service providers may be registered under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must be supervised under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Non-bank payment service providers may offer virtual accounts, payment initiation, and SWIFT-style disbursements but cannot take deposits, do not have Payment NZ clearing membership, and are not authorised as banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021. Offshore EMIs face significant legal constraints: they are generally not licensed deposit-takers in New Zealand and must rely on New Zealand partner banks for local clearing; many cannot provide NZD Direct Debit/Credit via the Payments NZ bulk electronic clearing system or real-time ESAS settlement. A registered agent should establish a primary banking relationship with a tier-1 bank and use EMIs only for multi-currency receivables or disbursement convenience.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Cross-border transactions with associated non-resident enterprises aggregating NZD 2,000,000 or more in an income year are the Inland Revenue's administrative threshold for maintaining contemporaneous Local File transfer pricing documentation. Master File and Country-by-Country reporting obligations apply to multinational groups with consolidated group revenue equal to or above EUR 750,000,000. Where the aggregate transaction amount exceeds NZD 5,000,000, a full OECD-aligned transfer pricing policy and economic analysis report is required.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) code as in certain offshore centres; nevertheless, the Income Tax Act 2007, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s BG 1), the reconstruction provision (s GB 1), and associated-person rules (ss GC 1–GC 14) impose a substance-over-form standard. A registered-agent International Company must evidence real premises, qualified staff, board meetings, minutes, bank accounts, and accounting records in New Zealand. If the company is wholly administered offshore and the New Zealand registered office performs no substantive secretarial function, Inland Revenue may disregard the entity, reconstruct the taxable income, or treat the arrangement as a sham, exposing the offshore shareholders to New Zealand-source income assessments and NRWT.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Filing

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under s 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994: imprisonment for a term not exceeding 5 years and a fine not exceeding NZD 50,000 per offence (for a body corporate, a fine not exceeding NZD 500,000). Civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax, plus late payment penalties and use-of-money interest on the full reassessment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participated in the fraudulent filing are jointly and severally liable for the company's tax shortfall under s 61 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Directors additionally face personal criminal fines of up to NZD 50,000, travel restraint orders, freezing of personal assets, and disqualification from managing companies for up to 10 years under s 383 of the Companies Act 1993.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Reconstruction of the taxpayer's position under s GB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, resulting in additional assessable income; shortfall penalty of 100% for an 'abusive tax position'; strike-off from the New Zealand Companies Register for failure to deliver annual returns; revocation of the certificate of incorporation.

Director personal liability: Directors can be made personally liable for company debts where the company traded recklessly under s 135 of the Companies Act 1993; liability for PAYE, GST, and NRWT unpaid as a 'director penalty' under the Tax Administration Act 1994; in egregious substance-abuse cases, directors face prosecution for criminal tax evasion.
AML/CFT and Beneficial Ownership Deficiency

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty proceedings in the High Court under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009: up to NZD 1,000,000 per breach for a body corporate and NZD 200,000 for an individual. Criminal liability (for wilful or reckless conduct) carries a fine of up to NZD 500,000 and imprisonment up to 12 months for a natural person; the reporting entity's registration may be suspended or revoked.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the company's AML programme failures where they failed to exercise due diligence; personal penalties of up to NZD 200,000 per contravention may be imposed directly on directors, alongside prohibition orders banning them from acting as directors or managers of any reporting entity for up to 10 years.
Transfer Pricing / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner of Inland Revenue may adjust the non-arm's length consideration to an arm's length price under ss GC 6–GC 14 of the Income Tax Act 2007. Shortfall penalties apply at 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, and 100% for an abusive tax position; late payment penalties and use-of-money interest compound until satisfaction of the reassessment.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or certify tax returns adopting artificial transfer prices may be personally assessed as directors for unpaid corporate tax liabilities; where the pricing was fraudulently documented, criminal evasion charges under s 143A may be laid against the directors individually, with exposure to imprisonment of up to 5 years and personal fines.
