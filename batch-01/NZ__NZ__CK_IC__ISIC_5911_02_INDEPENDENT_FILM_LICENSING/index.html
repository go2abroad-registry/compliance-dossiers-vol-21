Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone — Under New Zealand law, a company incorporated in New Zealand or managed in New Zealand is ordinarily a tax resident subject to entity-level taxation at 28% corporate income tax on worldwide income; however, the International Company (CK_IC) in this dossier is a foreign-incorporated limited liability vehicle that is not tax resident in New Zealand and has no New Zealand permanent establishment. New Zealand applies a territorial source rule to non-residents: the IC is assessable only on New Zealand-source income. Because the independent film licensing business involves royalties derived from non-resident licensees for rights exploited wholly outside New Zealand, the offshore income falls outside the New Zealand income tax net, functionally equivalent to a tax-exempt zone for foreign-source film licensing income. New Zealand-source income, if any, would be taxed at 28% with no preferential international business company rate. The IC must continuously defend its non-resident status and the absence of a New Zealand permanent establishment.

Available election forms

New Zealand has no elective 'check-the-box' classification regime comparable to the United States. The relevant statutorily available mechanisms are: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, available only to closely held New Zealand-resident companies with five or fewer eligible underlying owners, which produces full pass-through treatment; this election is not available to the non-resident International Company. (2) Qualifying Company (QC) election, a legacy imputation-based pass-through regime no longer open for new elections; only transitional rules apply to existing qualifying companies. (3) De facto non-resident election by maintaining offshore board of directors control under s YD 4 of the Income Tax Act 2007; no filing is required but contemporaneous evidence of offshore central management and control must be maintained. (4) Hybrid and branch mismatch rules in Part FH of the Income Tax Act 2007, which operate automatically and override contractual or entity form; a hybrid entity such as an IC that is treated as opaque in New Zealand but transparent in the counterparty jurisdiction will have deductions, credits, or inclusions adjusted to neutralise the mismatch, with no affirmative taxpayer election available. (5) GST elections: an eligible IC may elect the payments basis of accounting for GST, may select monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly filing periods, and may elect to treat cross-border services as zero-rated where the statutory conditions of s 11(1)(m) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 are satisfied.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 — a person, including a non-resident supplier, must register for New Zealand GST when the value of taxable supplies made in New Zealand in any 12-month period exceeds or is expected to exceed NZD 60,000; the threshold applies equally to non-resident suppliers of 'remote services' (including digital film content and electronic rights delivery) supplied to New Zealand consumers; an International Company making only zero-rated export supplies is not required to register but may voluntarily register under s 51 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 to recover input GST on New Zealand costs.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported by the supplier within 28 days of supply qualify for zero-rating under s 11(1)(a) and s 11(2)(a) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Services supplied to a non-resident person who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply are zero-rated under s 11(1)(m) and s 11(1)(n) where the services are not supplied directly in connection with land or goods situated in New Zealand. Independent film licensing constitutes a supply of services by virtue of s 5(5) of the GST Act, as the supply of a right or interest in intellectual property is a supply of services; the supply is zero-rated if (i) the licensee is not resident in New Zealand, (ii) the licensed film copyright is exploited wholly outside New Zealand, and (iii) the supply is not directly connected with New Zealand land or New Zealand-situate goods. Remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers, including digital delivery of film content, are instead taxable at 15% under the remote services provisions (Part 8 and s 8B of the GST Act). Zero-rating is a privilege that requires contemporaneous documentary evidence of export and offshore use; where that evidence is deficient, the supply is recharacterised as taxable at 15%.

Inland Revenue scrutiny of export zero-rating focuses on fictitious licences, shell-licensing structures, and overstated refund claims. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 escalate with the level of culpability: 20% of the tax shortfall for failure to take reasonable care, 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or fraudulent intent. Use-of-money interest accrues on all unpaid or clawed-back GST. Input tax credits previously allowed on exported supplies are retrospectively clawed back where the supporting export documentation is not retained for the 7-year statutory period and the supply is recharacterised as taxable at 15%. Criminal liability arises under s 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for wilfully evading GST, punishable by imprisonment for up to 5 years and fines of up to NZD 100,000 for individuals or NZD 300,000 for body corporates; knowingly furnishing false returns or creating false export invoices also attracts penalties under ss 143A and 143D of the TAA 1994, and persons who aid or direct structured export-GST fraud face accessory criminal liability.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains an entirely open capital account with no exchange controls and no approval gates for current or capital transactions. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is a free-floating, fully convertible currency. For the CK_IC (a non-resident, tax-exempt international company holding film distribution and licensing rights), the following flows are unrestricted: (a) current account – inbound licence fees, minimum guarantees, advance royalties, profit-sharing receipts and sub-licence income payable from foreign distributors; (b) capital account – shareholder equity injections, intercompany loans, loan repayments, dividends and capital distributions. There is no surrender requirement, no central-bank conversion mandate, no repatriation deadline, and no restrictions on holding foreign currency accounts in NZD, USD, EUR, GBP or AUD. The only real structural hurdles are bank-level KYC/AML and sanctions screening under the AML/CFT Act 2009, the mandatory FATCA/CRS due-diligence rules administered by Inland Revenue, and the New Zealand source rules in the Income Tax Act 2007 (in particular Subpart YD), which could theoretically expose a non-resident licensor to non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) under Part R if any royalty is paid by a New Zealand-resident distributor. Because the CK_IC derives income from offshore sub-licensees rather than NZ payors, ordinary film-licensing royalties are not NZ-sourced, and profit/principal remittance can proceed through standard commercial banking channels without regulatory licence or FX permit.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. New Zealand dismantled its exchange-control machinery in the mid-1980s and imposes no statutory timeline for repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no obligation to surrender foreign currency to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. Film-licensing export receipts (royalties, minimum guarantees, territorial licence fees) may be retained offshore in original currencies, pooled in multi-currency accounts, converted to NZD at freely negotiated bank rates, or redeployed for co-production and distribution funding without any surrender condition. The only applicable constraints are transactional: New Zealand banks are required by the AML/CFT Act 2009 to report suspicious transactions and large cash transactions above NZD 10,000, and to perform enhanced due diligence on high-value wire transfers; these are financial-intelligence obligations, not exchange-control filings, and in no way restrict the CK_IC's treasury discretion.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) – the sole central bank and prudential regulator of registered banks in New Zealand. Foreign exchange control supervision under the Exchange Control Act 1953 is vestigial and inoperative; the operative regulatory architecture consists of the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021 for bank licensing and prudential supervision, the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) for financial crime controls, and the Financial Markets Authority and Department of Internal Affairs for reporting-entity supervision. Settlement infrastructure resides with the Reserve Bank's Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) and the NZClear systemically important payment system administered by the RBNZ.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM for operational collections and LOW for core treasury functions. Offshore digital EMI platforms registered in the United Kingdom or European Union (Wise, Airwallex, Currenxie, Revolut Business, Payoneer) can legally provide multi-currency IBAN/wallet services to a non-resident international company, and may on-board the CK_IC remotely; however, they are not prudentially licensed banks in New Zealand, cannot issue documentary letters of credit, cannot provide capital escrow accounts under NZ law, and are subject to restrictive risk appetite that usually rejects tax-exempt offshore IP holding companies absent a strong economic-substance file. A non-resident EMI providing financial services to an NZ nexus client must register on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008, and may become a reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009, which creates compliance friction. Optimal treasury design: use a licensed EMI only as a first-mile collection rail for small-value consumer-territory royalty receipts (under USD 50,000 per transaction), then sweep balances to the tier-1 NZ bank account; retain all escrow, LC, hedging, and large-value settlement functions with ANZ, BNZ, ASB or HSBC.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound routing for equity capital: a SWIFT MT103 wire from the shareholder, parent trust or ultimate holding company to the CK_IC's NZD or USD account at the selected tier-1 NZ bank. [X] PURSUANT TO BOARD RESOLUTION DATED [DATE]'; for shareholder loans, use 'INTERCOMPANY LOAN TO CK_IC REF [LOAN NO.]'. (3) Deliverable-conditioned payments to production companies and completion guarantors: commercial letters of credit issued by the NZ tier-1 bank under UCP 600 (SWIFT MT700/MT707), payable at sight or usance against presentation of the agreed documentary bundle including the delivery receipt from the distributor, the E&O insurance certificate, chain-of-title report and an invoice/commercial statement; this rail is used for minimum-guarantee-funded instalments conditioned on delivery of DCP or negative materials; (4) NZD domestic settlement: payments within New Zealand clear in real time through the RBNZ's Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) and NZClear, with same-day finality; foreign-currency legs settle through the bank's correspondent network (e.g., USD via New York CHIPS/Fedwire, EUR via Frankfurt/TARGET2). Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand transfer pricing thresholds: Local File required if related-party cross-border transactions exceed NZ$10 million in aggregate annual value; Master File required if group annual turnover exceeds NZ$1.1 billion and cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZ$10 million. For smaller International Companies, contemporaneous documentation should still be prepared to support arm's length pricing on film licensing and distribution arrangements.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) like the UAE. However, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under the Income Tax Act 2007 s BG 1, together with transfer pricing rules under s GC 13-GC 14, require genuine commercial substance. A CK_IC deriving film licensing income must demonstrate real decision-making, active management of intellectual property, and substantive contractual functions to avoid recharacterization as a conduit or sham. In the absence of substance, Inland Revenue may attribute income to a New Zealand permanent establishment or to a New Zealand resident controlling entity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 143B for evasion or defeat of tax: fines up to NZ$50,000 for a first offence and NZ$100,000 for a subsequent offence, plus three times the evaded tax. Imprisonment up to 5 years may apply for serious wilful evasion under s 145A.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for tax evasion penalties under the Companies Act 1993 s 300, and Inland Revenue may issue a director penalty notice under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 139B if company tax remains unpaid. Civil restitution, travel bans (where an arrest warrant is issued), and imprisonment up to 5 years for directors who knowingly participate in tax evasion.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may apply the GAAR to cancel the tax advantage; reassess income as New Zealand-source and impose shortfall penalties of 50% (for dominant purpose of tax avoidance) and use-of-money interest. Transfer pricing adjustment penalties up to 150% of the underpaid tax for gross carelessness or intentional breach.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company found to be a sham or facade may face personal liability for the full tax liability plus penalties under the Companies Act 1993 s 301, and could be subject to disqualification orders under the Companies Act 1993 ss 382-383.
AML / Counter-Terrorism Financing

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties up to NZ$2 million for each offence against the AML/CFT Act, plus remediation orders. Criminal penalties for money laundering under s 243 of the Crimes Act 1961: imprisonment up to 7 years. Enforcement action by the Financial Markets Authority or Internal Affairs.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for AML/CFT failures if they were knowingly concerned in the breach; criminal conviction can lead to imprisonment up to 7 years and personal fines up to NZ$200,000. Travel bans may be imposed at the border pending investigation.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustment under s GC 13 to reflect arm's length consideration; shortfall penalty under s 141A of 20% of the resulting tax shortfall for not taking reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and 150% for evasion. A master file/local file failure can result in additional administrative penalties and increased scrutiny.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable under the director penalty regime for unpaid tax arising from transfer pricing adjustments if the company cannot pay, and for signing false transfer pricing documentation.
