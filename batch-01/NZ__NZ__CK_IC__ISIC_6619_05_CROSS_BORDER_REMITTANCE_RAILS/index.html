Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office, an operational arm of the Ministry of Business, Innovation, and Employment (MBIE), administered under the Companies Act 1993 and the Financial Markets Authority (for governance matters).

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (New Zealand), principally sections 12–16 (constitution and incorporation), section 13 (limitation on registered office), section 19 (filing requirement for incorporation), section 34 (registered office requirement), sections 122–153 (share capital and shareholder rights), and sections 128–157 (director duties and officer appointments).

Formation / constitutional filings

Form NZ Company Incorporation Application (Companies Office Form 1), filed electronically via the Companies Office 'Register a Company' online portal, together with the Company Constitution (if adopted) and Consent to Appointment forms (Director Consent, Shareholder Consent, and Officer Consent as applicable).

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up or authorized share capital is prescribed under the Companies Act 1993 for a standard limited liability International Company (IC). The company may issue shares with nil or nominal paid-up capital, provided the issued consideration is fully lawful under the Companies Act 1993 and any applicable anti-money-laundering obligations. No escrow account or capital deposit is required for incorporation; however, the company must maintain a registered office in New Zealand and a local or appointed agent for service of documents.

Funding & unlock path: Compliance with the Fire and Emergency New Zealand Act 2017 requiring a Fire Safety and Evacuation Scheme for commercial premises; Building Act 2004 compliance requiring a Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) and annual inspection certificates for any buildings with active fire-suppression systems; Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA) requiring a workplace incident management plan, hazard registry, first-aid facilities, and appointment of a health and safety officer; structural load certification confirming floor loading capacity for server racks and safe electrical wiring certification under the New Zealand Electrical Code of Practice; and the Privacy Act 2020 requiring a security and storage clearance for any facility processing personal data of consumers in cross-border remittance volumes exceeding 10,000 transactions annually.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Confirm the International Company (CK_IC) is non-resident for New Zealand tax purposes — incorporated offshore, with central management and control exercised outside New Zealand, no New Zealand-sourced income, and no fixed establishment or permanent establishment in New Zealand. Step 2: If the foreign company carries on business in New Zealand or establishes a branch, register the overseas company with the New Zealand Companies Office to obtain an NZBN (New Zealand Business Number); a purely offshore remittance platform with no NZ nexus may not require NZBN registration. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box entity classification regime; every company is taxed at entity level under the Income Tax Act 2007 unless it validly elects look-through company (LTC) status under s HB 11, but that election is generally unavailable to a non-resident International Company with corporate shareholders. The elections relevant to this cross-border remittance entity include: (i) voluntary GST registration by a non-resident under s 51(3) of the GST Act 1985; (ii) GST grouping election under s 55 to treat a New Zealand branch and related NZ-resident group members as a single GST group; (iii) election to account for GST on a cash basis under s 19A where annual turnover does not exceed NZD 2 million; (iv) election to adopt an approved GST apportionment or ratio method for mixed exempt and zero-rated financial supplies; (v) election to zero-rate remote and financial services supplied to non-residents under s 11A, supported by contemporaneous non-resident declarations; (vi) election to account in a foreign functional currency under s EC 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 for income tax and, in limited offshore circumstances, for GST; and (vii) hybrid mismatch arrangements under Subpart FH — including CFC and interest-attribution elections and IRD notification forms — required to neutralise double non-taxation in cross-border financing, remittance float, or interbank arrangement structures.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalized, free-floating currency regime with zero exchange controls on either current or capital account transactions. The Historical Foreign Exchange Control Regulations were abolished in 1984, and there is no statutory requirement for exchange control approval, central bank license, or prior notification for inbound capital injections, dividend repatriation, interest, royalty, or service fee remittances to non-residents. Capital account transfers (equity, shareholder loans, disposals) are freely convertible without any quantitative ceiling. The residual compliance obligations arise solely under the AML/CFT regime: all cross-border wire transfers must include full originator and beneficiary information under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act), and cash border declarations apply above NZD 10,000. Profit remittance for a Tax-Exempt-Zone entity is further unrestrained by any withholding tax or thin-capitalization hurdle, though the parent entity must maintain a physical or registered presence evidence trail to satisfy the economic substance expectations of the bank relationship.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No export proceeds surrender rules apply in New Zealand. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation of export or service receipts and no mandatory conversion ratio into NZD. An International Company conducting cross-border remittance services may lawfully retain all gross receivable balances in foreign currency-denominated correspondent accounts, convert only as required for operational settlement, and freely deploy proceeds for on-lending or treasury purposes. The only non-exchange-control constraints are record-keeping obligations under the AML/CFT Act, which require retention of all transaction records for a minimum of five years, and the general duty to report to the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) any suspicious transaction irrespective of amount.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), exercising prudential regulation and monetary policy under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989, together with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for conduct regulation and the Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) as anti-money laundering supervisor for money remitters.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-Low with conditional approval. New Zealand does not maintain a distinct Electronic Money Institution (EMI) or Payment Institution license class comparable to the UK FCA or EU PSD2 regime. Foreign fintech EMIs (e.g., Wise, Airwallex, Conotoxia) may operate cross-border into New Zealand only by: (i) registering on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008; (ii) becoming a reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act with the Department of Internal Affairs as supervisor; and (iii) obtaining a money remittance / money service business registration with the Reserve Bank of New Zealand if engaging in deposit-like or stored-value activities. In practice, offshore EMIs must partner with a New Zealand bank or a locally incorporated non-bank deposit taker to hold settlement funds in a segregated trust account, because NZ banks will not directly open settlement accounts for unregistered foreign EMIs. For a consumer remittance agency targeting Local_Cash_Pickup_Clearing, onboarding with an offshore EMI is operationally feasible but strategically inadvisable as the primary rail, given the heightened reputational risk, restricted access to ESAS clearing, and the inability to provide SWIFT-insourced settlement without a licensed local entity.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocol for an inbound capital injection from a non-resident shareholder to the CK_IC entity: (i) remitting bank must transmit SWIFT MT103 with field 50K (originator) indicating the ultimate beneficial owner entity or named individual; field 59 (beneficiary) using the exact legal name and New Zealand bank account number per the NZD or USD operational account; field 70 (remittance information) explicitly quoting the purpose code 'CAP' or 'EQUITY CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION' together with the phrase 'Shareholder Contribution – no exchange control approval required'; and field 72 (bank-to-bank information) referencing the underlying corporate resolution. (ii) Beneficiary bank onboarding must be completed with the 'Institutional Investor Certificate' and a copy of the board resolution approving the capital injection, the subscription agreement and share register update; the bank will apply FATCA/CRS reporting based on entity tax classification; since NZ has no CFC thin-cap rules, no cap is imposed, but the injection must be booked via the ESAS (Exchange Settlement Account System) settlement window in NZD if conversion is needed, or held as USD Nostro balance to fund outbound SWIFT payments without FX conversion. (iii) Notification obligation: if the injection exceeds NZD 10,000 and is transported by cash, border declaration is required; for bank-wire injection there is no pre-notification, but the sending bank may require a purpose of payment statement for capital account statistics compiled by Statistics New Zealand under the Balance of Payments framework.Clearing rails for outbound trade settlements and beneficiary cash-out operations: (i) SWIFT MT103 (customer credit transfer) for banked beneficiaries in final destination countries, executed through the ANZ or BNZ correspondent banking network with gpi tracking; (ii) MT202 COV for interbank cover, only where the beneficiary bank requires multi-layered cover because of correspondent limits; (iii) Commercial Letters of Credit (LC) for larger trade-facilitation settlements, where the New Zealand remittance company's treasury acts as instructing party to issue an irrevocable LC on behalf of an expatriate worker paying for goods or services, secured by the segregated settlement account; (iv) Local_Cash_Pickup_Clearing via a licensed payout-agent network (e.g., in Pacific Island countries, India, China via UnionPay cash-point partners), with funds pre-funded as Nostro balances or settlement via SWIFT MT103 and the agent crediting itself through a local clearing channel (e.g., India's IMPS/NEFT, China's CNAPS); (v) within New Zealand, the domestic leg of any refund or error-correction payment is cleared via the ESAS real-time gross settlement system using the CLS (Continuous Linked Settlement) window for cross-currency swaps; all outbound disbursements above NZD 10,000 must carry full originator information under the AML/CFT Traveller's Rule and FIU reporting thresholds. For unbanked beneficiaries, the local cash pickup rail is secured by daily aggregation of payouts in the agent network, where the New Zealand entity holds the agent's cash float via a margin deposit account and performs daily reconciliation through a web-based settlement file (SWIFT MT191, MT300 for FX conversion).Outright Forward FX Contracts (plain vanilla, deliverable) with maturities of 1-week to 18-months, executed with ANZ, BNZ, or Westpac dealing desks under ISDA or NZFMA Master Agreement terms, used to hedge the NZD/USD working-capital mismatch between inbound USD funding and outbound USD settlement flows.Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) on restricted or thinly traded destination-corridor currencies (e.g., Indian Rupee, Chinese Renminbi, Nigerian Naira, Indonesian Rupiah) where deliverable liquidity is unavailable via onshore clearing; all NDFs must be executed with a licensed New Zealand lender or an authorized registered bank to remain outside the ambit of the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 retail derivatives regime.Cross-Currency Swaps (XCY) for medium-term hedging of any shareholder loan subordinated debt provided alongside equity, enabling the CK_IC entity to swap NZD funding into USD or AUD without embedding speculative exposure; must be classified as treasury hedging under the fair-value hedge designation with full documentation in accordance with NZ IFRS 9.FX Call Options (long-only, purchased puts and calls) to create a risk-reversal collar on the largest single remittance corridor; New Zealand law permits the purchase of options as hedging instruments, but the writing (short sale) of unhedged options is prohibited for a non-regulated corporate treasury under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 and may be deemed a speculative derivative requiring a FSP license.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% (standard New Zealand company income tax rate for NZ tax resident companies; no progressive corporate rate structure)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% statutory non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder. Under an applicable double tax agreement, the portfolio dividend rate is generally 15%, with a 5% rate available to a beneficial-owner company that meets substantial shareholding thresholds (e.g. 10%, 25%, or 80%, depending on the treaty and entity type). Fully imputed dividends may be structured under the New Zealand imputation and supplementary dividend regimes to align the final tax burden with the treaty rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not prescribe a single statutory threshold in dollars, but Inland Revenue requires contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation whenever cross-border related-party transactions in an income year aggregate to NZ$1 million or more. This includes affiliations for remittance platform licences, IT services, management fees, intercompany loans, and payment-processing charges. For multinational groups with consolidated revenue of NZ$1.125 billion or more, Country-by-Country Reporting is mandatory, with an accompanying OECD Master File. A Local File is expected for any New Zealand entity with significant related-party transactions, regardless of the CbCR threshold.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations. Instead, substance is assessed under ordinary tax principles: (1) central management and control determines corporate tax residence; (2) presence of a permanent establishment or fixed base in New Zealand can subject a non-resident to tax; and (3) the general anti-avoidance rule in Income Tax Act 2007 s BG 1 permits Inland Revenue to recharacterise arrangements lacking commercial substance. A cross-border remittance company must demonstrate real physical presence: an office, employees, board meetings, strategic decisions in New Zealand, a local AML/CFT licence, bank accounts, and customer contracts. Absence of these indicia will cause Inland Revenue to treat the arrangement as a sham and reassess the income in New Zealand.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Income Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: On conviction under s 143A or s 144B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, an individual is liable to a fine not exceeding NZ$50,000 (or NZ$100,000 for a subsequent offence) and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years. A body corporate may be fined up to NZ$250,000. Inland Revenue will also reassess the tax shortfall and may add maximum shortfall penalties of 150% of the avoided tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in or sign false returns can be prosecuted personally. The corporate veil is pierced where the International Company is merely a façade; directors face criminal conviction, civil restitution orders for the company's unpaid tax, disqualification from managing a company under the Companies Act 1993, and, where criminal proceedings are pending, court-imposed travel bans and surrender-of-passport conditions.
AML/CFT Supervisory Breach for Remittance Rails

Statutory sanction: A body corporate can be liable to civil penalties of up to NZ$2 million per contravention under the AML/CFT Act 2009, with individual officers exposed to civil penalties up to NZ$60,000. A criminal conviction for money laundering under the Crimes Act 1961 carries imprisonment of up to 7 years and forfeiture of derived assets. The Financial Markets Authority or Department of Internal Affairs may also suspend or cancel the remittance licence.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers are personally liable where they knew or ought to have known of the AML/CFT failure. Sanctions include civil pecuniary penalties, compensation orders to affected persons or Inland Revenue, director disqualification, and imprisonment for deliberate involvement in money laundering. Travel bans and asset-preservation orders are commonly obtained during prosecution and extradition processes.
Shell Company / Sham Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue will apply s BG 1 to disregard the form of the arrangement and reconstruct assessable income on substance over form. The taxpayer will face reassessment, shortfall penalties up to 150%, late payment penalties, and possible deregistration under the Companies Act 1993. Where the AML/CFT licence was obtained through false declarations, the regulator may cancel the licence and publish a formal warning.

Director personal liability: Directors of a sham International Company can be made personally liable for company debts and unpaid tax under s 300 of the Companies Act 1993 for reckless trading or carrying on business in an oppressive manner. Court orders may include civil restitution, prohibition from acting as a director of any New Zealand company for up to five years, and travel bans pending asset-recovery or extradition proceedings.
