Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital exists and no escrow account is required for an ordinary New Zealand limited liability company. The company must have at least one share and one shareholder, but the share capital may be nil paid, partly paid or fully paid in cash or non-cash consideration. However, the company must have at least one director resident in New Zealand or, if none, an eligible agent under s 10(2) of the Companies Act 1993; a separate physical registered office in New Zealand is mandatory.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD), New Zealand Government; online portal: myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz/myIR)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity engaging in New Zealand tax-affected activities must apply for an IRD number using the Non-Resident Companies IRD Number application. The application requires completion of form IR742 (Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application) or registration through myIR where eligibility exists, supported by certified constitutive documents (Certificate of Incorporation, Memorandum and Articles of Association), evidence of foreign tax residence, and details of the New Zealand resident agent or tax representative if the company is not carrying on a taxable activity but has New Zealand-sourced income. For VAT/GST purposes, registration is effected separately under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 using form GST1 (for non-resident businesses) where the non-resident carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand or makes supplies in the course of that activity. Compulsory registration applies to any person carrying on a taxable activity whose total value of supplies in a 12-month period exceeds or is expected to exceed NZD 60,000; non-resident suppliers of remote services (including digital services, franchise royalties, and licensing fees) are subject to the offshore supplier registration regime under section 60(1B) of the GST Act.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone: A Cook Islands International Company (CK_IC) is a tax-exempt offshore entity under the Cook Islands International Companies Act 1981-82; it is resident for tax purposes in the Cook Islands only if its management and control is exercised there, and if it obtains a tax exemption certificate it is exempt from Cook Islands tax on foreign-sourced income. For New Zealand tax purposes, the entity is treated as a non-resident company under the Income Tax Act 2007 unless it has a fixed establishment in New Zealand and derives New Zealand-sourced business income. The CK_IC will be classified as a foreign company with entity-level tax treatment in New Zealand only to the extent it derives New Zealand-sourced income that is not exempt under a Double Tax Agreement or the residual non-resident withholding tax regime; no New Zealand pass-through eligibility applies, and the entity is outside the New Zealand look-through company (LTC) and partnership regimes.

Available election forms

Available elections include: (1) New Zealand GST registration election for non-resident suppliers of remote services under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 to register and charge GST on a reverse-charge basis where the recipient is a non-resident; (2) election to use the special GST margin scheme for secondhand goods, not applicable to franchise business; (3) election under section FC 17 of the Income Tax Act 2007 for a foreign company to file a New Zealand return and claim deductions against New Zealand-sourced income; (4) hybrid mismatch election mechanisms under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018, including the ability to elect to align tax treatment of the CK_IC with New Zealand entity treatment for a controlled foreign company (CFC) or foreign branch where applicable, subject to the OECD hybrid mismatch rules; (5) no check-the-box election equivalent exists under New Zealand tax law; the CK_IC should instead rely on the CFC attribution rules and the branch equivalent test for foreign entity classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Compulsory registration threshold: NZD 60,000 (total value of taxable supplies in any 12-month period, or expected to exceed NZD 60,000 in the following 12 months, for a person carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand). Non-resident offshore suppliers of remote services (e.g., franchise royalties, digital platform services, licensing of syrups formulations) are required to register and charge GST on supplies made to non-resident recipients residing in New Zealand once their total supplies to New Zealand-resident customers exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period. Voluntary registration is available to non-residents even below the threshold where the person carries on a taxable activity.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from New Zealand are zero-rated for GST where the supplier removes the goods from New Zealand within 28 days after supply and supplies them to a recipient who is outside New Zealand, in accordance with section 11(1)(a) and section 11(2)(a) of the GST Act 1985. The supply of services (including franchise licensing, royalty payments, and the supply of a business or part thereof) is zero-rated where the services are performed for a person who is non-resident and not in New Zealand when the services are performed, and the services are supplied in the course of the supplier's taxable activity (section 11A(1)(a)). The CK_IC exporting syrups and beverage formulations from New Zealand to international franchisees must retain contemporaneous documentary evidence proving physical export, including the Customs Export Declaration. Zero-rating for offshore supplies of intellectual property and franchise rights also applies to non-resident recipients, but where the supply is deemed to be a supply of services that is connected with New Zealand land or goods situated in New Zealand at the time of supply, domestic GST at 15% applies. Zero-rating is also denied for services physically performed in New Zealand where the benefit is enjoyed in New Zealand, and for supplies to non-resident business recipients who are GST-registered in New Zealand.

New Zealand has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or continuous transaction controls regime. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and governed by the GST Act 1985 and Inland Revenue guidelines; e-invoices must contain all prescribed tax invoice details (supplier name, GST number, invoice date, description, amount, GST amount, and zero-rating statement where applicable). The New Zealand Tax Invoice and e-Invoicing framework aligns with the Peppol network; registration on the Peppol Business Interoperability Framework is optional. There is no statutory requirement for digital stamping, fiscal API integration, or pre-clearance approval from Inland Revenue before invoice issuance. However, a registered person must issue a tax invoice within 5 working days of a recipient's request, and DEAR or other accounting systems must be capable of producing full GST-compliant tax invoices and GST return filings via myIR.

New Zealand Inland Revenue and New Zealand Customs Service undertake joint audit scrutiny of export zero-rating claims. Where a taxpayer incorrectly zero-rates a domestic supply or fails to retain the required export documentation, the GST output tax is retrospectively clawed back and assessed as a shortfall in GST payable, with interest pursuant to section 107 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, plus a shortfall penalty of 20% for an unacceptable tax position (or 40% where gross carelessness or an abusive tax position is established). Knowingly or recklessly lodging a false GST return with an intention to evade GST is a criminal offence under section 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994, carrying a maximum fine of NZD 50,000 for the first offence and NZD 100,000 for subsequent offences, as well as a possible term of imprisonment under section 143A(2)(e). Anti-money laundering countermeasures under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 require banks to furnish transaction records; any false export documentation or circular trading patterns for fraudulent zero-rating may trigger a report to the Financial Intelligence Unit, and the New Zealand Customs Service may impose penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 of up to NZD 10,000 per false declaration and forfeiture of goods.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% (New Zealand resident company rate, Income Tax Act 2007, Sch 1 Part B). The CK_IC (Cook Islands International Company) is tax-exempt in the Cook Islands (0% CIT on offshore income) and is treated as a non-resident company in New Zealand; NZ tax exposure is limited to NZ-sourced income (royalties, syrup sales with source in NZ, and income attributable to a NZ fixed establishment).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory NRWT on dividends to non-residents: 30% (Income Tax Act 2007, Sch 3). Treaty reduced rates: generally 15% for portfolio dividends; 5% for fully imputed dividends to a 10% corporate shareholder under the NZ-Australia DTA; 15% for most other comprehensive DTAs. The Cook Islands has no comprehensive DTA with New Zealand, so dividends paid from the NZ operating entity to the CK_IC attract the full 30% NRWT, subject to imputation credits reducing the economic burden where the NZ payer attaches credits.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a 31 March balance date: return due 7 July of the same calendar year (agent extension: 31 March of the following year); terminal tax due 7 February (agent: 7 April). For non-standard balance dates, the return is due on the 7th day of the 4th month after balance date and terminal tax on the 7th day of the 7th month after balance date.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: mandatory contemporaneous documentation where aggregate cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZD 1,000,000 in an income year (general goods/services/intangibles) or NZD 3,000,000 for related-party interest/financial transactions. Master File and Country-by-Country Report: mandatory for MNE groups with consolidated group revenue of at least NZD 1,150,000,000 (EUR 750,000,000 equivalent). The CK_IC franchise and syrup supply structure must maintain transfer pricing documentation even below the NZD 1m threshold where the IRD's risk assessment identifies unclear pricing, excessive royalty rates or loss-making NZ franchise entities.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no codified 'Economic Substance Regulations' statute analogous to the UAE/BVI/Cayman regimes. However, the CK_IC must demonstrate real economic substance under: (1) the NZ General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s BG 1, ITA 2007) which recharacterises tax avoidance arrangements where the IC lacks commercial reality; (2) ss GC 13-14 transfer pricing rules requiring the actual allocation of functions, assets and risks (OECD DEMPE analysis) - the IC must own, develop, enhance and manage the boba tea IP and syrup formulation; (3) the OECD Multilateral Instrument Principal Purpose Test (PPT) where treaty benefits are sought - unavailable to the Cook Islands IC but relevant to any chain using a treaty-resident intermediary; (4) NZ thin capitalisation rules (s FE 4) limiting interest deductibility in the NZ borrower to a 60% debt-to-asset safe harbour where the CK_IC lends to the NZ entity; (5) the Indefeasible Anti-Money Laundering / Know-Your-Customer framework (AML/CFT Act 2009) requiring beneficial ownership and substance verification of the IC by NZ banks and franchisees. Practical substance for the IC: registered office, local corporate secretary, at least two resident directors, board meeting minutes of genuine IP/DEMPE decisions, a substantive IP register, royalty benchmarking study, and a material intercompany agreement with contemporaneous documentation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Offence (s 143A, Tax Administration Act 1994)

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction: fine up to NZD 250,000 for a company and imprisonment for up to 5 years (for individuals involved); civil shortfall penalty of 100% of the evaded tax (s 141E); re-assessment with the 4-year time bar extended indefinitely for evasion; UOMI and late payment penalties apply; restraint and forfeiture of assets under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers are treated as principals: personal fines up to NZD 50,000 per offence and imprisonment up to 5 years. Directors of the NZ borrower/franchise entity are personally liable for unpaid withholding taxes (PAYE/RWT/NRWT) under s 172-174 TAA; fraudulent trading liability under s 300 Companies Act 1993 for debts; departure prohibition orders (travel bans) may be issued under s 174B TAA for serious tax debt; disqualification from directorship up to 10 years.
Shell Company and Lack of Economic Substance (s BG 1 GAAR / OECD MLI PPT / AML/CFT Act 2009)

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may cancel tax benefits retrospectively and reconstruct the transactions: royalty and syrup payments recharacterised as dividends to the real controllers or re-attributed to the NZ trading entity; additional tax at 28% on the reassessed profit; shortfall penalties: 20% for unacceptable tax positions, 40% for abusive tax positions (s 141D); 100% penalty where evasion is proven; reversal of NRWT offsets; cancellation of any treaty claim; the AML/CFT supervisor may impose a civil penalty up to NZD 2 million for reporting entities failing CDD.

Director personal liability: Directors holding the ultimate economic interest cannot hide behind the corporate veil: joint and several liability for the company's tax debt under GAAR reconstructions; personal exposure for reckless trading (s 135 Companies Act 1993); adverse findings by banks automatically trigger account termination and reporting to the NZ Financial Intelligence Unit; travel bans under departure prohibition orders; disqualification and potential extradition proceedings where fraud is suspected.
