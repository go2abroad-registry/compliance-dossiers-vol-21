Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office or commercial mailbox service is not permissible as the sole registered office. The Companies Act 1993 requires a physical registered office in New Zealand, not being a post office box or document-only address, which is open to the public during normal business hours on every working day. New Zealand Inland Revenue (IR) and the New Zealand Companies Office will reject a commercial mailbox-only address for GST registration, payroll withholding obligations, or employment responsibilities. For a payroll and HR compliance SaaS platform with medium-risk AML exposure, banks and the FMA would require physical office presence, local directorship, and a genuine local decision-making presence. Nonetheless, a virtual office provider can be used as a supplementary correspondence address and for mail handling, but the registered office must remain a physical location where statutory records are kept and service of process can be effected. High-risk use of a pure virtual office with no human presence would almost certainly be refused by trading banks and may trigger enhanced tax information exchange reviews.

Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office (Te Tari Kamupene o Aotearoa), an operational business unit of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE), Wellington, New Zealand; online lodgement portal: https://app.companiesoffice.govt.nz (Companies Office electronic register platform).

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (New Zealand), particularly sections 10 (company name), 12 to 16 (registration and incorporation), 31 and 32 (effect of certificate of incorporation), 124 to 127 (share capital and share subscription terms), 152 and 153 (director residency and consent), 189 to 199 (records and registers), and 224 to 226 (registered office); supplemented by the Financial Reporting Act 2013, sections 35 to 38 (reporting exemptions for overseas-invested companies), and the New Zealand Business Number Act 2016 (automatic NZBN allocation upon incorporation).

Formation / constitutional filings

Form NZCN/1 (Application for Registration of a Company), lodged online through the Companies Office 'Register a Company' service; also uses Form NZCN/2 for name reservation, Form NZCN/4 for director consent, and Form NZCN/6 for registered office provider consent.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital or authorised capital threshold exists under the Companies Act 1993; a New Zealand incorporated company may lawfully issue subscriber shares with a nominal value of NZD 1.00 each. No escrow account or bank guarantee is required by the Registrar of Companies for subscription proceeds; however, the directors must account for subscription monies in the company financial records and must be able to evidence payment upon request. For a foreign-owned company, the initial share capital consideration may include non-cash assets such as software intellectual property, provided fair value is documented by board resolution.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification: annual Building Warrant of Fitness issued by Auckland Council under the Building Act 2004, together with Fire and Emergency New Zealand approval of the building fire evacuation scheme and annual evacuation drills. Structural load inspections: the office floor BWoF and biennial building inspections confirm compliance with occupancy load limits and means of egress. Workplace clearance: a current Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 policy, worker exposure assessment, first-aid and emergency lighting provisions, and an electrical safety certificate (EWOF) for all electrical equipment in the premises.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital or authorised capital threshold exists under the Companies Act 1993; a New Zealand incorporated company may lawfully issue subscriber shares with a nominal value of NZD 1.00 each. Industrial Access / EIA & Civil Defense Approval Exempted / Not Required

No industrial licensing authority applies to ISIC 6201 (computer programming and payroll SaaS operations). For premises and occupational compliance, Worksafe New Zealand and the territorial authority (Auckland Council Building Consent and Compliance) are the competent bodies. Should the company later add regulated financial services such as money transmission or payroll funds holding, the Department of Internal Affairs (AML/CFT supervisor) and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand would become relevant, but such licensing is not part of the statutory industrial formation stage.

Category: No dedicated operating or manufacturing permit is required for a cloud payroll-automation company under New Zealand law. The company is not subject to a sectoral licensing regime; it holds only general administrative clearances, namely the NZBN registration, Building Warrant of Fitness for the leased office, Fire and Emergency New Zealand evacuation scheme approval, and an Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 workplace plan.

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime; available statutory elections include: (a) Look-Through Company (LTC) election for closely held companies; (b) consolidated group election; (c) GST group registration election under the Goods and Services Act 1985; (d) hybrid mismatch election adjustments under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018; and (e) accounting basis and foreign currency elections under the Income Tax Act 2007.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (annual taxable supplies threshold; a foreign non-resident supplier is generally required to register for GST once the value of remote services or goods supplied to New Zealand consumers exceeds NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to: (a) goods physically exported by the supplier within the statutory time frames, ordinarily 28 days from the date of supply; (b) services supplied to non-resident persons who are outside New Zealand at the time of supply and where performance occurs outside New Zealand, excluding services physically rendered in New Zealand or relating to land or improvements in New Zealand; (c) certain supplies of offshore aircraft or marine craft; and (d) remote services consumed outside New Zealand as determined under the cross-border business-to-consumer rules.

  • Customs Export Declaration (form T599) lodged through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from New Zealand
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT statement evidencing receipt of foreign funds in the exporter's New Zealand bank account
  • Purchase and production documentation retained under the Goods and Services Act 1985 and the Tax Administration Act 1994

No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping in New Zealand; e-invoicing operates using the Peppol PINT BIS format through certified access points, with mandatory use for federal government agencies and voluntary adoption in the private sector; GST returns are filed electronically through myIR, while e-invoices serve as audit evidence and are not pre-cleared or pre-approved by the tax authority.

Inland Revenue conducts risk-based GST audits of zero-rating claims and requires full contemporaneous export evidence; unsubstantiated claims result in default GST assessments, shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax shortfall, official interest on unpaid tax, and potential criminal sanctions for tax evasion; retrospective clawbacks of zero-rated treatment are available for fraudulent or unsupported supplies, with the Commissioner able to reconstruct tax positions within the statutory amendment period, generally up to seven years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains fully liberalised current and capital account convertibility under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989; there are no exchange control approvals, quota limits, repatriation taxes or currency surrender obligations on the New Zealand dollar. The NZD floats freely and cross-border movements of export proceeds, dividends, interest, software royalties and payroll disbursements are unrestricted. The only material remittance hurdle is tax-driven rather than FX-driven: a Tax-Exempt Zone International Company must ensure that any profit repatriation from New Zealand-attributable income is correctly characterised under the Income Tax Act 2007, including Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) on dividends or interest (30% statutory, treasonable under Double Tax Agreements) and the Approved Issuer Levy (AIL) regime for fixed-interest securities. The treasury blueprint should therefore treat FX conversion risk, settlement timing and tax-at-source classification as the three operative constraints.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand abolished all statutory export proceeds surrender rules in the 1984 financial liberalisation programme. There is no legal timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts or SaaS subscription fees; a payroll automation vendor may retain foreign currency balances indefinitely in offshore or New Zealand multi-currency accounts, may convert at will, and is subject only to ordinary IRD taxation of recognised profits, transfer pricing documentation under Subpart GC of the Income Tax Act 2007, and AML/CFT record-keeping obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) is the central bank and prudential supervisor of all registered banks, operator of the wholesale Exchange Settlement Account System and the sole issuer of New Zealand dollars. Foreign exchange administration is not exercised through exchange controls; the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) additionally regulates derivatives issuers and financial advice, while the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) administers the Overseas Investment Act 2005 for inbound capital in sensitive land, fishing quota and significant business assets. For a non-resident-owned International Company engaging in payroll software services, the RBNZ requires adherence to prudential standards of the licensed custodian banks, and the OIO requires no approval unless the entity acquires sensitive New Zealand assets.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a secondary/supplementary rail, Low as a primary treasury anchor. New Zealand has no statutory equivalence to the UK/European EMI licence; offshore electronic money institutions (e.g., Wise, Airwallex or other foreign EMI cards) cannot take deposits or issue New Zealand bank money without a banking licence under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989. They may, however, operate via a registered New Zealand branch as financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must separately register under the AML/CFT Act 2009, supervised by the Department of Internal Affairs. The practical legal constraint is that an offshore EMI may collect inbound subscription funds and disburse low-value payroll payments, but cannot legally provide escrow attestation, statutory capital custody, commercial letter-of-credit opening, or ESAS high-value settlement, all of which require a licensed New Zealand registered bank. The treasury design should therefore place the EMI rail strictly for low-value disbursement diversification and hold the primary liquidity position at a tier-1 domestic bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For a non-resident-owned New Zealand International Company, inbound equity capital can flow freely without exchange control registration; the Companies Act 1993 requires an allotment resolution and lodgement of a return of allotment with the NZ Companies Office within 20 working days, and this Companies Office filing constitutes the evidential anchor for the bank. Recommended protocol: the remitting shareholder initiates a SWIFT MT103 wire to the beneficiary NZD account at a tier-1 New Zealand bank, stating payment purpose code such as 'Share Capital Contribution / Equity Investment', referencing the board allotment resolution number and the company's IRD number, and defining the beneficiary as the escrow or operational account flagged at onboarding. The recipient bank will apply its own reason-for-payment tagging (typically 'CAPS' or 'INVC' style codes) and will verify source of funds for amounts above a transaction threshold. Pre-wire documentation should include the executed allotment resolution, updated share register, CRS self-certification, NZ Companies Office extract showing the shareholding, and a corporate bank reference from the remitter. The Overseas Investment Office (OIO) screening is only engaged where the capital injection also acquires sensitive New Zealand land or fishing quota, which is outside the ordinary scope of a payroll SaaS operating company; an OIO clearance letter can nevertheless be pre-emptively produced to remove any delay.The payroll SaaS company will disburse through four principal clearing rails: (i) SWIFT MT103 messages for high-value cross-border vendor settlements, salary remittances for international staff, and tax payments to non-resident contractors; (ii) Commercial Letters of Credit issued via SWIFT MT700/MT707 where escrow-backed trade documentation is mandated by a foreign client or a regulated payroll partner; (iii) the Payments New Zealand BECS bulk electronic clearing system for low-value domestic ACH/direct-debit payroll files; and (iv) the RBNZ-operated Exchange Settlement Account System and ISO 20022 payment messages for urgent New Zealand dollar high-value clearing and same-day settlement. Each outbound transfer should include a SWIFT purpose code consistent with ISIC 6201 services (e.g., 'Professional fees — software services', 'Wages/salary — foreign employees', 'Dividend distribution'), dual-authorisation sign-off, file hashing for payroll batches, and a masked-beneficiary validation step to prevent payee substitution. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium as a secondary/supplementary rail, Low as a primary treasury anchor. New Zealand has no statutory equivalence to the UK/European EMI licence; offshore electronic money institutions (e.g., Wise, Airwallex or other foreign EMI cards) cannot take deposits or issue New Zealand bank money without a banking licence under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989. They may, however, operate via a registered New Zealand branch as financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must separately register under the AML/CFT Act 2009, supervised by the Department of Internal Affairs. The practical legal constraint is that an offshore EMI may collect inbound subscription funds and disburse low-value payroll payments, but cannot legally provide escrow attestation, statutory capital custody, commercial letter-of-credit opening, or ESAS high-value settlement, all of which require a licensed New Zealand registered bank. The treasury design should therefore place the EMI rail strictly for low-value disbursement diversification and hold the primary liquidity position at a tier-1 domestic bank.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

New Zealand's standard non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) rate on fully imputed dividends is 28% (matching the corporate tax rate). For unimputed dividends, NRWT is withheld at 30% (or lower if the recipient is resident in a treaty country and the treaty rate applies). Supplementary dividend and imputation credit mechanics may reduce the effective NRWT to the applicable treaty rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand's transfer pricing rules under the Income Tax Act 2007 sections GC 6–GC 14 and the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines apply. Mandatory documentation thresholds: NZD 1 million or more per category of cross-border intercompany transactions (e.g., sale of goods, services, interest, royalties) requires contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation sufficient to demonstrate arm's length pricing. Consolidated group revenue exceeding NZD 100 million with cross-border related-party transactions is subject to enhanced Master File and Local File documentation expectations aligned with the OECD's Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) Action 13 guidance, but no separate statutory annual filing of a TP return exists; documentation must be available on IRD request.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand operates a general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007. There are no formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as found in certain offshore financial centres, but the IRD actively applies substance doctrines, including the permanent establishment concept, transfer pricing adjustments, and the GAAR, to recharacterize arrangements lacking commercial substance. For non-resident owned International Companies or structures with offshore income, the IRD may challenge artificial arrangements with no substantive New Zealand economic activity, lack of management and control in New Zealand, or treaty shopping structures.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Returns

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, a company convicted of tax evasion is liable to a fine of up to NZD 50,000 for each offence, and the court may order repayment of the evaded tax plus interest and shortfall penalties. Inland Revenue shortfall penalties for evasion (section 141E) are 150% of the tax shortfall; evasion that involves intent to hide the tax liability may attract criminal prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for tax debts if they knowingly participate in evasion or are party to the company's fraudulent activities. The court may impose imprisonment of up to 5 years for individuals convicted of tax offences (including aiding, abetting, or inducing false tax returns). Directors may face civil recovery orders, management and control orders, and under the Companies Act 1993, liability for reckless trading. Cross-border travel bans may be issued under the Tax Administration Act to prevent departure from New Zealand where tax is owing.
Shell Company and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The IRD may apply the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (section BG 1) to void the tax benefit, reconstruct the arrangement on an arm's length basis, and assess additional tax plus shortfall penalties up to 100% (for abusive tax position) or 150% (for evasion). The company may also face deregistration under the Companies Act for failure to carry on business, and inclusion on the prohibited entities register.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the company's tax liabilities under the Tax Administration Act through the 'trustee' or 'director' gateway if the company's lack of substance amounts to phoenix company behaviour. Penalties include disqualification from managing companies for up to 5 years, personal civil liability for unpaid PAYE and GST remittances, and potential criminal liability for reckless or fraudulent trading.
Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing (AML/CFT)

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, failure to comply with due diligence, reporting, or record-keeping obligations attracts civil penalties up to NZD 2 million for a company for each offence, and criminal penalties of up to NZD 200,000 and/or imprisonment of up to 2 years for individuals. The Financial Markets Authority and NZ Police are the designated supervisors/enforcers.

Director personal liability: Directors of payroll companies that are reporting entities face fines up to NZD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years for knowingly facilitating money laundering or terrorist financing. Personal confiscation orders may be issued for proceeds of crime under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. Senior managers may be banned from managing a reporting entity.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Unsupported Cross-Border Charges

Statutory sanction: The IRD may make a transfer pricing adjustment under sections GC 6–GC 14, increasing assessable income by the arm's-length differential, apply a shortfall penalty of 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross lack of care, 100% for an abusive tax position, or 150% for evasion, plus use-of-money interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign tax returns or approve transfer pricing policies may be jointly and severally liable for penalties if they are knowingly a party to the false statement. Under the tax shortfall penalty regime, a director who 'turns a blind eye' to the absence of TP documentation may be treated as having gross carelessness. Individual liability for the company's tax may arise under section 34 of the Tax Administration Act for assisting with an unlawful tax position.
Withholding Tax (NRWT) Non-Compliance on Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The New Zealand payer is liable to pay the full NRWT amount to the IRD regardless of whether it was withheld. Shortfall penalties of 20% to 150% apply depending on culpability, plus late payment interest. Failure to register as a payer and file monthly NRWT returns attracts a late filing penalty of NZD 250 or NZD 500 per month and eventual prosecution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for NRWT and penalties if they are knowingly concerned in the company's failure to withhold. The Tax Administration Act permits Inland Revenue to recover unpaid tax from directors and statutory authorities where the company is unable to pay, including imposition of a statutory charge over assets, prohibition from leaving New Zealand, and in extreme cases imprisonment.
