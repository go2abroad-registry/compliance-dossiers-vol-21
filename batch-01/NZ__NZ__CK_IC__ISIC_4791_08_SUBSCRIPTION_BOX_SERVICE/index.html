Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office, an operational business unit of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE), which administers the Companies Register and the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) register; online lodgement via the Companies Register portal at companies-register.companiesoffice.govt.nz with RealMe authentication.

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (Public Act 1993 No 105), particularly ss 10-13 (application and registration of companies), ss 14-17 (constitution), ss 20-22 (name and reservation), ss 31-32 (alteration and rectification), ss 36-39 (certificates), ss 45-49 (share capital and allotment), ss 122, 151-152 (directors and New Zealand residence requirement), ss 189-194 (registered office and address for service), and ss 200-201 (minutes); supplemented by the Companies Regulations 1994 (SR 1994/329) and the Companies (Fees) Regulations 2016; the Financial Reporting Act 2013 governs accounting records.

Formation / constitutional filings

Companies Office digital filing known as 'Application for Registration of a Company' (the functional equivalent of Form 2 under the Companies Regulations 1994), accompanied by the company constitution, director/officer consent-to-act declarations, registered office and address-for-service details pursuant to section 189 of the Companies Act 1993, and a share allotment or paid-up capital statement.

Incorporation processing time

⚡ 21 business days (SOP sum)

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

New Zealand imposes no statutory minimum paid-up share capital and no escrow, capital-deposit, or collateral obligation: a company limited by shares may incorporate with a single share at a nominal amount (Companies Act 1993 s 46(1)). The application must state the number of shares to be issued and the amount paid or unpaid on them. Capital may be subscribed in cash, property, or services and may remain held offshore. Any distribution or reduction of capital is governed by the solvency test codified in ss 4 and 52-56 of the Companies Act 1993.

Funding & unlock path: Physical clearance prerequisites for the subscription-box fulfilment site include: (a) a Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) and compliance schedule under sections 100-108 of the Building Act 2004 covering fire alarms, emergency lighting, automatic doors, and sprinkler systems; (b) a Fire and Emergency New Zealand means-of-escape certification for any staffed warehouse premises under the Fire and Emergency New Zealand Act 2017; (c) registration as a person conducting a business or undertaking with WorkSafe New Zealand and compliance with the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, including the worker engagement and workplace risk-assessment duties; (d) an electrical safety certificate under the Electricity (Safety) Regulations 2010 for all operational wiring and equipment; and (e) a business-continuity and civil-defence arrangement aligned with the Emergency Management Act 2002 and the relevant CDEM Group plan, such as Auckland Emergency Management, covering earthquake, storm, and utility-failure response.

Paid-in capital rule: New Zealand imposes no statutory minimum paid-up share capital and no escrow, capital-deposit, or collateral obligation: a company limited by shares may incorporate with a single share at a nominal amount (Companies Act 1993 s 46(1)). The application must state the number of shares to be issued and the amount paid or unpaid on them. Capital may be subscribed in cash, property, or services and may remain held offshore. Any distribution or reduction of capital is governed by the solvency test codified in ss 4 and 52-56 of the Companies Act 1993.

Industrial Access / EIA & Civil Defense Approval Exempted / Not Required

No compulsory industrial licensing board applies to ISIC 4791 online retail subscription-box merchandising under New Zealand law. Contingent competent authorities include the Ministry for Primary Industries (MPI) where food, cosmetics, or biosecurity-relevant goods are packed in the box, the Environmental Protection Authority (EPA) together with the relevant regional council under the Resource Management Act 1991 for any discharge or hazardous-substance activity, WorkSafe New Zealand for occupational health and safety of the fulfilment premises, and Fire and Emergency New Zealand for means-of-escape certification.

Category: Not applicable - no operating or manufacturing licence is prescribed for a subscription-box merchandiser in New Zealand, and the governing scope data confirms requires_statutory_license equals false. Contingent authorizations that may become obligatory on product mix expansion include an MPI Food Control Plan registration under the Food Act 2014 where food items are included, an EPA hazardous-substance location approval under the Hazardous Substances Regulations 2017 where batteries, aerosols, or corrosive liquids are warehoused, and a remote-sale liquor licence under the Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act 2012 should alcohol ever be distributed.

Competent tax authority

The statutory digital portal is 'myIR' (www.ird.govt.nz/myir), supported by the RealMe government identity verification service. Company incorporation and the New Zealand Business Number ('NZBN') are administered by the New Zealand Companies Office (www.companiesoffice.govt.nz), with the company's IRD number automatically assigned by Inland Revenue upon Companies Office registration.

E-government filing portal

Stage A — Nexus assessment: a non-resident entity with no New Zealand-sourced income and no fixed establishment in New Zealand is not obliged to obtain a New Zealand IRD number. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' entity classification election. The relevant statutory elections are: (i) Look-Through Company (LTC) election (Income Tax Act 2007, ss 147–151; Form IR 448) — a close company with five or fewer look-through owners may elect tax transparency so that profits and losses are taxed at the shareholder level and no entity-level income tax is imposed; (ii) GST group registration election (Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, s 55) — related companies may elect to be treated as a single GST-registered person; and (iii) consolidated group election (Income Tax Act 2007, Part IC) — a 100-percent New Zealand-owned group may elect to file as a consolidated tax group. Hybrid mismatch and branch mismatch rules (subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007, implementing the OECD BEPS Action 2 outcomes) apply compulsorily and are not subject to taxpayer election; cross-border financing arrangements require disclosure to the Commissioner under the financing statement rules (FA 23). The Cook Islands 'International Company' exemption is a separate offshore-law status and is not an election available under New Zealand domestic income tax law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period (Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, s 51). Registration is compulsory once the 12-month turnover reaches or exceeds NZD 60,000, and voluntary registration is available where the threshold is expected to be exceeded within the next 12 months. The same NZD 60,000 threshold applies to offshore suppliers of remote/digital services and to offshore suppliers of low-value imported goods (goods valued at NZD 1,000 or less supplied to New Zealand consumers) for GST collection purposes.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under s 11(1)(a) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, a supply of goods is zero-rated where: (i) the supplier is GST-registered; (ii) the supply is made on a condition that the goods are exported from New Zealand; and (iii) the goods are in fact exported within 60 days of the time of supply, or within such longer period as the Commissioner approves. Services, including offshore services performed for a non-resident recipient outside New Zealand, are zero-rated under s 11A where the services are supplied to a non-resident and the benefit of the services is received outside New Zealand; remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers are instead subject to GST at 15%. For the subscription-box model, physical boxes exported from New Zealand to customers outside New Zealand qualify for zero-rating only if the 60-day export condition and supporting export evidence are satisfied; physical boxes shipped into New Zealand from offshore are not zero-rated, and GST at 15% applies as low-value imported goods (value not exceeding NZD 1,000) collected at the point of sale by the offshore supplier once registration is effective.

  • Customs Export Declaration (New Zealand Customs Service export entry lodged through the Trade Single Window)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing the physical departure of the goods from New Zealand within 60 days of the time of supply
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank foreign-exchange credit advice) evidencing the receipt of export sale proceeds

New Zealand has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, tax-authority API e-invoicing gateway, or digital VAT stamping requirement (no equivalent of the Italian SDI clearance, Mexican CFD certification, or Brazilian NF-e authorization). E-invoicing operates on the voluntary, interoperable Peppol network (PEPPOL BIS 3.0), administered in New Zealand by the NZ Peppol Authority; since 31 March 2022 all New Zealand government agencies are required to accept e-invoices. Tax invoices, including electronic invoices, must satisfy the content requirements of s 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 (supplier name and GST registration number, recipient name, date of issuance, description of the supply, amount of consideration, and GST payable), and supporting records must be retained for seven years.

New Zealand Inland Revenue subjects export zero-rating claims to documentary audit. If goods are not exported within the 60-day statutory window, or if the goods are diverted to domestic use or returned to New Zealand, the supply is taxable at 15%, and any GST refund previously claimed is retrospectively clawed back with use-of-money interest under Part 7 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Shortfall penalties under s 141 TAA 1994 are imposed as a percentage of the resulting tax shortfall: 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for an unacceptable tax position, 75% for an abusive tax position, and 100% for evasion. Knowingly creating or using false export documentation constitutes a criminal offence under s 143A TAA 1994 (fines up to NZD 50,000 for a first offence and NZD 100,000 for a subsequent offence) and, under s 143B, tax evasion is punishable by imprisonment for up to five years. Reassessment and clawback are generally limited to four years under s 108(1) TAA 1994, but the Commissioner may reassess up to seven years where a return was fraudulent, wilfully misleading, or contained a gross valuation error (s 108(2)).

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar is fully convertible under a free-floating exchange rate regime. New Zealand does not maintain exchange controls on current account or capital account transactions. Profit remittance, dividends, interest, royalties, and repayment of shareholder loans may be transferred abroad without prior exchange-control approval. The practical hurdles are limited to standard AML/CFT verification of source of funds, withholding tax obligations imposed by the Income Tax Act 2007, and, in rare cases, Overseas Investment Act screening for substantial land or sensitive asset acquisitions. Capital account convertibility is open for equity injection and internal treasury transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement in New Zealand. Export receipts may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency or converted freely to NZD. The mandatory conversion ratio is zero percent. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not impose timelines, repatriation obligations, or directed conversion thresholds for export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand - Te Pūtea Matua

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium feasibility. Offshore digital EMI platforms such as Wise, Airwallex, and Payoneer may provide multi-currency collection and disbursement accounts that are operationally convenient for subscription-box receivables and supplier payments. However, in New Zealand they are generally not licensed as registered banks and are not covered by the New Zealand Deposit Compensation Scheme. If they accept New Zealand customers or maintain a New Zealand nexus, they must comply with the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and AML/CFT obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. They are viable for treasury disbursement optimization but not recommended as the primary capital-escrow banking rail.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity capital should be routed through the permitted SWIFT MT103 channel from the foreign shareholder to the New Zealand operational bank account. The payment message must include a clear remittance instruction such as 'Equity injection — subscription box service venture' and the corresponding balance-of-payments classification 'Direct Investment: Equity Capital'. The receiving bank will apply standard customer due diligence and source-of-funds verification, but there is no Reserve Bank of New Zealand approval requirement for equity conversion to NZD. If the shareholder capital is structured as a shareholder loan, the parties must document the loan agreement and consider New Zealand non-resident interest withholding tax rules.Outbound trade disbursements for subscription box inventory, freight, logistics, and international service providers should be cleared through SWIFT MT103 payments maintained as direct transfers from New Zealand foreign-currency accounts or NZD accounts converted under the bank's foreign exchange service. For high-value or documentary transactions, commercial letters of credit and documentary collections are available but are generally unnecessary for recurring low-value subscription-box purchases. Domestic NZD legs may clear through the Bulk Electronic Clearing System and the Exchange Settlement Account System where real-time settlement is required. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends: 30% statutory rate; reduced to 15% under the New Zealand–China DTA (applicable to a Chinese IC parent); further reduced to 5% for an IC owning at least 25% of a New Zealand subsidiary (subject to Limitation on Benefits, if any, under the relevant treaty). Supplementary dividend regime available for fully imputed dividends paid to non-residents: a supplementary dividend of 15/85 of the cash dividend up to the NRWT rate reduction; where the IC is the shareholder and holds at least 10% of voting power, the supplementary dividend mechanism may apply in conjunction with an AIL-bearing company election.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Filing due 12 months after the company's balance date (e.g., 31 March balance date → due 31 March of following year); payment of terminal tax due on the 4th, 6th, or 7th instalment dates depending on balance date and provisional tax regime; non-resident companies without a fixed establishment are generally required make provisional tax payments on the 6th and 2nd months of the income year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand transfer pricing documentation requirements apply where the taxpayer (including a non-resident IC) has related-party transactions of any amount that are 'significant' or where it transacts with associated persons in low-tax jurisdictions; explicit Local File threshold: New Zealand revenue from cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZD 10 million in the income year; Master File required where group consolidated revenue exceeds NZD 500 million and cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZD 10 million; Country-by-country (CbC) report mandatory for groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million (NZ equivalent NZD 1.5 billion). Non-resident IC operating in New Zealand through a dependent agent PE or a fixed base should prepare Local File if the NZ revenue threshold is met.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR); however, anti-avoidance rules under the Income Tax Act 2007 (ss BG 1, GA 1, and the rewritten s GA 2) and the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) imply substantive economic purpose. A non-resident IC must not structure as a mere shell; the New Zealand Inland Revenue will pierce through a 'letterbox' company under the appointment of agent or nominee rules (s YD 4(17B)) and may treat the IC as having a fixed establishment in New Zealand if it has a dependent agent habitually exercising authority to conclude contracts. Also, the 'apportionment of business profits to PE' under the OECD PE provision requires functional and factual analysis; the IC must maintain substance (meeting minutes, bank accounts, decision-making in its jurisdiction) to avoid GAAR challenges. New Zealand's new permanent establishment source rule (effective 1 July 2018 for contracting with NZ customers) specifically targets offshore e-commerce; an IC selling subscription boxes to New Zealand consumers must ensure that if it uses a local warehouse or logistics provider, no dependent agent PE is triggered; otherwise profits attributable to New Zealand are taxed at 28%.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal penalty for tax evasion: a fine of up to NZD 50,000 for individuals or NZD 100,000 for a company on each count, plus evasion penalty of 150% of the tax shortfall (shortfall penalty for evasion or an abusive tax position is 100% of the tax shortfall; the maximum criminal fine is NZD 100,000 for a company for each offence); a civil shortfall penalty of 150% of unpaid tax and late payment penalties with UOMI accruing; imprisonment for individuals up to 5 years (s 143A).

Director personal liability: Directors of the IC may be jointly liable under s 143A if they directed or acquiesced in the evasion; Inland Revenue can seek a civil pecuniary penalty order under s 145A. Directors may also be disqualified from managing companies under the Companies Act 1993 s 382. Travel bans may be imposed under the Tax Administration Act for non-payment of tax liabilities when the Commissioner considers leaving New Zealand with unpaid tax; imprisonment up to 5 years applies to individuals, and directors of a corporate IC may be personally held for tax shortfalls if they signed the return knowing it was false. Civil recovery may extend to personal assets of directors who knowingly put money beyond Inland Revenue's reach.
Shell Company / Facade / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: GAAR under s BG 1 and s GA 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 applies to void the tax advantage; recharacterization of the IC as a New Zealand resident or having a PE in New Zealand; 100% shortfall penalty for taking an 'abusive tax position' (s 141B); reassessment of all income at 28% plus UOMI; potential 150% shortfall penalty for evasion if dishonest intent is found; no ability for the IC to claim treaty relief under the DTA because the main purpose test or limitation-of-benefits clause fails (if applicable).

Director personal liability: Directors of the IC can be held personally liable for aiding and abetting a sham arrangement; corporate veil piercing under s 188 of the Companies Act 1993 where the company is used to evade an existing obligation; travel bans under s 157 of the Tax Administration Act (Commissioner may require security or prevent departure); directors can be ordered to pay the entire tax shortfall out of personal funds if they signed false documents; criminal liability under s 143B applies to directors acting in bad faith.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Unsupported TP Position

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under s GC 1–GC 14 of the Income Tax Act 2007; imposition of a 40% shortfall penalty for 'substantial under-reporting' (s 141A) or 100% for 'abusive tax position'; assessment of additional tax and UOMI; for cross-border mismatch, the CbC reporting notification penalties: NZD 5,000 per offence under subpart 79 of the Tax Administration Act for failing to provide documentation; the Commissioner can disregard the arrangement and treat the IC as having a permanent establishment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign transfer pricing documentation that is false may be liable as principals under s 143B (evasion) leading to imprisonment up to 5 years; they may also be personally liable for the shortfall under s 145A if they actively participated in the tax shortfall; travel ban and seizure of property at the taxpayer's risk; under s 158 of the Tax Administration Act, directors who knowingly assist in a tax shortfall are jointly and severally liable for the civil penalty.
AML/CFT and Money Laundering (Structured Payment Rails)

Statutory sanction: Corporate penalties under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act): up to NZD 2 million for non-compliance per section by a body corporate (s 84); for serious non-compliance, additional civil penalties up to NZD 5 million for a company; criminal liability if a director knowingly provides false information to the FIU or obstructs a review; the High Court may order a pecuniary penalty up to triple the value of any transaction laundered (s 90); the Companies Office may prosecute for failure to maintain a register of ultimate beneficial ownership under the Companies Amendment Act 2014–2017 (s 96B Companies Act 1993) with default fines of NZD 10,000 per day.

Director personal liability: Directors face criminal liability under the AML/CFT Act s 86: up to 2 years' imprisonment and a fine of up to NZD 300,000 for knowingly participating in money laundering; civil penalty orders can be made directly against directors (s 90); travel bans may be imposed under the Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters Act if extradition proceedings arise; permanent director disqualification under s 383 of the Companies Act 1993 for fraud-related offences; the veil is pierced where the company is a mere façade for the director's money-laundering activity.
Bogus DTA Relief Claim (Improper Treaty Benefit)

Statutory sanction: Full 30% NRWT (or 15% royalty withholding) re-imposed; a 40% shortfall penalty for 'lack of reasonable care' or a 100% penalty for 'abusive tax position' if the taxpayer knew the claim was doubtful; Inland Revenue can charge interest on late payment and can prosecute under s 143B (penalties up to NZD 100,000 for a company and 5 years' imprisonment for individuals); the denial certificate (IR303) may be revoked retrospectively, making the taxpayer liable.

Director personal liability: Directors who personally certified the benefit ownership without due diligence are accessory to the false declaration; can be jointly liable for the unpaid tax and penalties under s 145A; travel bans for leaving New Zealand with tax unpaid; disqualification as a director under s 383 for making false statutory declarations (maximum 5 years' imprisonment or NZD 200,000 fine); the Companies Office may liquidate the company on the Commissioner's application, and directors may then be personally liable for debts of the company if they have traded with intent to defraud (s 301 of the Companies Act 1993).
