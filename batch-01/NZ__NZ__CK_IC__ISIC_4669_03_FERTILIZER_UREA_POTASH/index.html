Competent corporate registry

Registrar of Companies — New Zealand Companies Office, a business unit of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE); official online portal: New Zealand Companies Register (companies-register.companiesoffice.govt.nz).

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (New Zealand), as amended — ss 10-17 (incorporation and certificate of incorporation), ss 18-22 (company names and restrictions), ss 26-28 (constitution), ss 33-39 (shares, no par value, and allotment returns), ss 128-134 (board authority), ss 152-153 (directors and consent to act), ss 187-194 (registered office and address for service), ss 199-205 (annual return and financial reporting), and Part 17 (removal and restoration). Subsidiary legislation: Companies (Fees) Regulations 2016 and Companies (Forms) Regulations 1994. The 'International Company' designation is a commercial label applied to a non-resident-oriented private company limited by shares; the Companies Act 1993 is the sole incorporation statute. Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department of New Zealand (Te Tari Tauhake), administered under the Tax Administration Act 1994; online portal: Inland Revenue Secure Online Services (myIR), https://www.ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity deriving New Zealand-source income or making taxable supplies in New Zealand must obtain an IRD number. Corporate entities incorporated in New Zealand are automatically issued an IRD number upon registration with the New Zealand Companies Office. A non-resident International Company not carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand is generally not required to register for New Zealand income tax or GST. If registration is required, the foreign entity must apply to Inland Revenue using the relevant registration form, supported by its certificate of incorporation, constitution, directors' particulars, and evidence of its non-resident status; a New Zealand tax agent may act as agent for this purpose. For GST, a non-resident supplier carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand or supplying remote services to New Zealand consumers must register if total taxable supplies in any 12-month period exceed or are likely to exceed NZD 60,000 (GST Act 1985, sections 51 and 54). Registration is effected through myIR or Form GST 101. The IRD number serves as the single taxpayer identifier for income tax, GST, PAYE, and other taxes administered by Inland Revenue.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box election. Entity tax classification is determined by legal form under the Income Tax Act 2007 and the Companies Act 1993. A foreign company that is not resident in New Zealand (i.e., incorporated offshore, with its centre of administrative management or governance outside New Zealand, and not carrying on business through a New Zealand fixed establishment) is subject to New Zealand income tax only on New Zealand-sourced income. A company may elect transitional residency in limited personal cases, but this is not generally available to corporations. Under Subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007 (hybrid and branch mismatch rules), certain elections may be made in relation to hybrid financial instruments and hybrid entities, including dual-inclusion income elections, to neutralize deduction/non-inclusion mismatches. For GST purposes, a non-resident supplier may elect to register for GST from a specified date, may elect to apply the reverse-charge rules for remote services in limited circumstances, and may apply to form a GST group under section 55 of the GST Act 1985 where entities satisfy the 100% ownership or common control test.

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under section 11 of the GST Act 1985 for supplies of goods that are exported from New Zealand by the supplier or by a third party on behalf of the supplier, provided the supplier holds the required documentary evidence at the time the supply is returned in a GST return. For a supply of prilled urea and potassium chloride where the International Company acts as offshore principal and title passes outside New Zealand (goods never entering New Zealand commerce), the supply is outside the scope of New Zealand GST and is not required to be returned; zero-rating applies only where goods physically leave New Zealand. Services supplied to a non-resident person who is outside New Zealand at the time of performance may be zero-rated under section 11A, provided the services are not supplied in connection with land in New Zealand or goods in New Zealand, and the supplier holds evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient. An exporter must obtain and hold export documentation contemporaneously; failure to do so invalidates the zero-rating claim and renders the supply taxable at 15% GST.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CUSDEC) / Export Entry containing the New Zealand Customs unique consignment reference and export approval
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill showing the New Zealand supplier as shipper/exporter, the overseas consignee, destination port, and vessel/flight details
  • Inward Foreign Currency Remittance / Bank Credit Advice or letter of credit settlement record evidencing actual payment for the exported goods
  • Commercial invoice and GST tax invoice (zero-rated) referencing the export sale
  • Packing list, certificate of origin, and other shipping documents consistent with the customs declaration

New Zealand does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping for invoices. GST administration is return-based through Inland Revenue's myIR portal. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and is facilitated through the Peppol network, adopted by the New Zealand Government as the national standard for business-to-business e-invoicing. A GST-registered supplier must issue tax invoices in a form that meets the GST Act 1985 record-keeping requirements; no pre-clearance or revenue authority approval is required. All electronic records must be capable of being reproduced in English or Maori and retained for seven years. Certain digital platforms are required to account for GST on remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers under the 'platform economy' rules, but this does not create an e-invoicing clearance obligation for the underlying supplier.

Claims for zero-rating are subject to close audit scrutiny by Inland Revenue. Where documentary evidence is incomplete or not held at the time of return, output GST at 15% may be assessed with shortfall penalties under section 141 of the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for taking an unacceptable tax position, and 100% for evasion. Fraudulent GST refund or export zero-rating claims can result in criminal prosecution, a maximum fine of NZD 50,000, and/or imprisonment for up to five years. The Commissioner may amend assessments, reassess, and recover over-credited input tax or refunds within the time bar, generally four years from the end of the assessment period, extended to eight years in cases of fraud or evasion. Use-of-money interest applies to unpaid or overpaid tax from the due date. Export records must be retained for at least seven years; failure to produce export documentation on demand can lead to reversal of zero-rating, assessment of GST output tax, loss of refunds, and penalty exposure.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully convertible currency regime for both current and capital accounts under Section 7 of the Exchange Control Amendment Act 1961 (as effectively suspended by liberalization policy). There are no residual approval requirements for: (a) trade-related current-account payments, (b) dividend and profit remittances, (c) foreign direct investment inflows, or (d) overseas portfolio investment outflows. Legal authorities do not impose a convertibility ceiling for bona fide International Company (IC) entities. Practical hurdles are limited to Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, especially where the IC is tax-exempt and supplier invoices involve fertilizer commodities. Profit remittance is tax-unencumbered at source for non-resident shareholders (zero withholding on exempt IC distributions, but standard dividend withholding may arise in the shareholder's jurisdiction); bank credit departments will still demand a certified solvency and source-of-funds trail.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand has no statutory export proceeds surrender rule. No timeline or mandatory conversion ratio is imposed on urea/potash export receipts; the IC may retain 100% of export proceeds in foreign currency (USD, AUD, EUR) in offshore or onshore Foreign Currency Accounts (FCAs). Under the Financial Reporting Act 2013 and tax transparency obligations, export revenues must be fully reflected in financial statements and be traceable to customs declarations (NZ Customs Service Bill of Entry) and underlying warehouse/transshipment contracts, but no exchange-surrender certification is required. Due diligence practice for trade finance requires a complete bill-of-lading and currency-receipt reconciliation if the IC draws invoice discounting facilities.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), acting in coordination with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for market conduct; no separate foreign exchange administrative agency exists. New Zealand has no exchange-control statute; the RBNZ's regulatory remit covers the stability of the payment/clearing system and banking licensing, not conversion approvals.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory rate of non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends paid by a New Zealand company to a non-resident recipient is 30% under the Income Tax Act 2007, unless reduced by a DTA. For a fully imputed dividend, the NRWT rate may be reduced to 15% under NRWT rules where the dividend is fully imputed and the recipient is a non-resident company with at least 10% voting interest; supplementary dividend mechanisms may apply for certain treaty partners. Under the New Zealand–China DTA (applicable due to the composite key context), the withholding rate on dividends is generally 15% for portfolio shareholdings and 5% for beneficial owners being companies holding at least 10% of the voting power, provided the recipient is a resident of China and meets the limitation of benefits and beneficial ownership conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The IR4 return must be filed by the 7th day of the second month following the end of the company's balance date (e.g., for a 31 March balance date, the filing deadline is 31 May; for a 30 June balance date, the deadline is 7 August). Terminal tax (final residual tax) is due by the same date: 7th day of the second month after balance date, or 7 February for standard 31 March balance date taxpayers using the extended terminal tax date, subject to qualifying criteria.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand's transfer pricing documentation thresholds are set as a de minimis exception under s GC 12 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and the Transfer Pricing Guidelines issued by Inland Revenue: (i) for taxpayers with total related-party cross-border transactions of NZD 1 million or less, no contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is required if the transactions are not 'transfer pricing arrangements' (for interest/loan arrangements with associated non-residents, the threshold is NZD 1 million per group); (ii) for larger taxpayers, a 'transfer pricing arrangement' includes cross-border related-party transactions above the NZD 1 million threshold; (iii) the Local File and Master File documentation requirements follow OECD TP Documentation Guidelines adopted under Inland Revenue's Operational Statement OS 21/03; (iv) the transaction threshold for mandatory contemporaneous documentation is NZD 10 million for related-party cross-border transactions for a taxpayer in a corporate group with turnover above NZD 100 million, unless a specific exemption under the Administrative Guidelines applies; (v) Inland Revenue requires 'contemporaneous' documentation to be prepared by the filing date of the IR4 return (generally the 7th of the second month after balance date), and the Commissioner may allocate a burden of proof on the taxpayer if documentation is not prepared; (vi) non-compliance can result in a 100% shortfall penalty for substantial underpayment attributable to transfer pricing adjustments, plus interest.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply the OECD's Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as implemented by the EU's Code of Conduct Group or the UAE-style ESR regime. Instead, New Zealand adopts: (i) the statutory anti-avoidance rules under s BG 1 and s GA 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007; (ii) the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s BG 1, which applies when a tax avoidance arrangement has more than merely incidental tax avoidance purpose or effect; (iii) transfer pricing-specific substance rules in s GC 13 (reconstruction of non-arms-length arrangements), requiring the Commissioner to make a transfer pricing adjustment if the actual arrangement is not commercially rational and the effect is to reduce taxable income; (iv) the 'business test' and 'purpose test' under the Income Tax Act 2007, and the requirement that an arrangement have economic substance to be respected for tax purposes; (v) the anti-avoidance rules for land transactions and the controlled foreign company (CFC) rules under the CFC regime, which may attribute income from a CFC with passive income where no active business substance exists; (vi) for a non-resident wholesale fertilizer company, the key substance doctrine is the 'permanent establishment' analysis under Article 5 of the applicable DTA; a dependent agent or a fixed place of business in New Zealand (e.g., a leasehold warehouse, a logistics office, consignment stock facility, or an employee with authority to conclude contracts) may create a PE and expose the non-resident to New Zealand corporate income tax on attributable profits; (vii) Inland Revenue's 'Economic Substance Guidelines' for treaty eligibility require that a company claiming treaty benefits have real economic presence, employees performing core functions, and assumption of risk/rewards, otherwise a treaty benefits claim may be denied under the principal purpose test (PPT) if the applicable DTA includes the OECD Multilateral Instrument (MLI) provision; (viii) New Zealand has adopted the MLI, and most NZ DTAs are subject to the PPT, requiring the non-resident to demonstrate a bona fide commercial purpose for the structure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilful Misstatement (Tax Administration Act 1994)

Statutory sanction: Under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, evasion or wilful act with intent to evade tax is a criminal offence punishable by imprisonment for a term not exceeding 5 years and/or a fine of up to NZD 50,000; in addition, the taxpayer is liable for tax shortfall penalties under sections 141E and 141H of up to 150% of the evaded tax, plus use-of-money interest; a further civil penalty of up to 150% applies for abusive tax positions; there is no cap on the tax payable on the evaded amount. For failure to file an IR4 return, the maximum fine on prosecution is NZD 25,000 and a daily fine of NZD 100 for continued failure.

Director personal liability: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, directors (including de facto and shadow directors) of a company that fails to pay PAYE, GST, or other tax amounts may be personally liable for civil penalties under the director penalty notice regime for unremitted withholding obligations (but not generally for corporate income tax unless the company is acting as a mere sham). For criminal tax evasion, directors and officers can be prosecuted as parties under section 66 Crimes Act 1961 and section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, resulting in imprisonment up to 5 years; directors who orchestrate a fraudulent tax evasion scheme involving shell companies, nominee shareholders, and backdated contracts risk personal criminal conviction, orders to pay restitution, freezing orders, and pecuniary penalty orders under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 (up to the value of the illegal benefit, potentially NZD 20 million or more for large fertilizer commodity trades), and travel bans pending trial.
Transfer Pricing Adjustment / Non-Arms-Length Pricing

Statutory sanction: Under section GC 13 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and Subpart GC, the Commissioner may reconstruct the arrangement and increase taxable income by the arm's length adjustment; a transfer pricing shortfall penalty under section 141A and 141EB applies where the taxpayer takes an unacceptable tax position (below the 'tax position threshold'), equal to 20% of the resulting unpaid tax; if the understatement exceeds NZD 20,000 and the position lacks a reasonable basis, the penalty increases to 100% (section 141B); for arrangements with the dominant purpose of tax avoidance, the penalty is up to 150%; interest compounds monthly at the underpayment UOMI rate; criminal prosecution is possible for serious transfer pricing misstatements.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign an IR4 return containing a false or misleading declaration relating to transfer pricing face civil penalties for taking an 'unacceptable tax position' (up to 100% of the shortfall plus UOMI); criminal liability under section 143A for knowingly providing false information to the Commissioner is a strict liability offence punishable by a fine up to NZD 25,000 for each offence, and section 143B criminal liability for evasion carries imprisonment up to 5 years; under sections 141EA and 141EB, directors can be personally liable where the company failed to maintain acceptable tax documentation and the director is the 'controlling officer' of the tax function.
Company Formation / Shell Company and Anti-Money Laundering (AML)

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act), reporting entities (including financial institutions and certain non-financial businesses) must conduct CDD; failure to comply can attract civil penalties up to NZD 2 million for an individual and NZD 4 million for a company, or up to 30% of total annual turnover (if higher); criminal penalties for wilful non-compliance include up to 2 years imprisonment and fines up to NZD 300,000 (individuals) or NZD 3 million (companies); under the Companies Act 1993 (and its Beneficial Ownership Register), a false declaration could be an offence punishable by fine up to NZD 50,000; in respect of shell companies, the New Zealand Inland Revenue may invoke the 'sham' doctrine (the arrangement is a façade) and ignore the separate corporate personality, taxing the economic owner directly; the Cook Islands International Companies Act (applicable to CK_IC) imposes an annual license fee and requires a registered agent; using it from New Zealand without registration under the Financial Service Providers (Pre-Implementation Adjustments) Act 2008 for providing financial services can be a criminal offence.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company engaged in money laundering face personal criminal liability under sections 100-103 of the AML/CFT Act for knowingly facilitating money laundering: imprisonment up to 7 years for the underlying money laundering offence under the Crimes Act 1961 (sections 243-246) and up to 5 years for financing of terrorism; on conviction, directors are also subject to pecuniary penalty orders under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 equal to the value of the laundered proceeds; Inland Revenue may issue a 'personal liability order' against directors for tax debts where the shell structure is deemed a sham; travel bans can be imposed under the Bail Act 2000 or as a condition of the Financial Markets Authority / Criminal Proceedings; company directors who fail to keep proper accounting records to explain fertilizer commodity transactions face up to 3 years imprisonment under section 300 of the Companies Act 1993 (dishonest intent) or 1 year if ordinary intent.
Permanent Establishment (PE) Avoidance / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Under Article 5 and Article 7 of the applicable DTA (or the OECD Model), if a PE is found, the non-resident company is taxable on the profits attributable to the PE, and may be the subject of a transfer pricing allocation; under the Income Tax Act 2007 ss YD 4 and YD 4B, the PE is taxable on profits attributable under the authorised OECD approach; penalties include shortfall penalty up to 150% for deliberately failing to file returns, and prosecution for tax evasion; with treaty shopping, the tax authority may apply the Principal Purpose Test (under the MLI and NZ-China DTA as applicable) and deny treaty benefits, resulting in full 30% NRWT on dividends, 15% NRWT on interest/royalties, and no exemption for business profits; in severe cases, a 'scheme for a tax benefit' may trigger the general anti-avoidance rule and void the tax advantages.

Director personal liability: Directors and trust officers who knowingly structure the New Zealand management of a non-resident company to conceal a PE can be prosecuted as parties to tax evasion under s 143B of the Tax Administration Act (imprisonment up to 5 years); under s 149 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, directors of a defunct company may be personally liable for the company's unpaid taxes if the company is liquidated and the director engaged in phoenix activity; directors will face civil liability for breach of fiduciary duty to the company; professional advisers can be prosecuted under s 143A for knowingly assisting in the preparation of false tax documents, carrying a fine up to NZD 25,000, and under the Tax Administration Act s 143B for aiding and abetting evasion, risking imprisonment up to 5 years.
Imputation & Dividend Repatriation Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under sections RE 13 and RF 8 of the Income Tax Act 2007, a payer of dividends (or the non-resident recipient) that fails to withhold NRWT is liable to pay the tax itself, and penalties apply: late payment penalties of up to 5% per month; a shortfall penalty of up to 150% for an abusive tax position; interest on unpaid amounts; failure to maintain an imputation credit account can trigger a 'deemed dividend' and a penalty of $10,000/$20,000 per failure under the ICA rules; paying out imputed dividends when the ICA is in deficit creates a debit and an additional tax of 28% on the deficit under the ICA regime.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve a dividend distribution knowing that the company has no available distributable profits (under the Companies Act 1993 s 52) are personally liable to repay the dividend amount to the company's creditors; for NRWT evasion, directors who knowingly fail to withhold can be sued by the Commissioner as 'responsible persons'; criminal prosecution for fraud or misrepresentation can lead to imprisonment for up to 7 years under the Crimes Act 1961 (obtaining by deception or causing loss by deception), plus travel-ban and asset-freezing orders in the context of criminal proceedings.
