Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue, IRD) — official portal 'myIR' administered at https://www.ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned New Zealand limited company must first be incorporated with the New Zealand Companies Office. Following incorporation, the company applies to the Inland Revenue Department for an IRD number (tax file number) using form IR596 or via the myIR portal. A non-resident director or shareholder must provide certified identity documentation, evidence of the registered office in New Zealand, and details of the ultimate beneficial owner. Where the company expects taxable supplies to exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period, simultaneous registration for Goods and Services Tax (GST) is mandatory using form GST101A or the myIR online registration service. The IRD issues a single IRD number for all tax types (income tax, GST, PAYE) and the GST registration is reflected as a separate tax account. Foreign companies not carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand but seeking to claim GST input tax credits on imports may still apply for registration under the special registration provisions of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime. The default classification of a New Zealand limited company (Ltd) is a taxable company subject to the New Zealand corporate tax rate of 28%. Eligible close-held companies (five or fewer natural-person shareholders and no corporate shareholders) may irrevocably elect look-through company (LTC) status under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, whereby income, deductions, and tax credits flow through to shareholders; the LTC election is made in the first income year of eligibility and requires all shareholders to be New Zealand tax residents. A grandfathered qualifying company (QC) election exists only for companies registered before 30 March 2011. For cross-border hybrid mismatch purposes, the OECD hybrid mismatch rules in sections GB 13–GB 15 and subpart FN of the Income Tax Act 2007 may recharacterise payments, entities, or branches where a mismatched tax outcome arises, effectively overriding domestic classification for specific payments.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There are no restrictions, surrender requirements, or prior exchange-control approvals for ordinary trade payments, dividend remittances, intercompany loans, or profit repatriation. Exporters and investors may freely hold foreign currency balances, hedge in authorized FX instruments, and settle trades in any convertible currency. The sole practical limitation is the OIO screening threshold for inward investment in 'significant business assets' (ordinarily NZ$100 million or more for non-consent-fund investors) and for investments in sensitive land or fishing quota; a standard trading company engaged in dental clear aligner manufacturing and export below these thresholds faces no convertibility or capital inflow barrier.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. New Zealand imposes no statutory timeframe for repatriation of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio into NZD. Export receipts for dental clear aligner sheets and related orthodontic goods may be retained offshore in a foreign currency, credited to a registered bank's foreign currency account in New Zealand, or converted to NZD at the exporter's complete discretion. The only compliance obligations are ordinary tax reporting (IRD, goods and services tax if registered, and transfer pricing documentation where applicable) and the AML/CFT duty to evidence the commercial provenance of export collections through invoices, transport documents, and customs declarations.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), acting as New Zealand's central bank and prudential regulator, with supplementary liquidity and currency oversight powers. Inward foreign investment capital controls are co-administered by the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 for investments meeting the significant business assets or sensitive land thresholds.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium as a supplementary collection or disbursement channel only. Offshore digital money institutions and electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise Business, Airwallex, and OFX can provide New Zealand-dollar virtual account numbers through partnerships with RBNZ-registered banks, enabling faster inbound payments from overseas aligner distributors and convenient supplier payouts for low-risk ISIC 3250 trade flows. However, no EMI may act as the primary custodian of customer deposits or provide capital escrow functions without holding a New Zealand banking licence or non-bank deposit taker registration under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021. For a non-resident-owned NZ trading company, the legally robust route is a full corporate account at a registered commercial bank, using the EMI solely for operational convenience, while maintaining an underlying bank master account for statutory audit, FX hedging, and capital verification.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory dividend withholding tax: non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at 30% on gross dividends paid to non-resident shareholders, reduced to 15% under the New Zealand-Australia double tax agreement and to 15% under most other comprehensive DTAs (including the NZ-US DTA, NZ-UK DTA, and NZ-Singapore DTA). The imputation credit portion of a fully imputed dividend is exempt from NRWT, provided the dividend is paid from a NZ-resident company's imputation credit account.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally the due date is the 7th day of the second month following balance date (e.g., for a 31 March balance date, due by 7 July). Inland Revenue may extend the filing deadline to 31 March of the following income year where the company has a tax agent; payment deadlines remain based on the terminal tax date. Provisional tax is paid in three instalments during the income year using a standard uplift or estimation method; final terminal tax is due by 7 April following the balance date for non-tax-agent filers or 7 February / 7 April per tax agent extension arrangements.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand has no statutory monetary thresholds that trigger mandatory Local File or Master File preparation; however, a non-resident-owned company with related-party cross-border transactions must prepare and retain transfer pricing documentation under section GC 13 and section 78D of the Tax Administration Act 1994 where the transactions exceed NZD 10 million in aggregate annual value (for financing arrangements, NZD 10 million) or where the arrangement is considered 'large' (annual value greater than NZD 10 million for goods/services and financial transactions). The Inland Revenue Transfer Pricing Guidelines (TPG) and OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines apply. For a typical mid-sized dental aligner sheet manufacturer with related-party sales of finished sheets or raw polyethylene/polyurethane pellets, documentation must include a functional and risk analysis, an economic analysis using an approved method, and supporting comparables; the absence of statutory thresholds does not exempt a taxpayer from the requirement to keep sufficient records to establish arm's length pricing.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a Gulf-Cooperation-Council-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime. Instead, the economic substance doctrine is embedded in the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and in the transfer pricing adjustments under section GC 1 to GC 14. A company that is nominally a New Zealand resident but lacks real commercial activity, physical premises, employees, management decisions, or operational substance in New Zealand may be disregarded for tax purposes if the arrangements lack commercial reality. The 'land' and 'shell company' anti-avoidance provisions, together with the OECD BEPS 2018 reforms (interest limitation rules under s FE 1, hybrid mismatch rules, and the 'non-resident's New Zealand sourced income attributable to a fixed establishment' rules), reinforce the requirement that the New Zealand entity demonstrate substantive decision-making, operational capability, and risk-bearing in respect of its dental aligner manufacturing functions. Inland Revenue may recharacterise transactions to reflect economic substance and impose transfer pricing penalties up to 10% of the shortfall in tax where the taxpayer fails to take reasonable care.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under section 143A and section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994; maximum fine of NZD 50,000 for an individual or NZD 250,000 for a body corporate, plus a further fine of up to three times the amount of the tax evaded. Civil penalties: shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax shortfall for evasion (evasion or similar act) and 40% for lack of reasonable care; use-of-money interest accrues daily; unlimited reassessment period (no time bar) for fraudulent or wilful evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who aid, abet, or are knowingly concerned in the evasion are personally liable for the company's tax shortfall under section 149 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. A travel ban may be imposed by a court to secure the presence of a director for criminal proceedings; personal bankruptcy proceedings may be initiated upon judgment debt for unpaid penalties. Imprisonment: up to five years' imprisonment for tax evasion under section 143B(1), and up to five years for aiding and abetting, plus restitution orders to the Crown for the unpaid tax. Directors may also be disqualified from managing companies under the Companies Act 1993 following conviction.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may invoke the GAAR under section BG 1 to void the tax avoidance arrangement; transfer pricing adjustments under sections GC 1 to GC 14 reallocate income to reflect the economic substance of the parties; the company may be treated as not resident in New Zealand for tax purposes under section YD 4 if its centre of management is outside New Zealand, triggering full loss of access to New Zealand imputation credits and potential NRWT on deemed dividends. Administrative penalties: NZD 500 per month late filing penalties for failure to file, and shortfall penalties up to 40% for taking an unacceptable tax position; deregistration of the company may be sought by the Registrar of Companies under the Companies Act 1993 for 'sub-standard' compliance, including failure to maintain a registered office or satisfy reporting obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for debts of the company incurred while trading while insolvent (reckless trading) under section 135 of the Companies Act 1993, which permits civil recovery from directors where the company has entered into arrangements with intent to defraud creditors or for any fraudulent purpose. Inland Revenue may recover unpaid PAYE, GST, or other taxes from directors under the 'director penalty' regime if the company fails to pay deducted amounts, including PAYE and GST, within specified periods. Travel bans may be issued by the High Court to compel director attendance in examinations under the Tax Administration Act; serious fraud or dishonesty prosecutions under the Crimes Act 1961 can result in imprisonment of up to seven years for obtaining by deception and up to ten years for forgery or false accounting.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under sections GC 1 through GC 14 of the Income Tax Act 2007, reallocating profits to the New Zealand manufacturer; shortfall penalty under section 141A of the Tax Administration Act 1994 of 20% of the resulting tax shortfall for taking an unacceptable tax position; 40% for lack of reasonable care; 150% for evasion or similar act. Late payment penalties and use-of-money interest accrue; a 'large multinational group' (annual worldwide turnover above EUR 750 million) faces publication of a country-by-country report and risk of specific anti-avoidance measures under BEPS 2018.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve transfer pricing policies that are not at arm's length may be personally liable to the company for losses occasioned by breach of director duties (sections 131-137 of the Companies Act 1993); they may also be personally liable for penalties where they sign and file an incorrect return under section 143A(2) (filing false or misleading return) and are subject to fines up to NZD 50,000. In the case of deliberate tax evasion by the company, directors may be prosecuted personally and face imprisonment up to five years; a court may also order a director to repay the tax shortfall under a restitution order, and overseas travel may be restricted by bail conditions or by an order of the High Court.
AML / Anti-Money Laundering Breach

Statutory sanction: AML/CFT penalties under the AML/CFT Act 2009 for reporting entities (including financial institutions and payment service providers like Stripe, and the NZ company if it is itself a reporting entity) range up to NZD 200,000 for natural persons and NZD 2 million for body corporates for each breach of the prescribed customer due diligence, suspicious transaction reporting, or record-keeping obligations. Breaches of the Financial Transaction Reporting Act are prosecuted by the Department of Internal Affairs; the High Court may order civil penalty orders; continuing failures attract further daily penalties. For money laundering or financing of terrorism, criminal conviction under the Crimes Act 1961 carries imprisonment of up to seven years for money laundering (section 243), and up to fourteen years for participating in an organised criminal group or financing terrorism.

Director personal liability: Directors of reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2009 are personally exposed to civil penalty proceedings if they knowingly or recklessly cause the company to breach its obligations; the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and Department of Internal Affairs may commence proceedings against 'any person' who is a director or senior manager. In criminal money-laundering cases, directors who control the transactions are principal offenders and may be sentenced to imprisonment (up to seven years per count), and a pecuniary penalty order may be imposed under the Proceeds of Crime Act 1991 or the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, empowering the Crown to seize property up to the value of the benefit derived from the offending. Travel bans are commonly imposed as bail conditions for charged directors, and there is no statutory cap on the cumulative value of civil penalty orders for multiple breaches.
Dividend and Imputation Fraud

Statutory sanction: Dividend penalty tax under section OB 40 of the Income Tax Act 2007, being the amount of the imputation credit attached to the dividend divided by 28% (i.e., 72/28 of the debit balance) imposed on the company; NRWT of 30% must be withheld on the gross dividend; a company that attaches imputation credits in excess of its ICA balance must pay the 'further income tax' at 28% plus dividend penalty tax. Late payment penalties and use-of-money interest apply. Criminal sanctions for knowingly false declarations or return of misleading information under the Tax Administration Act 1994 attract fines up to NZD 250,000 for a company and forfeiture of the benefit.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorise the payment of an imputed dividend when they know the ICA is in debit are jointly and severally liable with the company to pay the dividend penalty tax under the trust and agency provisions of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Directors may be personally liable to the company for breach of fiduciary duty for causing an unlawful distribution, and creditors may sue under section 174 of the Companies Act 1993. Criminal liability for false accounting under the Crimes Act 1961 may lead to imprisonment of up to seven years, and the court may order reparation to Inland Revenue for unpaid taxes.
