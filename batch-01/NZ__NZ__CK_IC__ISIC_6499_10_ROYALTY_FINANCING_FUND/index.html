Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

Registrar of Companies, New Zealand Companies Office, Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE); online filing through Companies Office Online using a RealMe verified identity.

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (Public Act 1993 No 105), New Zealand, as consolidated; principally: sections 12 to 16 (incorporation, registration and certificate of incorporation), section 27 (constitution), sections 38 to 54 (capital, shares and no-par-value shares), sections 122 to 129 (board of directors' powers), sections 131 to 140 (directors' duties), section 151(2) (at least one New Zealand-resident director), and section 180 (registered office). New Zealand has no separate 'International Company' incorporation statute; the International Company (CK_IC) designation is an administrative and governance label recorded in the company constitution and shareholder documents, with legal personality derived solely from the Companies Act 1993.

Formation / constitutional filings

Form 5 – Application for Registration of a Company (electronic filing in Companies Office Online), together with Form 6 – Company Name Reservation where a name reservation is lodged separately, and Form 2 – Consent to Appointment as Director; the Registrar issues a Certificate of Incorporation and allocates a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) upon approval.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is required under the Companies Act 1993. Shares may be issued with no par value for lawful consideration to be fixed by the board. No escrow account or upfront capital deposit is mandated. For the International Company status, the initial subscriber will subscribe one ordinary share at NZD 1.00 (fully paid) as nominal consideration; the company must maintain a registered office in New Zealand and at least one director resident in New Zealand.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand, Te Tari Taake; online portal: myIR (secure web portal and taxpayer digital services)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity, including an International Company formed under the Cook Islands International Companies Act, must apply for a New Zealand IRD number for company tax and GST purposes. The application is made via the myIR portal or by filing the relevant IRD application for non-resident companies (IR596 or equivalent), accompanied by certified copies of the certificate of incorporation, registered office certificate, director/shareholder information, and evidence of the entity's tax residence status. If the entity is not resident in New Zealand, it may still be required to register for GST if it carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand or supplies remote services to New Zealand consumers above the registration threshold. The IRD number is issued without a legal requirement for a local registered office, but a New Zealand agent or tax representative may be required for service of documents and compliance purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box election comparable to the U.S. regime. Instead, the Income Tax Act 2007 provides for specific elections and deeming rules including: the election for look-through treatment for certain closely held companies (LTC election); the controlled foreign company (CFC) attribution election and active business exemption; the qualifying company election regime (now closed to new entrants); and the hybrid mismatch rules which contain elections to adjust the treatment of certain instruments and entities. The Cook Islands International Company is generally treated as a non-resident company, and New Zealand's territorial tax rules only subject it to income tax on certain New Zealand-sourced income, if it carries on a business through a New Zealand fixed establishment or has other statutory nexus.

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of exported goods under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 applies where goods are exported by the supplier, delivered to a carrier for direct export, or supplied to a non-resident for delivery outside New Zealand. Services supplied to non-residents may be zero-rated under section 11A where the services are supplied in relation to goods outside New Zealand, or are supplied in the course of a taxable activity and the services are not physically performed in New Zealand and the non-resident is outside New Zealand at the time of supply. An International Company engaged in royalty financing and revenue-based financing of digital brands may zero-rate offshore financing and ancillary services where the services are supplied to non-residents and the place of supply is outside New Zealand. Interest income and certain financial services are generally exempt (input-taxed) rather than zero-rated under GST.

  • Commercial Invoice / Tax Invoice showing NZ GST number and zero-rating basis
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical export of goods
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank statement proving receipt of foreign currency export proceeds
  • Underlying contract or royalty/financing agreement supporting the off-shore service supplied

No universal real-time fiscal clearance mandate or digital stamping system currently exists. New Zealand operates a voluntary e-invoicing framework aligned with the Peppol standard, and taxpayers are required to maintain sufficient electronic records in a format accessible to Inland Revenue. Tax invoices (including buyer-created tax invoices) must be issued within 28 days of the supply, or as agreed under a GST group election. Inland Revenue has increasingly mandated electronic filing of GST returns and supports API-based data exchange for payroll and tax information, but there is no transaction-by-transaction real-time fiscal clearance requirement.

Inland Revenue may audit zero-rated export supplies and require full documentary substantiation. Where a taxpayer fails to hold sufficient evidence that goods were exported or services were supplied to a non-resident, the supply is recharacterised as standard-rated and GST becomes payable at 15%, with late payment penalties and use-of-money interest applying from the original due date. Knowingly or negligently zero-rating ineligible supplies may attract a tax shortfall penalty of up to 150% of the resulting tax shortfall for abusive tax positions, and up to 100% for not taking reasonable care. Fraudulent GST refund claims and intentional evasion can lead to criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994, imprisonment up to 5 years, and fines up to NZD 50,000 for individuals and NZD 250,000 for companies. The Commissioner may issue a Notice of Assessment clawing back refunds and, in significant cases, obtain freezing or charging orders over assets. There is no time bar where evasion or fraud is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible under a free-floating exchange rate regime. There are no residual exchange controls under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989, and the Exchange Control Act 1953 has been fully repealed. Current account transactions (royalties, interest, dividends, trade payments, and profit remittances) and capital account transactions (foreign direct investment, intercompany loans, portfolio investment, and repatriation of invested capital) may be effected freely without prior approval from the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. No material profit remittance hurdles exist; however, outward remittances by a New Zealand banking customer are subject to standard non-resident withholding tax obligations (15%–30% depending on applicable Double Tax Agreement or domestic concession) and to the bank's AML/CFT transaction verification protocols. The Overseas Investment Act 2005 imposes consent requirements only for acquisitions of sensitive New Zealand land, significant business assets above statutory thresholds, or fishing quota; a non-dilutive revenue-based financing fund investing in digital brands through contractual financing arrangements does not trigger this regime. The NZD is not subject to mark-to-market conversion restrictions, nor are there custodian requirements for foreign currency holdings.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no export proceeds surrender rules, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no statutory time limit for repatriation of export receipts. Exporters, including fintech financing funds receiving royalties from cross-border merchants, may lawfully retain foreign currency balances in New Zealand foreign currency accounts indefinitely. The only obligations are statistical and monitoring in nature: transactions exceeding NZD 10,000 must be reported through the underlying bank for the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's Balance of Payments survey, and all reporting entities must maintain records of the economic substance of receipt/disbursement flows for at least five years under the AML/CFT Act. No requirement exists to convert export earnings into NZD.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), exercising prudential supervision over Registered Banks, payment systems, and licensed non-bank deposit takers, acting alongside the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for market conduct, and the Department of Internal Affairs for the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) supervisory framework.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for operational payment flows and Low for statutory capital escrow. New Zealand does not maintain a distinct Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licensing class comparable to the European Union regime. Cross-border digital payment providers operate in New Zealand as registered financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and, where they hold client funds, as reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act supervised by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. Wise (Wise Payments NZ Limited), Airwallex (NZ) Limited, and similar API-driven multi-currency providers are legally viable for merchant mass payouts, expense management, and rapid cross-border settlement, but none may lawfully operate a deposit-taking Capital Escrow Account equivalent to that of a Registered Bank. Consequently, the treasury architecture mandates a Registered Bank for the escrow/statutory layer and permits a licensed fintech FSP only as an aggregation and disbursement interface, never as the custodian of client capital.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward capital injections from investors or lending providers must be executed as SWIFT MT103/MT202 messages to the fund's New Zealand Registered Bank account. The payment instruction must include the fund's legal name and account number, the bank's SWIFT/BIC, a balance-of-payments purpose code designated by the bank — either 'Direct Investment – Equity' or 'Direct Investment – Intercompany Lending' as applicable — and a non-ambiguous remittance field referencing the executed Subscription Agreement, Capital Call Notice, or Intercompany Loan Agreement. The originating bank must send the wire from the investor's own account; third-party or cash-deposit wires are rejected. Any transaction equal to or exceeding NZD 10,000 attracts records and verification requirements; amounts exceeding NZD 500,000 will ordinarily trigger enhanced due diligence, including a governing-body resolution, an ultimate-beneficial-owner certificate, and confirmation that no politically exposed person is involved. Intercompany loans from connected entities must be supported by a formal loan agreement with commercial-rate interest to preserve the debt characterisation and to enable future interest deductions if the borrower becomes New Zealand-resident. No Reserve Bank approval is required for the inward capital injection.Domestic disbursements to New Zealand digital-brand merchants settle through the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) using Direct Debit on merchant-signed ACH mandates and, for same-day high-value payments, through the CorePlus high-value clearing system operated by the New Zealand Clearing Association with final settlement across Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) balances at the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. Cross-border merchant disbursements are routed as SWIFT MT103 remittances via the bank's correspondent network (for example, JPMorgan Chase or Citibank for USD) including ISO 20022 structured remittance advice, or through Commercial Letters of Credit (UCP 600) where a supplier requires documentary protection. For recurring multi-currency mass payouts to digital brands in multiple overseas jurisdictions, the fund may instruct a licensed fintech FSP (Wise or Airwallex) to become the payment processor, with the NZD funding leg held in the Registered Bank account; final beneficial delivery into each merchant's local clearing system is effected by the FSP's licensed subsidiaries. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium for operational payment flows and Low for statutory capital escrow. New Zealand does not maintain a distinct Electronic Money Institution (EMI) licensing class comparable to the European Union regime. Cross-border digital payment providers operate in New Zealand as registered financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and, where they hold client funds, as reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act supervised by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. Wise (Wise Payments NZ Limited), Airwallex (NZ) Limited, and similar API-driven multi-currency providers are legally viable for merchant mass payouts, expense management, and rapid cross-border settlement, but none may lawfully operate a deposit-taking Capital Escrow Account equivalent to that of a Registered Bank. Consequently, the treasury architecture mandates a Registered Bank for the escrow/statutory layer and permits a licensed fintech FSP only as an aggregation and disbursement interface, never as the custodian of client capital.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

0% for a Cook Islands International Company (IC) operating in the statutory tax-exempt zone under the International Companies Act 1981; residual domestic corporate income tax rate of 20% applies only to non-exempt local-source income if IC status is revoked.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

0% on dividends paid by a Cook Islands International Company to non-resident shareholders (full statutory exemption under the International Companies Act 1981); residual statutory non-resident dividend withholding tax of 15% applies only to dividends paid by domestic Cook Islands companies not enjoying IC status.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No formal transfer pricing statute applies to Cook Islands International Companies; however, prudent practice mandates preparation of a Local File and Master File where all related-party cross-border transactions—including subordinated royalty financing advances and intercompany funding—exceed CI$1,000,000 (Cook Islands Dollar, pegged 1:1 to New Zealand Dollar) annually, aligned with OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines for Financial Transactions (2022).

Economic substance scope: The Cook Islands has not enacted Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) directly comparable to BVI/Cayman; however, the jurisdiction is subject to OECD Global Forum review and has committed to implementing economic substance requirements for geographically mobile financial activities, including financing and leasing. The royalty financing fund must demonstrate adequate substance in Rarotonga: a physical registered office, at least two resident directors, documented board meetings where financing decisions are made, an operational bank account with discretionary fund management, and complete accounting records covering the non-dilutive revenue-based financing and royalty streams. Failure to maintain substance exposes the IC to recharacterization as a shell by New Zealand or foreign tax authorities under general anti-avoidance doctrines.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion through Mischaracterized Income

Statutory sanction: Revocation of International Company status; assessment at the 20% corporate income tax rate; imposition of late payment penalties up to 100% of the tax and daily interest at 2% per month; criminal fines under the Cook Islands Income Tax Act 1997 and Crimes Act 1969; mandatory disclosure under automatic exchange of information agreements.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false declarations become personally liable for unpaid corporate tax through piercing of the corporate veil; civil restitution orders; travel bans pending the completion of tax investigations; imprisonment for up to 5 years for tax fraud.
Shell Company / Sham Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Application of general anti-avoidance rules recharacterizing the IC as a sham; denial of the 0% exempt rate; deregistration by the Financial Supervisory Commission; adverse OECD Global Forum rating triggering cross-border tax investigations in New Zealand and Australia; potential inclusion in automatic exchange of information (CRS) filings exposing beneficial owners.

Director personal liability: Director disqualification orders; personal liability for restitution to the tax authority for the amount of tax evaded; extradition risk and international travel bans under mutual legal assistance treaties; imprisonment up to 7 years for fraudulent concealment.
AML / Counter-Terrorist Financing Breach in Payment Rails

Statutory sanction: Penalties under the Cook Islands AML/CFT Act 2019 applicable to reporting entities: financial penalties up to CI$1,000,000 for non-compliance; mandatory closure of payment rails; revocation of any financial services authorization; criminal prosecution for unlicensed financial service provision where fund activity crosses the licensing threshold.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal criminal liability for money laundering offences where they knowingly accepted undisclosed or tainted funds; imprisonment up to 10 years; asset forfeiture and confiscation orders; travel bans and extradition exposure to requesting states.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalization Failure

Statutory sanction: If the counterparty is a New Zealand resident, the New Zealand Inland Revenue may apply transfer pricing adjustments under the Income Tax Act 2007 (sections GC 13-GC 16) and thin capitalization rules (section FE 6), imputing additional taxable income and denying interest deductions; penalties of up to 150% of the resultant tax shortfall for documented transfer pricing failure plus use-of-money interest; equivalent adjustments may be made by any treaty partner under OECD Pillar Two rules.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign incorrect transfer pricing documentation face personal liability for penalties in certain jurisdictions; civil proceedings for reimbursement of corporate tax liabilities; disqualification from serving as directors of international companies; travel bans where criminal tax evasion is established.
