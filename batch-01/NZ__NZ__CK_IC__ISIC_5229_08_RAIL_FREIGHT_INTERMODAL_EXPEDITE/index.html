Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Taake), New Zealand Government; online portal: myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz/myIR)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity (including a non-resident offshore International Company) that begins carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand must first be registered with the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993 if it establishes a place of business or carries on business in New Zealand. Upon Companies Office registration, the entity applies to Inland Revenue for an IRD number (New Zealand tax file number) via the myIR portal or paper form IR596. GST registration is a separate statutory application made simultaneously or subsequently on the same myIR account; the IRD number serves as the taxpayer identifier and the GST registration is evidenced by a GST number (same IRD number with a GST suffix). Where the foreign entity has no New Zealand taxable activity and derives no New Zealand-source income, it is not required to obtain an IRD number for income tax purposes, but it may voluntarily register for GST if it carries on a taxable activity and satisfies the non-resident GST registration rules under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985.

Default tax classification

This classification does not constitute a tax exemption certificate and remains subject to general anti-avoidance and hybrid-mismatch rules.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime. Relevant statutory elections for a foreign International Company are: (1) GST grouping election under section 55 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, permitting GST-registered entities in a wholly-owned group to be treated as a single group; (2) GST apportionment election for mixed-use inputs; (3) election to use a payments basis for GST accounting under section 19 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (4) for qualifying close-held New Zealand companies, the look-through company (LTC) election under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, generally unavailable to non-resident companies; and (5) the hybrid mismatch timing and imported mismatch elections under the BEPS hybrid mismatch rules in the Income Tax Act 2007, which must be exercised where the International Company participates in hybrid instruments or otherwise disregarded payments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (annual turnover from taxable supplies made in New Zealand over any consecutive 12-month period; registration is compulsory once the threshold is exceeded or supplies are made with the intent of exceeding it. A non-resident supplier providing remote or imported services may be required to register under the reverse charge and remote services rules even below the threshold if it elects the simplified return filing regime for non-resident suppliers).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of exported goods (section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985) where the supplier exports the goods within 28 days of the time of supply and retains evidence of physical export from New Zealand; to services supplied to a non-resident person outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed (section 11A), except services supplied for or in connection with movable property in New Zealand at the time of supply or services supplied to a non-resident who is in New Zealand at the time of supply; and to international freight, transport and ancillary logistics services supplied in connection with goods exported from New Zealand, including transcontinental rail freight forwarding where the New Zealand leg is limited to pre-export landside handling and the onward movement is supplied to a non-resident. The taxpayer must hold contemporaneous documentary evidence proving the goods left New Zealand and the services were performed for the overseas recipient.

  • Customs Export Declaration (New Zealand Customs Service export entry / EX form filed through the Trade Single Window and Customs New Zealand Cargo Processing System), Bill of Lading, Inward Remittance Certificate
  • Air waybill or railway consignment note (CIM/SMGS consignment note) for intermodal shipments
  • Supplier's tax invoice and GST registration number
  • Packing list and export shipping instructions
  • Evidence of payment or bank statement evidencing foreign currency remittance
  • Insurance certificate evidencing overseas transit cover

New Zealand does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction controls, or digital stamping. Inland Revenue does not operate a pre-clearance e-invoicing gateway. E-invoicing is voluntary and operates through the Peppol network facilitated by the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE); Peppol PDF and UBL formats are acceptable. Tax invoices must contain the words 'tax invoice', the supplier's name and GST number, the date, description, quantity, price and GST amount. Inland Revenue accepts electronic records as primary evidence and requires taxpayers to maintain sufficient records in paper or digital form for export zero-rating and refund substantiation requirements.

Zero-rating is audited strictly by Inland Revenue on documentation and physical export evidence. If goods are not exported within 28 days, or services do not satisfy section 11A of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, the supply becomes taxable at 15% GST and the supplier is liable for the GST output tax plus use-of-money interest from the date the GST should have been returned. Inland Revenue can reassess outside the standard four-year time bar where tax evasion, including deliberate false export declarations, is established; the reassessment period then extends to ten years. Civil shortfall penalties under sections 141-141KA of the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply: 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and up to 150% for evasion, plus late payment penalties and use-of-money interest. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims may also attract criminal prosecution under section 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994 and under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, including seizure of goods and fines up to NZD 250,000 for an individual and NZD 500,000 for a body corporate. A clawback reassessment that shifts the supply from zero-rated to 15% taxable automatically nullifies input credits claimed in reliance on the zero-rating and requires the taxpayer to file an amended GST return.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible on both current account and capital account, and New Zealand has maintained a deliberately free-floating exchange rate regime since March 1985. There are no exchange controls, no quantitative limits on trade-related foreign currency payments, no prior approval requirements for import settlement or freight charges, and no restrictions on the conversion or repatriation of profits, dividends, interest, royalties, or loan principal by non-residents. Current-account transactions, including railway freight charges, intermodal terminal handling, customs duties, insurance premia, and forwarding commissions, may be settled freely in any convertible currency at commercial bank rates. Capital-account remittances remain open and unrestricted, subject only to: (i) the screening of specific investments that acquire New Zealand sensitive land or significant business assets above statutory thresholds, which triggers OIO consent obligations; (ii) the non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) framework under the Income Tax Act 2007, which applies to dividends and interest paid by an NZ-resident entity to non-residents and may be reduced by an applicable double tax agreement or, in the case of interest, mitigated by the approved issuer levy (AIL) regime of 2% where eligibility conditions are met; and (iii) the AML/CFT verification and sanctions-screening duties imposed on banks under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. For the CK_IC entity, profit remittance and shareholder-loan repayments encounter no FX convertibility hurdle; the only practical requirements are proper corporate authorisation, tax compliance certification, and standard banking due diligence.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender or mandatory conversion regime exists in New Zealand. Exporters and service providers, including freight forwarding entities, are entitled to retain 100% of export receipts and foreign-currency earnings in domestic or offshore foreign-currency accounts and may convert them to NZD or any other currency at their discretion. The legal timeline for surrender or conversion is not applicable (N/A), and the mandatory conversion ratio is 0%. The only compliance overlay is informational and statistical: banks must classify cross-border transactions, including export freight revenue, for the monthly balance-of-payments statistical returns submitted to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand and Statistics New Zealand, and the AML/CFT Act 2009 imposes cash transaction reporting for physical currency movements of NZ$10,000 or more. For the rail-freight intermodal expedite operation, the treasury file should nonetheless maintain invoices, CIM/SMGS or equivalent rail consignment documents, bills of lading, and bank receipts evidencing the trade and payment loop, which will support the legitimate export-business narrative during any enhanced due diligence review.

Central bank supervision: The Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) is the statutory central bank, monetary policy authority, and prudential supervisor of banks and payment systems under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021. There is no separate domestic foreign exchange control administration; residual screening authority over inward and outward capital movements involving sensitive land or significant business assets is exercised by the Overseas Investment Office (Toitū Te Whenua Land Information New Zealand) under the Overseas Investment Act 2005. The Financial Markets Authority regulates derivatives issuers and financial advice under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, which governs the permitted providers of FX hedging instruments.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for ordinary cross-border disbursements and low-value supplier payments; Low for fiduciary escrow, documentary letters of credit, and core treasury warehousing. New Zealand does not have a discrete EMI licence class; global digital payment operators are regulated onshore as financial service providers registered under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and as reporting entities under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise Platform, Airwallex, Currencycloud, and Ebury may lawfully offer multi-currency accounts, cross-currency conversion, global payment APIs, and batch disbursement rails to the CK_IC entity through a local FSP registration and a custodial relationship with a licensed partner bank. Principal legal constraints: (i) an EMI is not a registered bank under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989, may not accept deposits in the ordinary course of business, and may not call itself a bank; (ii) client funds must be held in segregated trust accounts at licensed banks, exposing the IC to the counterparty credit risk of both the EMI and its partner bank; (iii) an EMI cannot issue commercial letters of credit, standby credits, or on-demand guarantees, which must be routed through a licensed bank; (iv) EMIs carry identical AML/CFT and sanctions-screening obligations as banks, so the KYC documentary burden is not diminished; and (v) funds held with an EMI are not covered by the Depositor Compensation Scheme established by the Depositor Compensation Act 2023, since that scheme is limited to licensed deposit takers such as registered banks. For the Eurasia rail intermodal operation, the prudent architecture is to maintain the primary treasury, escrow, and hedging relationships with a tier-1 licensed bank and to use an EMI selectively as a secondary disbursement rail for low-value operational payments.

Principal legal constraints: (i) an EMI is not a registered bank under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989, may not accept deposits in the ordinary course of business, and may not call itself a bank; (ii) client funds must be held in segregated trust accounts at licensed banks, exposing the IC to the counterparty credit risk of both the EMI and its partner bank; (iii) an EMI cannot issue commercial letters of credit, standby credits, or on-demand guarantees, which must be routed through a licensed bank; (iv) EMIs carry identical AML/CFT and sanctions-screening obligations as banks, so the KYC documentary burden is not diminished; and (v) funds held with an EMI are not covered by the Depositor Compensation Scheme established by the Depositor Compensation Act 2023, since that scheme is limited to licensed deposit takers such as registered banks. For the Eurasia rail intermodal operation, the prudent architecture is to maintain the primary treasury, escrow, and hedging relationships with a tier-1 licensed bank and to use an EMI selectively as a secondary disbursement rail for low-value operational payments.
