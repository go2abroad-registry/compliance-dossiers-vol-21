Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue (Inland Revenue Department / Te Tari Taake), New Zealand; online portal: myIR (https://myir.ird.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

The Cook Islands International Company (CK IC) is a non-resident foreign company for New Zealand tax purposes. It is required to obtain a New Zealand IRD number only where it derives New Zealand-source income subject to income tax, or where it must register (or elects to register) for GST. Procedure for a non-resident entity: (1) assemble certified copies of the Cook Islands certificate of incorporation, the international company's constitution, register of directors and shareholders, and underlying beneficial ownership records; (2) lodge the prescribed non-resident company IRD number application (form IR596) or apply online through myIR, either directly or through an appointed New Zealand tax agent; (3) if GST registration is required or voluntary, complete form IR372 (or the online GST registration in myIR) indicating the estimated 12-month turnover of taxable supplies in New Zealand, the commencement date of the taxable activity, New Zealand bank account details, and the nature of the cross-border e-commerce operations; (4) Inland Revenue normally issues the IRD number and, where applicable, the GST registration number within 10 to 20 working days. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone: the CK IC is a Cook Islands International Company resident and incorporated in the Cook Islands, exempt from Cook Islands income tax on offshore-sourced income under the International Companies Act; for New Zealand tax purposes it is a non-resident foreign company taxable only on New Zealand-source income or income attributable to a New Zealand permanent establishment (PE), with foreign-source income fully outside the New Zealand income tax net absent a PE. Available election forms

New Zealand has no US-style check-the-box regime. Statutory elections relevant to a non-resident IC include: (i) voluntary GST registration for a non-resident supplier whose New Zealand taxable supplies are below the NZD 60,000 threshold; (ii) election to treat associated companies as a GST group under section 55 of the GST Act 1985; (iii) election to zero-rate supplies of a going concern or land; (iv) for New Zealand-resident close companies, the Look-Through Company (LTC) election (section HB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007) which confers fiscal transparency - unavailable to a non-resident IC; (v) elections and deemed elections under the hybrid and branch mismatch rules in Subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007 (including the imported mismatch 'CBC report' concession); and (vi) foreign-currency realisation timing elections for tax accounting. None of these elections alter the fundamental non-resident, offshore exemption character of the CK IC.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period (GST Act 1985, s 51). For non-resident suppliers of remote services (including digital services) and for non-resident sellers of low-value imported goods (each item not exceeding NZD 1,000) sold through an electronic marketplace, registration is mandatory once the NZD 60,000 threshold is exceeded, and voluntary below it. Where an approved marketplace operator (e.g., Amazon) is liable for GST on the low-value goods under sections 60B-60C of the GST Act 1985, the underlying non-resident seller (CK IC) is not required to register solely by reason of those supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under sections 11 and 11A of the GST Act 1985, a supply of goods is zero-rated when the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply (or such extended time as the Commissioner allows) and obtains the statutory export evidence. Goods sold through an Amazon FBA model are zero-rated where the goods are removed from New Zealand for supply to an overseas buyer. Services (including marketplace facilitation, advertising, logistics software and fulfilment services) are zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply and are not performed in connection with New Zealand land or goods, or where they are 'remote services' consumed outside New Zealand (s 11A). To sustain the zero rate, the supplier must retain evidence that the goods left New Zealand (customs and carrier records) and that the exported goods were paid for (foreign exchange receipts), and for services, evidence of the customer's non-resident status.

  • Customs Export Declaration (export entry validated in the New Zealand Customs Trade Single Window)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank or payment-rail evidence of receipt of foreign currency for the exported goods)
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Carrier shipping manifest / tracking report demonstrating physical departure of goods from New Zealand

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory tax-authority API clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in New Zealand. E-invoicing operates on the voluntary Peppol BIS 3.0 standard under the trans-Tasman e-Invoicing initiative, with certified access points exchanging standard e-invoices. From 1 April 2025, suppliers invoicing New Zealand central government agencies must deliver e-invoices via a certified Peppol access point. Inland Revenue does not pre-approve or digitally stamp invoices; GST returns and zero-rated export schedules are filed electronically via myIR, and export validation is performed through automated data matching between Inland Revenue and the New Zealand Customs Service (Trade Single Window).

Inland Revenue and New Zealand Customs conduct automated cross-matching of zero-rated export claims against export entries; any mismatch triggers a review and recharacterisation of the supply as taxable at 15% GST, with the tax shortfall plus use-of-money interest being assessed. A clawback also arises where goods claimed as exported are re-imported, diverted back into New Zealand, or where the 28-day export period is missed without a Commissioner extension. Civil shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 (ss 120-141) apply at 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and 150% for evasion or abusive tax positions; fraudulent export documentation attracts criminal prosecution with fines and imprisonment of up to five years. Under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, false export declarations also expose the exporter to seizure and forfeiture of the goods and additional monetary penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand has a floating exchange rate regime with full current account and capital account convertibility. There are no exchange controls, no restrictions on foreign currency accounts, and no approvals required for profit repatriation, dividends, or capital injections by non-residents. The only checks are AML/CFT verification by the reporting entity in the transfer chain. The NZD is freely convertible, and cross-border payments can be made without prior RBNZ approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand does not impose any statutory surrender requirements for export proceeds. Exporters may hold foreign currency earnings in NZ bank accounts or overseas without conversion or repatriation deadlines. No mandatory conversion ratio applies. Returns for statistical purposes are required only under RBNZ reporting forms (e.g., certain balance of payments reporting), but there is no surrender rule.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) and the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for conduct; the RBNZ administers the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989, and the Foreign Exchange (FE) regime is liberal. There is no New Zealand Foreign Exchange Administration; wire transactions and FX dealing are handled by registered banks and financial service providers subject to AML/CFT and FMA rules.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High feasibility for auxiliary payment processing and cross-border collection (e.g., Airwallex, Stripe, Windcave). However, an offshore digital EMI cannot provide full corporate banking services licensed under New Zealand law, cannot take deposits, and may require registration as a financial service provider under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 if it conducts business with the New Zealand public. The CK_IC may use EMIs as settlement rail for FBA proceeds and supplier disbursements, but main treasury liquidity should be housed in a licensed New Zealand bank for credibility and regulatory clarity.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% statutory Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders, unless reduced under an applicable Double Tax Agreement (DTA). For a resident shareholder, resident withholding tax (RWT) may apply at 33% (or 28% for imputed dividends).

For a standard 31 March balance date, the IR4 return is due no later than 7 July. For a non-standard balance date, the return must be filed by the 7th day of the fourth month after the balance date, unless a tax agent extension is granted (commonly extending to 31 March of the following year).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 (aggregate cross-border related-party transactions per income year) – based on Inland Revenue's administrative de minimis safe harbour. Above this threshold, contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation, including a Local File and (if applicable) a Master File, is expected to be maintained.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand is not an Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) jurisdiction; the IRD instead applies the general anti-avoidance rule (Part IVA of the Income Tax Act 2007) and New Zealand's controlled foreign company (CFC) rules. An International Company classified as 'Tax_Exempt_Zone' must nevertheless demonstrate real commercial substance (e.g., local management, physical presence, payroll, contracts) in the jurisdiction in which it claims residence; otherwise the IRD may impose tax on its New Zealand-sourced income by treating it as having a PE or by challenging the structure under GAAR. For Amazon FBA sellers, maintaining warehouse inventory in New Zealand (or via third-party fulfillment) and employing local agents will create a PE, regardless of any purported tax-exempt status.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Omission

Statutory sanction: Under Sections 143A and 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, a person convicted of tax evasion is liable to a fine of up to NZD 50,000 per charge and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Civil shortfall penalties may reach 150% of the tax underpaid. Inland Revenue may also issue a notice of assessment based on the amount actually repatriated, with compound interest accruing.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorise or are knowingly party to the evasion may be prosecuted personally. Courts can issue civil restitution orders, disqualify directorial office, impose travel bans for outstanding revenue debts, and impose terms of imprisonment of up to 5 years under the Crimes Act 1961.
Shell Company / Abusive Tax Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Part IVA (GAAR) enables the Commissioner of Inland Revenue to reconstruct the taxable profit and disregard the arrangement for tax purposes. Shortfall penalties apply at 20% for unreasonable tax position, 100% for abusive tax position, or 150% for evasion. The Companies Act 1993, s 634 (fraudulent trading) may be invoked to make the company and its directors liable for debts and taxes.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally and severally liable for company taxes under s 372 of the Companies Act 1993 if they signed false returns or allowed trading while knowing the company was insolvent. Restitution orders, travel bans, and potential imprisonment for up to 10 years under the Crimes Act for false accounting may apply.
AML/CFT Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, a body corporate commits an offence if it does not comply with its AML/CFT obligations; penalties include civil penalties of up to NZD 2 million per contravention and criminal fines for senior officers. Additionally, the Financial Markets Authority may issue public warning notices, and persistent non-compliance may result in the winding up of the company.

Director personal liability: Senior officers, including directors and beneficial owners with substantial control, may be individually fined up to NZD 300,000 and imprisoned for up to 2 years for knowingly permitting money laundering or terrorist financing. Courts may also issue banning orders against individuals from holding a directorship in any New Zealand company.
Transfer Pricing / Base Erosion

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may adjust the taxable profit under ss GC 8–GC 14 of the Income Tax Act 2007 to reflect an arm's length outcome. A shortfall penalty of 20% (inadequate documentation) or 50% (unacceptable tax position) applies; where the transfer pricing adjustment exceeds NZD 100,000 and an arrangement is abusive, a penalty of 150% may be imposed.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve transfer pricing documentation without exercising reasonable care may be subject to joint and several liability for the resulting shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act. If a director knew, or should have known, that the pricing was contrived and caused the company to evade tax, the court may impose personal civil liability and, for serious cases, travel bans or imprisonment.
