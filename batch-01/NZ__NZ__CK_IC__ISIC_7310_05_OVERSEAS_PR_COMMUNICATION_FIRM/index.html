Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by New Zealand law; there is no escrow account requirement. The company may issue shares without fully paying them at incorporation, and no bank-controlled capital deposit is required. For International Company status, the beneficial ownership and management must remain non-resident and the company must derive no New Zealand-sourced income to preserve the tax-exempt treatment.

Funding & unlock path: The company must obtain a Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) for the office premises if the building hosts specified systems (fire alarms, sprinklers, air-conditioning, emergency lighting, or lifts). A fire evacuation procedure, means-of-escape certification, and safety signage in compliance with the Building Act 2004 and the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 are required. Before occupation, the tenant must procure structural, electrical, and plumbing compliance statements from the building owner, and file a 'Form 12A - Change of Building Use or Extension of Building Life' if the use changes occupancy. There is no civil-defence clearance specific to a public relations firm, but emergency preparedness and evacuation arrangements must be documented and exercised annually.

Paid-in capital rule: No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by New Zealand law; there is no escrow account requirement. The company may issue shares without fully paying them at incorporation, and no bank-controlled capital deposit is required. For International Company status, the beneficial ownership and management must remain non-resident and the company must derive no New Zealand-sourced income to preserve the tax-exempt treatment.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department of New Zealand (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Tāke); official taxpayer portal: myIR Secure Online Services (myir.ird.govt.nz) and public information portal www.ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

A non-resident offshore International Company (CK_IC) that has any New Zealand GST-registrable activity must first obtain a New Zealand IRD number. Procedure: (1) file Form IR596 'Application for IRD number for a non-resident company' with Inland Revenue, attaching the certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents, board resolutions, and identification of directors and ultimate shareholders; (2) upon IRD number issuance, apply for GST registration either online through myIR or by filing Form IR356 'GST registration form', selecting the correct filing frequency (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly); (3) if the company carries on a taxable activity wholly outside New Zealand, GST registration is voluntary only when below the NZD 60,000 taxable-supplies threshold and compulsory when that threshold is exceeded; (4) obtain an NZBN (New Zealand Business Number) only if creating a local establishment, which is not required for pure offshore service operations; (5) appointing a New Zealand agent or tax adviser is not statutorily mandatory but is recommended for myIR credentials and service of notice, because the branch/agent may be treated as the company's representative for tax administration purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone: The CK_IC is classified as a non-resident exempt offshore International Company. It is outside the scope of New Zealand income tax on all foreign-source income provided its centre of effective management is outside New Zealand, it derives no New Zealand-sourced income, and it does not maintain a New Zealand permanent establishment (ss. YD 2 and YD 4, Income Tax Act 2007). The company has no New Zealand entity-level income tax liability while the exempt-zone conditions are continuously satisfied. For indirect tax purposes, it is not subject to New Zealand GST on supplies made wholly outside New Zealand, but it may have GST registration and return obligations to the extent it makes taxable supplies in or from New Zealand (for example, remote services provided to New Zealand-resident consumers or non-zero-rated supplies to New Zealand businesses).

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box election. The available classification elections and provisions are: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under Subpart HB, Income Tax Act 2007, available only to close companies with five or fewer shareholders, causing tax transparency while retaining limited liability; (2) grandfathered Qualifying Company (QC) status for companies that elected before the 2010 regime closure (no new QC elections permitted); (3) voluntary GST registration election under s. 51, GST Act 1985, available to any person carrying on a taxable activity, including non-residents; (4) the GST land zero-rating election based on taxable-use percentage under ss. 11(1)(mb) and 78C, GST Act 1985; (5) hybrid and branch mismatch rule-ordering positions under Subpart FH, Income Tax Act 2007, whereby a taxpayer may elect which mismatch rule applies to a cross-border structure; and (6) application for a private binding ruling under Part 5A, Tax Administration Act 1994, to confirm the zero-rating or offshore treatment of a specific international PR retainer arrangement.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any continuous 12-month period (general statutory threshold and the threshold applicable to non-resident offshore suppliers of remote services). Effective 1 April 2025, the NZD 60,000 registration threshold has been removed for offshore suppliers of remote services and low-value goods supplied to New Zealand consumers, making GST registration compulsory for all such offshore suppliers regardless of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST is available under s. 11(1), GST Act 1985. For goods: the goods must be exported by the supplier within 28 days of the time of supply (s. 11(1)(a)). For services: services are zero-rated if supplied to a person who is non-resident and is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed (s. 11(1)(c)), subject to exceptions for services supplied in connection with New Zealand land, goods in New Zealand, or services physically performed in New Zealand. For the overseas PR and communications firm, international media relations, crisis PR, and advisory retainer services provided to offshore non-resident clients outside New Zealand qualify for zero-rating under s. 11(1)(c). Remote digital deliverables supplied to a New Zealand GST-registered recipient may be zero-rated only where the B2B conditions in s. 11(1)(mb) are satisfied and the recipient acquires the services for the principal purpose of making taxable supplies; otherwise, 15% GST applies.

  • Customs Export Declaration (export entry lodged electronically through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window, evidencing physical export of goods within 28 days of supply and matching the GST zero-rating claim)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (transport document evidencing the goods left New Zealand and the overseas destination; for a pure services firm, this may be replaced by the executed retainer agreement, service delivery reports, and time logs demonstrating delivery to an offshore client)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank-issued certificate or remittance advice from a New Zealand registered bank evidencing receipt of the foreign currency proceeds from the overseas client within the mandated repatriation window)
  • Commercial invoice and inter-party service agreement (contractual documentation establishing the non-resident status of the recipient, the place of performance, and the arm's-length pricing of the PR retainer for audit substantiation)

New Zealand does not impose mandatory electronic invoicing, real-time fiscal clearance, or digital stamping. GST returns are filed electronically via myIR on Form GST101 using a monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly cycle, without government pre-clearance of individual invoices. Voluntary Peppol-based interoperable e-invoicing has been adopted through the NZ e-Invoice framework for B2B transactions, and New Zealand Customs uses the Trade Single Window / CDEX platform for export declarations. A taxpayer claiming zero-rating must nevertheless retain full substantiation of the export or offshore service supply, including tax invoices that meet the requirements of ss. 24 and 24B, GST Act 1985, with foreign-currency amounts and exchange-rate calculations properly documented.

Inland Revenue subjects export zero-rating claims and GST refunds to heightened audit scrutiny, particularly for services exporters and offshore-registered entities. Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, a tax shortfall arising from evasion or an intent to mislead attracts a civil penalty of 150% of the shortfall; an abusive tax position attracts 100%; gross carelessness attracts 40%; and late filing and non-payment penalties accrue in addition to use-of-money interest on unpaid or over-claimed GST. Fraudulent or unsupported zero-rating claims, including false inward remittance certificates, fabricated bills of lading, or fictitious offshore service contracts, trigger retrospective reassessment, because the four-year limitation period does not apply in cases of fraud or misrepresentation, and result in immediate GST refund clawback, deregistration or disqualification from voluntary registration, and criminal prosecution. Conviction carries fines of up to NZD 50,000 for an individual and NZD 250,000 for a company, with imprisonment of up to five years for tax evasion. Directors, shadow directors, and any New Zealand agent who aided the false claim may be held jointly and severally liable for the tax shortfall, penalties, and interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is fully convertible on both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no surrender requirements, no mandatory conversion ratios, no exchange-control permits, and no limitation on profit remittance, dividend distribution, branch repatriation, or liquidation proceeds for residents or non-residents. The Cook Islands operates in practice under the same open regime: NZD is legal tender in the Cook Islands, the Cook Islands dollar (CKD) is pegged at par with NZD and does not create a conversion barrier, and a CK_IC may hold and freely deploy NZD, USD, EUR, AUD, GBP and any other acceptable currency in accounts in New Zealand or abroad. The only profit-remittance hurdle is a tax compliance overlay, not an FX restriction: outbound income distributions sourced from New Zealand may attract non-resident withholding tax (NRWT), which is settled through the banking system and does not require central-bank approval. For capital-account flows, inbound shareholder loans and equity injections require no RBNZ consent, but must be classified accurately for bank reporting, CRS/FATCA disclosures, and the anti-money-laundering travel rule applicable to all cross-border wire transfers of NZD 1,000 or above.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Neither New Zealand nor the Cook Islands imposes export-proceeds surrender rules, statutory repatriation timelines, or mandatory conversion ratios. Receipts for international media relations, crisis communications, and overseas PR retainer services may be retained in the foreign currency of receipt (including USD ACH collections from US clients), held in offshore foreign-currency accounts, swept to the New Zealand operating account at the corporate's election, or redeployed directly to settle trade disbursements. New Zealand's Overseas Investment Act 2005 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 impose no exhaustion requirements on export or service receipts, but do require reporting entities to submit prescribed transaction reports (e.g., large-value cash and cross-border wire reports) to the New Zealand Financial Intelligence Unit, and every wire transfer must carry complete originator and beneficiary information under the domestic travel-rule regulations.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) — the statutory central bank and prudential regulator for the New Zealand banking system, responsible for monetary policy, bank licensing, and the settlement ultimate, and the de facto administrator of New Zealand's open foreign-exchange regime (New Zealand abolished statutory exchange controls in 1984 and no group of the Minister of Finance or the Reserve Bank exercises exchange-control approval powers). The Cook Islands Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) — the home-state offshore regulator for the Cook Islands International Company (CK_IC), administering the International Companies Act 2010-2011 and supervising the registered agent, but not a central bank. The New Zealand Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade (MFAT) administers financial sanctions and asset-freeze lists that bind all reporting entities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM-LOW, with bank-grade EMIs outranking consumer-oriented platforms. Regulated cross-border fintech EMIs legally permitted to onboard a Cook Islands International Company under the AML/CFT Act 2009 include Airwallex (Australian ADI-regulated and NZ FSP-registered), Currenxie (UK FCA-registered EMI), Payoneer, Deel, and Wise. In practice, Wise Business is the least viable because its licensing terms generally require the entity's registered jurisdiction to align with the account country, a condition the CK_IC cannot satisfy without also registering an NZ branch; Payoneer accepts the structure only with a live operating website, signed media contracts, and a director video call; Currenxie demonstrates tolerance for Pacific offshore companies but imposes a strict beneficial-ownership declaration. Airwallex is the most viable digital EMI: it accepts Cook Islands ICs carrying an FSC Certificate of Eligibility, provides virtual NZD and USD accounts, enables US ACH inbound collections, and routes outbound MT103-equivalent SWIFT payments. Legal constraint: any NZ-licensed EMI must apply a risk-based approach; the tax-exempt status of the CK_IC is not a prohibition, but the EMI must obtain a sealed extract of the confidential beneficial-ownership register from the Cook Islands registered agent, a New Zealand physical service address (a registered agent office or PO Box is rejected), and an executed customer due-diligence file before opening virtual accounts. Cross-border digital EMIs with no physical presence in New Zealand are not permitted to onboard the CK_IC without obtaining a Financial Service Provider (FSP) registration under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand's transfer pricing rules apply to cross-border related-party transactions where the New Zealand entity and the foreign entity are associated persons. There is no monetary threshold below which transfer pricing documentation is exempt, but administrative penalties can be neutralised where taxpayers prepare contemporaneous documentation. Inland Revenue's transfer pricing Guidelines require: (i) functional analysis, (ii) economic analysis, (iii) selection of the most appropriate transfer pricing method, and (iv) documentation maintained before the return is filed. The OECD Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) threshold requires a New Zealand-resident ultimate parent of a multinational group with consolidated group revenue of NZ$1.05 billion or more (approximately EUR 750 million under the OECD aligned threshold) to file a CbCR. The local entity of a qualifying MNE group must file a notification (IR402) and may be required to file a Master File and Local File. Although the CbCR threshold is NZ$1.05 billion, a mid-sized PR firm controlled by an offshore parent is unlikely to exceed it, but all related-party cross-border payments, including management fees, service fees, intellectual property royalties, and intercompany loans, must satisfy the arm's length standard regardless of size. New Zealand's transfer pricing penalty regime: where a transfer pricing adjustment arises, a shortfall penalty of 20% of the tax shortfall applies if the taxpayer did not take reasonable care, and 40% if the adjustment exceeds NZ$250,000 or the dominant purpose is tax avoidance. A 100% penalty may apply for evasion. The safe harbour for certain cross-border lease transactions has no general application to intra-group services. International Company entities registered as non-resident foreign companies should not maintain a New Zealand permanent establishment in order to avoid New Zealand transfer pricing and income tax obligations; however, if the IC has a New Zealand-resident director or an agency in New Zealand that habitually concludes contracts, a PE risk arises, requiring full transfer pricing documentation and income attribution.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand is not a jurisdiction that maintains formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the manner of the Caribbean/offshore ESR regimes enacted after the EU Code of Conduct Group. However, New Zealand's substantive tax law and the OECD's BEPS agenda impose anti-avoidance principles and general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) under section BG 1 Income Tax Act 2007, and the 'economic substance' concept appears in: (1) the offshore international company exemption from New Zealand taxing jurisdiction where the company is non-resident and has no fixed establishment in New Zealand, but if such company is centrally managed and controlled in New Zealand it becomes resident and fully taxable; (2) New Zealand's controlled foreign company (CFC) rules and foreign investment fund (FIF) rules; (3) thin capitalisation rules for residents and non-residents with excess debt; and (4) the substantial business presence exception for hybrid mismatch and permanent establishment attribution. For an International Company (IC) structured as an offshore PR communication firm, both the Cook Islands International Company regime (where the entity is likely resident) and New Zealand tax law require that the entity demonstrates real substantive presence in its claimed jurisdiction of tax residence, including physical office, employees who conduct core income-generating activities (signing client contracts, executing campaigns, handling media relationships), local discretion over fees and budgets, bank accounts, and adequate records. A pure 'mailbox' entity registered in the Cook Islands or other tax-neutral jurisdiction but administered from New Zealand would be deemed a New Zealand resident under case law applying the central management and control test, causing full New Zealand taxation on worldwide income and application of the same GAAR. The Cook Islands International Companies Act provides that an international company has no tax liability on international income and no requirement to maintain financial records locally, but this exemption does not override New Zealand's resident tax jurisdiction where management and control are exercised in New Zealand. The New Zealand IRD actively applies the 'creation of value' analysis to determine economic substance and has the power to assess a person as having a permanent establishment in New Zealand if there is a fixed place in New Zealand through which the business of the non-resident is wholly or partly carried on, or if an agent in New Zealand habitually exercises authority to conclude contracts in the name of the non-resident.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Avoidance Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, a tax shortfall arising from an avoidance arrangement is subject to a penalty of 20% (not reasonably arguable position) or 40% (dominant purpose of tax avoidance) of the shortfall. Evasion is subject to a 100% penalty. Late payment penalties and use-of-money interest accrue at up to 10% per month. Criminal prosecution can result in a fine of up to NZ$50,000 for a company and up to NZ$50,000 against an individual plus imprisonment of up to 5 years for tax evasion or knowingly providing false returns. For severe cases of fraud, the Crimes Act 1961 penalties apply. Inland Revenue also has the power to issue a 'notice to produce documents' and conduct dawn raids under judicial warrants.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for tax shortfalls where they are involved in the preparation of false documents or knowingly participate in evasion. The veil of incorporation may be pierced under the Companies Act 1993, the Tax Administration Act 1994, and the Financial Markets Conduct Act where the company is used as a sham or façade to conceal illegality. Directors may be liable for civil penalties equal to the amount of tax not withheld under the PAYE and RWT regimes (director penalties), plus interest and penalties. Travel restrictions may be imposed by the District Court at the request of Inland Revenue to prevent departure from New Zealand when taxes are outstanding; commercial or personal assets may be restrained or forfeited under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. Directors who breach their duties by allowing the company to trade while insolvent may face personal liability for unpaid tax and for reckless trading under the Company Act. Imprisonment applies where evasion is proven, and directors may be disqualified from managing companies for up to 5 years.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Reclassification of the offshore entity as a New Zealand tax resident or as a PE of the offshore entity results in a full assessment of New Zealand corporate tax at 28% on profits, plus NRWT on any deemed distributions. Penalties for an avoidance arrangement or evasion range from 20% to 100% of the shortfall, plus use-of-money interest. If a company is found to be a 'shell' for tax purposes, Inland Revenue may also impose non-filing penalties and administrative penalties of NZ$250 for each late return. More broadly, the entity could be struck off the New Zealand Companies Register if considered non-compliant, and if registered overseas as an IC, the relevant international company registry may impose fines for failure to file annual declarations of a paid registered agent (typically ranging from US$100 to US$1,000 depending on the jurisdiction).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly operate a shell company as a tax avoidance vehicle are joint and several liability with the company for the tax shortfall. Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, they can be made personally liable for penalties and interest. In criminal proceedings, directors face imprisonment of up to 5 years and fines of up to NZ$50,000. International travel by directors can be restricted through a departure prohibition order where tax is unpaid. Personal assets can be pursued in civil recovery, and directors may be liable under the Companies Act for allowing the business to carry on with the intent to defraud creditors (fraudulent trading) or reckless trading. The New Zealand Directors' duties under the Companies Act 1993 (sections 135-137) require care, diligence, and prudent business judgment; a director of a foreign IC managing operations from New Zealand cannot ignore liability by pleading lack of local director status, as the place of effective management is New Zealand.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / Financial Crime

Statutory sanction: Under the AML/CFT Act 2009, reporting entities failing to carry out CDD or KYC procedures are liable to civil penalties up to NZ$200,000 for an individual and NZ$2,000,000 for a company per contravention; significant contraventions may be up to NZ$400,000 and NZ$4,000,000 for companies. Money laundering itself is an offence under the Crimes Act 1961 (section 243), carrying a maximum sentence of 7 years' imprisonment for natural persons and unlimited fines for companies. Financing of terrorism is punishable by imprisonment of up to 14 years (Crimes Act 1961, sections 244-245 and Terrorism Suppression Act 2002). For arranging or facilitating transactions involving a designated terrorist entity, conviction can lead to 7 years to 14 years imprisonment. For shell companies controlling financial accounts, the court may order criminal forfeiture of the funds and assets involved, up to the full value, under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009; a restraint order or asset freezing order may be issued immediately. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) can suspend the entity's ability to transact through the New Zealand banking system.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers can be personally prosecuted for AML/CFT offences if they authorised, participated in, or failed to prevent the offending. They face imprisonment of up to 7 years for laundering and up to 14 years for terrorist financing; fines of up to NZ$2,000,000 per contravention for the entity and individual liability for compensation orders. Directors' and officers' insurance does not cover criminal conduct. A director of a non-resident IC with New Zealand management who is found guilty of money laundering also automatically faces seizure of personal assets, travel bans under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act, and prohibition from being a director of any New Zealand company or managed entity. New Zealand Police can issue a 'production order' requiring a corporate service provider to disclose the director's identity, and the director may be extradited depending on bilateral treaties between New Zealand and the IC's registry jurisdiction in the Cook Islands.
Transfer Pricing / Associated Enterprise Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may make a transfer pricing adjustment, imputing a commercially realistic arm's length profit to the New Zealand company. The adjusted taxable income is taxed at 28%, and a shortfall penalty of 20% applies where the taxpayer did not adopt a reasonably arguable position; 40% where the adjustment exceeds NZ$250,000 or the arrangement's dominant purpose is tax avoidance. Evasion adds a 100% penalty. Interest accrues from return due date. In addition, unpaid withholding tax on interest/royalties (NRWT 15% or 2% AIL) is assessed, plus penalties of 10% per month. The dividend may be recharacterised as a dividend, giving rise to NRWT of 30% or applicable treaty rate. The CbCR and Country-by-Country reporting notification failure can result in penalties of NZ$50,000 per entity per year plus NZ$10,000 per month continued.

Director personal liability: A director who signs incorrect transfer pricing documentation or assists in creating false intercompany agreements is personally exposed to civil penalties for aiding and abetting tax evasion. Personal liability for a company's tax debt may arise under the 'director penalty' regime for unpaid PAYE/RWT, and for other taxes where reckless behaviour is proven. Courts may pierce the corporate veil to recover unpaid tax where there is evidence of fraud, irregularity, or the company being an instrument of its director. Indictment for tax evasion committed as an associated enterprise could result in imprisonment. Travel restrictions are available where tax is unpaid and a director attempts to leave New Zealand.
Breach of Statutory Return Obligations / Non-Compliance with IC Registry Filings

Statutory sanction: In New Zealand: late filing penalty of NZ$250 per return, automatic late payment penalty of 1% plus 2% additional penalty, incremental monthly penalties up to 10%, use-of-money interest. Failure to provide requested documents can draw penalties of NZ$2,000 per month per document under the Tax Administration Act, up to NZ$50,000. In the Cook Islands: annual company licensing fees are assessed for late penalty, and a company in default of its annual license return is subject to a fine of up to NZ$500 plus the loss of its international company status; continued default may result in strike-off and the company's assets vesting in the Cook Islands government. However, the New Zealand-controlled 'IC' structure may face a reclassification as a New Zealand resident if it is not actually managed and controlled in the Cook Islands, and any representation that the entity is 'tax exempt' while operating from New Zealand is fraudulent.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for filing fees and penalties in the Cook Islands, and the Cook Islands Financial Intelligence Unit can impose AML penalties. In New Zealand, a director who fails to ensure timely filing of tax returns is subject to director penalty assessments under the Tax Administration Act, equal to the unpaid tax amount plus interest. A director who permits a company to be struck off while outstanding tax liabilities exist can be personally liable for unpaid debts. Under the Companies Act, a director who fails to comply with record-keeping obligations commits an offence, punishable by a fine of up to NZ$200,000, and an individual can be disqualified. Travel bans may be issued under Inland Revenue's Tax Recovery enforcement, potentially for unlimited duration until tax payable is settled, as well as a court-ordered sale of assets.
