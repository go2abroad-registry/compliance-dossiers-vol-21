Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

For an International Company (tax-exempt zone, non-resident owned) incorporated under the standard New Zealand Companies Act regime: no minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by statute; capital may be zero or nominal. No escrow account is required at incorporation. If the company will hold an FMA financial services licence or rely on factoring-related licensing exemptions, the Registrar may require evidence of solvency or a minimum net tangible equity, typically NZD 50,000 (approx. USD 30,500) for financial services licence applicants; funds must be held in a New Zealand bank account or as otherwise directed by the Financial Markets Authority. For ordinary factoring/credit under trade receivables, no statutory paid-up capital escrow applies.

Funding & unlock path: Fire Safety and Building Compliance: If the company occupies an office, a Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) issued under the Building Act 2004 must be maintained by the building owner and displayed; the company must conduct a fire evacuation scheme approved by Fire and Emergency New Zealand (FENZ) if the premises are a workplace with more than 10 employees or high-risk. Occupational Health and Safety: Under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, the company must (a) appoint a competent health and safety officer, (b) issue a written health and safety policy, (c) perform hazard identification and risk assessments, (d) provide first-aid kits and trained first-aiders based on headcount, and (e) obtain a WorkplaceSafe New Zealand clearance / worker engagement and participation training if operating from registered commercial premises. Structural load inspections are not required for office use without heavy machinery. Civil defence: prepare a business continuity / civil defense plan (earthquake/storm) and maintain worker protection supplies as recommended by the National Emergency Management Agency. Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) / Te Tari Taake — New Zealand's national tax administration; taxpayer self-service portal: myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz/myir).

E-government filing portal

A foreign (non-resident) entity must obtain a New Zealand IRD number by filing Form IR742 (Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application) with Inland Revenue, accompanied by certified copy of the certificate of incorporation, registered office details, and director/shareholder particulars. For a Cook Islands International Company (CK_IC), the entity is typically non-resident for New Zealand income tax and requires an IRD number only if it derives New Zealand-source income, carries on business in New Zealand through a fixed establishment or permanent establishment, or registers for GST. The application may be filed by a New Zealand tax agent via myIR; Inland Revenue issues the 8-9 digit IRD number within 5-10 working days following verification. Where New Zealand-source withholding income is anticipated, the company should simultaneously register for non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone — a Cook Islands International Company is an offshore entity holding statutorily exempt status under the Cook Islands International Companies Act 1981-82; for New Zealand domestic tax purposes it is treated as a non-resident foreign company subject to entity-level taxation only on New Zealand-source income, with no New Zealand CFC or attribution exposure while outside the controlled-foreign-company perimeter unless controlled by New Zealand residents.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate U.S.-style check-the-box elections. Available statutory mechanisms include: (i) GST grouping election under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 s. 55 to treat associated companies as a single GST group; (ii) loss grouping election under Subpart IC of the Income Tax Act 2007 permitting offset of tax losses between commonly owned companies; (iii) hybrid mismatch adjustment elections under Part FM aligned with OECD hybrid rules to neutralize deductible/non-inclusion mismatches; (iv) NRWT tax treaty election to reduce domestic withholding rates; and (v) for non-resident companies, an election to be treated as carrying on business through a New Zealand branch where applicable.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period. Factoring, invoice discounting, and other receivables-financing intermediation services are exempt financial services under the First Schedule of the GST Act 1985, thereby excluded from the taxable-supply threshold calculation. A non-resident cross-border factoring platform supplying services to New Zealand GST-registered businesses with no New Zealand fixed establishment generally has no New Zealand GST registration obligation; registration may be required only for remote services supplied to New Zealand resident consumers (B2C) under the remote-services rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods: zero-rated when exported from New Zealand under s. 11(1)(a) of the GST Act 1985, provided the supplier obtains and retains credible evidence of export (customs export declaration, bill of lading or airway bill, insurance and freight documentation) within 28 days of supply and the goods leave New Zealand within 12 months. For services: zero-rated under s. 11A where supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed and the services do not relate to New Zealand land or moveable goods situated in New Zealand. Factoring: invoice financing and factoring are exempt financial services rather than zero-rated supplies; consequently the supply is GST-free without input-credit entitlement. Cross-border factoring of receivables originated from non-resident sellers with no New Zealand nexus falls outside the New Zealand GST net entirely and requires no zero-rating claim.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Electronic CUS/BAC lodged via Trade Single Window)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical departure of goods from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT/MT103 evidence proving receipt of export consideration
  • Duly executed factoring agreement and assignment of receivables (for cross-border trade receivables)
  • Commercial invoice and packing list evidencing the underlying exported goods

New Zealand imposes no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no tax-authority API e-invoicing clearance. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and follows the Peppol PINT interoperability framework (e-Invoicing). GST returns and payments are filed electronically via myIR; transactional data is reported on a summary basis rather than transaction-by-transaction. For exported goods, the mandatory digital filing is the Customs Export Declaration lodged through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window (CUS/BAC electronic declaration).

Inland Revenue may reassess GST zero-rating positions within 4 years for a tax return with acceptable disclosure and within 8 years where the return was not provided, was materially incomplete, or involved fraudulent or evasive conduct (Tax Administration Act 1994 s. 108). Unsupported zero-rating attracts reinstatement of the 15% GST plus use-of-money interest, a shortfall penalty of 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, escalating to 100-150% for evasion (ss. 141A-141D TAA 1994), and for invoicing or refund fraud, a criminal fine of up to NZD 250,000 for a company and/or imprisonment of up to 5 years. Exporters are also subject to clawback of input credits previously claimed on exported goods or services if export evidence is later discredited or the goods are re-imported or diverted to domestic consumption.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand's Foreign Exchange Control regime is fully liberalised; there are no exchange controls applicable to current account transactions, capital account transactions, profit remittances, or dividend distributions by non-resident-owned entities. The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is freely convertible onshore and offshore, and non-resident investors may repatriate capital, dividends, interest, and factoring proceeds without prior exchange control approval. Practical hurdles are limited to bank-level anti-money laundering (AML) / know-your-customer (KYC) documentation for the beneficial owner of the International Company, tax residency/withholding obligations for outbound interest and dividends, and the residual requirement to ensure any suspense or settlement accounts are not used to circumvent sanctions screening or anti-money laundering filters.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement in New Zealand for export proceeds to be surrendered to the RBNZ or converted into NZD. Exporters and factoring entities may retain foreign currency proceeds in external accounts indefinitely, and there is no mandatory conversion ratio or repatriation deadline. For trade-supply-chain factoring structures, the only applicable compliance touchpoints are New Zealand's anti-money laundering reporting obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT) Act 2009, which require reporting of suspicious transactions and prescribed transactions of NZD 10,000 or more, rather than any FX surrender mandate.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-High for the International Company (CK_IC) conducting cross-border trade receivables factoring, subject to strict structural constraints. New Zealand does not issue FinTech electronic money institution (EMI) licences equivalent to the EU EMI passport; non-bank payment providers must register as 'financial service providers' under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and, if they hold or control customer funds, be licensed under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 or operate through a registered bank. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g. UK or Lithuanian EMIs) may be viable as ancillary collection/settlement rails, but they cannot provide a New Zealand bank-issued escrow account, cannot execute SWIFT MT103 with a New Zealand BIC in a fully NZD-clearing manner without an underlying NZ correspondent bank, and may trigger regulated credit-activity implications because factoring and reverse factoring constitute a financial service that requires registration and an approved dispute resolution scheme under the Financial Service Providers Act. The recommended approach is to use the Offshore EMI solely for non-NZD collection in jurisdictions where the obligor is resident, while maintaining a core NZD operational account with a Tier 1 New Zealand registered bank.

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) applies to dividends paid by a New Zealand resident company to a non-resident shareholder. The statutory rate is 15% for fully imputed dividends where the company has attached imputation credits on an imputation account balance sufficient to fully impute the dividend, and 30% on non-imputed dividends and for dividends declared from retained earnings with no imputation credit. The standard NRWT rate under the Income Tax Act 2007 and the NRWT rules is 15% for fully imputed dividends paid to a resident of a treaty country or under an approved issuer levy (AIL) regime, but 30% is the default for unfranked/non-imputed dividends unless a lower treaty rate applies. Under the New Zealand–Hong Kong DTA (which would govern a CK_IC International Company with a Hong Kong/offshore parent), the dividend withholding tax rate may be reduced to 0% or 5% depending on the level of shareholding, but note the DTA does not apply unless the recipient is the beneficial owner and is a resident of the other contracting state. For a domestic New Zealand entity structured as an International Company with ultimate non-resident ownership, dividend repatriation to the foreign parent falls under NRWT, unless the portfolio investment entity or supplementary dividend regime applies (not available for straight factoring).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand has no mandatory statutory thresholds that expressly require Local File or Master File on the OECD-aligned basis for all taxpayers; transfer pricing documentation must be prepared and retained by taxpayers who have international related party dealings, applying the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines. However, the Inland Revenue's Administrative Guidelines (and the OECD's Country-by-Country (CbC) Reporting rules adopted in New Zealand) trigger mandatory CbC documentation for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of NZ$1.125 billion or more (including non-resident groups operating in New Zealand). For local file/master file purposes, the Commissioner can request documentation under section 9 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, and the taxpayer must provide it within a reasonable time. The practical safe harbor threshold used by the IRD to reduce TP compliance burdens is NZ$3 million in aggregate non-risk-related cross-border related party transactions (or $15 million for low-risk routine financing transactions) for a simplified contemporaneous documentation requirement, but this is administrative guidance rather than a statutory threshold. For a trade supply chain factoring IC, if it enters into cross-border factoring transactions with related parties (e.g., purchases or discounts receivables from a foreign affiliate), it must comply with Part 4A of the Income Tax Act 2007 (transfer pricing rules) regardless of size.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a separate 'Economic Substance Regulations' (ESR) statute equivalent to the UAE or Crown Dependencies; instead, the Inland Revenue applies the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG 1 and sections GA 1, GB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, plus the transfer pricing rules, the non-resident company attribution rules, and the DTA limitation-of-benefits provisions. A factoring International Company must demonstrate real economic substance in New Zealand: substantive decision-making, employees, premises, and operational functions must be present if it claims New Zealand residence benefits (e.g., treaty access). If the entity has no commercial substance and is used solely to avoid tax or obtain tax benefits, the Commissioner may reconstruct the arrangement under the GAAR or apply the OECD/G20 BEPS multilateral instrument (MLI) principal purpose test. For New Zealand purposes, the 'economic substance doctrine' is effectively codified in the business continuity and residency tests, and the IRD may disregard a shell entity under the general anti-avoidance provisions. The Companies Act 1993 also allows the court to pierce the corporate veil for sham or facade arrangements.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Tax Avoidance (GAAR)

Statutory sanction: Under section 141 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, evasion or similar offence carries a penalty of up to 100% of the resulting tax shortfall (or 150% for abusive tax positions under section 141D). Criminal prosecution under section 143A can impose a maximum fine of NZ$50,000 for individuals and NZ$250,000 for companies, and imprisonment for up to 5 years for individuals. The Commissioner may also issue a default assessment to the maximum amount, cancel tax losses, and deny deductions. For failure to keep adequate tax records, a penalty of up to NZ$20,000 may apply under section 139AA, and daily penalties of NZ$500 can be imposed for failure to supply information under Section 19 or 21.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable under the Companies Act 1993 for allowing the company to trade recklessly or incur obligations without reasonable grounds (section 135), and for the company's tax debts if they signed a declaration of solvency or caused the company to avoid tax. The Tax Administration Act 1994 (section 163A) provides that directors and officers can be jointly and severally liable for certain PAYE, GST, RWT, and NRWT amounts withheld from others but not remitted. Under section 143A, directors of a company convicted of tax evasion may be imprisoned for up to 5 years. Civil penalties can include repayment of tax shortfalls plus interest under section 12 of the Interest on Tax (IOT) provisions (currently 7% per annum initially, adjustable quarterly). The Commissioner may also obtain a civil judgment and bankruptcy or liquidation orders; directors may face travel bans under the Summary Proceedings Act 1957 only after court-ordered examination or where they have failed to comply with an IRD investigation summons, and exit orders are available under the Tax Administration Act 1994 for deliberate tax defaulters with outstanding debt exceeding NZ$10,000.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under sections GC 6–GC 14 and Part 4A of the Income Tax Act 2007, the Commissioner may adjust the taxpayer's transfer price to the arm's length amount, increasing taxable income and imposing shortfall penalties. The shortfall penalty for not taking reasonable care is 20% (section 141B); for unacceptable tax positions, 20%; for abusive tax positions, 50%; and for evasion or fraudulent intent, 100% (section 141E). The IRD may also impose late payment penalties and use-of-money interest on the increased tax assessment. Failure to provide documentation may result in an adverse inference and higher penalties because the taxpayer cannot demonstrate reasonableness.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in preparing false transfer pricing documentation or causing the company to file an inaccurate tax return may be personally liable for penalties as an aider and abettor under sections 141 and 144 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. If the company is liquidated, directors can be personally liable for tax debts under section 165 of the Companies Act 1993 (reckless trading) or section 300 for debts incurred while insolvent. Overseas travel bans can be imposed by the High Court for failure to comply with discovery orders, and directors may be disqualified from managing companies for up to 5 years under section 383 of the Companies Act 1993.
AML/CFT and Regulatory Breach (Factoring as Money Remittance / Lending)

Statutory sanction: Under the AML/CFT Act 2009, a financial institution (including a factoring entity that provides financial services by lending or providing financial accommodation) that fails to register or comply with its AML/CFT obligations may be subject to civil liability of up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate per contravention (section 89), and directors/officers can be fined up to NZ$300,000 (section 90). Failure to report suspicious transactions is a criminal offence with a maximum penalty of 2 years' imprisonment and a fine of NZ$10,000 (section 90A), and breaches of the identity verification and record-keeping requirements attract civil penalties up to NZ$100,000. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and Department of Internal Affairs supervise enforcement, and a contravention may also constitute a criminal offence under the Financial Transactions Reporting Act. The FSP Act requires registration on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) for providing factoring services; unregistered provision of financial services can lead to a compliance order from the FMA or a fine of up to NZ$500,000. The Commerce Commission can also levy penalties for misleading conduct in trade finance promotional statements under the Fair Trading Act 1986: up to NZ$200,000 for individuals and NZ$600,000 for companies per breach.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for AML/CFT civil penalties under section 90 AML/CFT Act if they knew or ought reasonably to have known that the company was breaching its obligations, and criminal liability with imprisonment up to 2 years applies for knowingly providing misleading information. Under the FSP Act, directors who provide unregistered financial services commit an offence and may be personally fined up to NZ$300,000. If the company is used as a shell to move funds without appropriate AML controls, directors may additionally be charged with money laundering offences under the Crimes Act 1961 (sections 243–245), which carry a maximum penalty of 7 years' imprisonment; the criminal proceeds can be restrained or forfeited under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. Travel bans may be imposed under the Bail Act and criminal justice procedures; the High Court may order surrender of passports in relation to extradition or proceeds-of-crime investigations.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance (Corporate Veil Piercing)

Statutory sanction: The Companies Act 1993 allows the court to pierce the corporate veil in cases of fraud, sham, or where the company is an alter ego. The Inland Revenue may reconstruct the arrangement under the general anti-avoidance provision (section BG 1) and section GB 1, treating the income as derived by the controlling persons. The New Zealand tax law also contains the 'branch equivalent' and 'controlled foreign company' (CFC) attribution rules (sections EX 1–EX 24B) that can attribute the passive or factoring income of a controlled non-resident company to New Zealand residents. The penalty for a company being a facade is the cancellation of tax benefits and assessment of tax as if the arrangement had not occurred; the Commissioner can issue assessments on a reconstruction basis, even on a gross basis. A finding of sham exposes the IC to an interlocutory declaration that the entity has no legal existence for tax purposes, and all income becomes taxable in the hands of the beneficial owner, with potential penalties up to 150% under the abusive tax position rules.

Director personal liability: Directors of a sham/shell IC face personal liability at common law for misrepresentation, and under sections 135, 137, and 301 of the Companies Act 1993 can be held liable to repay company debts or compensate the company for losses caused by reckless or fraudulent conduct. Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, directors can be held personally liable in respect of unpaid tax where the company's tax avoidance arrangement is deemed a sham and the director was knowingly party to it. Civil penalties cannot be discharged by corporate liquidation. Travel restrictions apply: the High Court may, on application of the IRD or the Serious Fraud Office, make a travel ban order under section 55 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 against a director who is a New Zealand tax defaulter, or under the Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters Act 1992 if extradition proceedings arise from tax evasion charges (maximum imprisonment for tax evasion is 5 years; for serious fraud under the Crimes Act it is 7 years). Directors may also be disqualified from acting as directors for up to 10 years under the Companies Act 1993 in connection with their management of a company that is deregistered for tax offences.
