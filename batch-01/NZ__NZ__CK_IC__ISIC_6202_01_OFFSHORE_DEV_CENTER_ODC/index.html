Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD / Te Tari Tauhake) of New Zealand; official portal: myIR at https://www.ird.govt.nz/myir/ (operated under the Tax Administration Act 1994 and Goods and Services Tax Act 1985)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity in the Offshore Dev Center (ODC) model must first obtain a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) via the Companies Office if it incorporates locally; alternatively, a non-resident company must file Form IR596 (Application for an IRD Number for a Non-Resident Company) with Inland Revenue. Required attachments include the certificate of incorporation or equivalent foreign registry extract, full entity chart and director/shareholder details, overseas tax identification numbers, and a statement of New Zealand business activities and expected income sourcing. The IRD issues a unique IRD number (8 or 9 digits) for income tax, and the same number is used for GST registration. For a tax-exempt international company with no New Zealand source income, the IRD number is obtained primarily for administrative, banking, and withholding-treaty purposes, not for routine NZ income tax filing. Non-residents may register for GST through the MyIR portal by filing form GST1, providing the IRD number, business contact details, bank account information, and evidence of offshore trading intention.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone: The CK_IC structure is characterized as a non-resident International Company that, under New Zealand's source-based taxation regime, is taxable only on New Zealand-sourced income. Foreign-sourced revenue derived from the ODC's offshore software engineering and maintenance services supplied to non-resident clients falls outside the New Zealand tax net. Consequently, the default classification reflects territorial exemption for offshore business profits, making the entity effectively a tax-exempt zone in New Zealand for non-NZ source income, while remaining subject to GST and economic substance disclosure expectations.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style 'check-the-box' election; however, the following statutory elections and mechanisms are available or relevant for a non-resident International Company: (1) Voluntary GST registration election under Section 51(1)(c) of the GST Act 1985, allowing the ODC to register for GST even where the NZD 60,000 threshold has not been reached, in order to recover input credits on local hosting, cloud, and subcontractor costs; (2) Election to treat a qualifying entity as a Look-Through Company (LTC) under the Income Tax Act 2007, though this is generally unavailable to non-resident-controlled offshore companies and is listed only for completeness; (3) Hybrid and branch mismatch elections under Part FM of the Income Tax Act 2007, which the International Company must use to neutralize any deductible hybrid mismatches in cross-border arrangements, particularly with parent entities in trust or zero-tax jurisdictions; (4) Election under the cross-border worker and foreign employment income provisions where relevant; (5) Option to apply for a binding rulings regime (Section 91 of the Tax Administration Act 1994) to obtain irrevocable confirmation of zero-rating and non-resident source treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 in cumulative taxable supplies over any 12-month period; voluntary registration permitted below threshold if the supplier carries on a taxable activity (Section 51(1)(c) of the GST Act 1985). A non-resident International Company that makes only zero-rated offshore services may still be eligible to register for GST to facilitate efficiency of input tax recovery, but must meet the threshold or opt-in test.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The ODC may zero-rate its offshore software engineering and maintenance services under Section 11A of the GST Act 1985 when the services are supplied to a person who is not resident in New Zealand and who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, provided the services are not physically supplied to another person within New Zealand and are not directly in connection with New Zealand land or tangible personal property located in New Zealand. Software development, data processing, maintenance, and remote project management services delivered from a New Zealand-based ODC to a foreign parent, trust, or end-client typically qualify as zero-rated 'export of services' where the contractual counterparty is offshore and risk and benefit are located offshore. Alternatively, Section 11(1)(c) can apply for services supplied in connection with goods or land situated outside New Zealand. The zero-rating is conditional on bona fide offshore consumption and cannot be used for services enjoyed or utilized within New Zealand.

  • GST tax invoice in compliance with Section 24 of the GST Act 1985, bearing a clear 'Zero Rated Export Service under Section 11A' notation and the client's offshore tax/company identifier

New Zealand does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance regime, digital stamping, or API-based tax authority pre-clearance for e-invoices. Instead, New Zealand operates a voluntary national PEPPOL-based e-invoicing network (the Digital Business Network) aligned with the NZ Government's 'e-invoicing now' initiative; businesses may issue PEPPOL 3.0 BIS or UBL invoices voluntarily. For GST purposes, all invoices (electronic or paper) must satisfy the mandatory content requirements of Section 24 of the GST Act 1985, including seller name and address, seller GST registration number, invoice issue date, description of services, consideration, and the GST amount (or a clear zero-rated indication). No pre-issuance fiscal approval or real-time IRD clearance is required; compliance is enforced through retroactive audit and data-matching, with IRD empowered to issue information-gathering notices under Section 17 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Standard non-PEPPOL e-invoices (e.g., PDF, email, software-generated) remain valid if they meet Section 24 statutory content.

IRD scrutinizes zero-rating claims through transfer pricing and source audits under Section 108 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Where zero-rating is improperly claimed, IRD may issue a default assessment reinstating 15% GST output tax on the full invoice consideration, together with recovery of any input credits previously claimed, imposition of shortfall penalties scaled by culpability — 20% for not taking reasonable care (Section 141A), 40% for gross carelessness (Section 141B), 100% for an abusive tax position (Section 141D), and 150% for evasion or fraudulent intent (Section 141E) — plus use-of-money interest under Section 120 from the original due date. Criminal prosecution for GST fraud under Section 143A can result in fines up to NZD 50,000 per offense and imprisonment, and repeat offenders face enhanced penalties. IRD also enforces the 'unjust enrichment' doctrine to claw back GST refunds where the supplier cannot prove that the export services were actually consumed offshore, with a documentary retention period of seven years under Section 50 of the Tax Administration Act 1994; failure to maintain the mandatory documentation bundle within the 180-day FX repatriation window triggers a rebuttable presumption of intra-NZ supply.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under IMF Article VIII status. There are no general exchange controls, no restrictions on the purchase or remittance of foreign currency, and no requirement for central bank approval for cross-border equity injections, dividend distributions, or trade-related payments. Profit remittance is free from FX-control constraints; however, outbound dividends and interest remain within the tax compliance perimeter of New Zealand law, including non-resident withholding tax and the general anti-avoidance rules. Banking practitioners will still require source-of-funds evidence and a clear economic purpose to satisfy the AML/CFT Act 2009, but this is a financial crime compliance process rather than an FX convertibility restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand does not impose statutory surrender requirements, mandatory conversion timelines, or minimum conversion ratios for export receipts. Export proceeds may be retained indefinitely in foreign currency or held in New Zealand or offshore bank accounts. There is no obligation to repatriate receipts within a prescribed period. Nevertheless, any retained foreign-currency balances must be properly reflected in the corporate tax accounts, and realised or unrealised foreign-exchange movements may create taxable or deductible outcomes under general New Zealand revenue law. For transfer pricing and thin-capitalisation purposes, the invoicing entity must document the arm's-length nature of intercompany software-development payments and the commercial substance of the ODC cash flows.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand - Te Pūtea Matua (RBNZ)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-High as a supporting cross-border collection and payout rail, but Low as the primary treasury depository. Offshore digital EMI solutions such as Wise B2B, Payoneer, and Airwallex may be used for receivables collection, contractor disbursements, and small-ticket vendor payments, subject to each provider's own AML/KYC onboarding and sanctions-screening policies. The material legal constraint is that these offshore digital EMIs are not New Zealand registered banks and therefore do not hold call accounts as protected New Zealand deposits; funds are typically pooled in safeguarding accounts at tier-1 financial institutions and may lack the same creditor protection and regulatory supervision as a direct RBNZ-licensed bank deposit. Corporate treasury policy should therefore restrict EMI balances to working-capital float, maintain direct bank equivalency through at least one tier-1 New Zealand bank, and ensure all material settlement flows are reconcilable to SWIFT MT103 or new-generation instant payment rails.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound equity injection should be executed by SWIFT MT103 from the source jurisdiction to the New Zealand operating bank account, with the beneficiary bank identified by BIC, the account number fully stated, and the payment memo expressly reading 'equity contribution to share capital', 'paid-up capital', or 'shareholder loan' as objectively accurate. Where the corporate structure uses an International Company in New Zealand, the receiving bank will require corporate authority, source of wealth, and ultimate beneficial-ownership evidence. No prior RBNZ FX approval is required because there is no exchange-control regime. The standard OECD balance-of-payments purpose classification for the originating institution should be captured as direct investment in equity capital, and the receiving bank should be notified in advance with a covering corporate resolution to reduce suspicion and undue hold-up. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% (Income Tax Act 2007, Pt A of Schedule 1)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% domestic NRWT on non-imputed or partially-imputed dividends paid by a NZ company to a non-resident shareholder; 0% NRWT on fully imputed dividends (the NZ corporate tax borne at 28% is the final tax); most NZ DTAs reduce the NRWT rate on non-imputed dividends to 15%. The approved issuer levy is not available for dividends. Supplementary dividends may apply under specific DTA arrangements (notably Australia).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

For a standard 31 March balance date, the IR4 is due by 31 March of the year following the balance date (12 months after year end). Terminal tax for a 31 March balance date is due 7 February (non-GST registered), 15 January (GST registered on a quarterly basis), or 7 April (GST registered with an early balance date); provisional tax fall due in three instalments (28 August, 15 January and 7 May).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: No NZD-denominated statutory Local File/Master File trigger in dollars: Country-by-Country reporting and notification are mandatory for each NZ constituent entity of a multinational group whose consolidated annual revenue equals or exceeds EUR 750 million (approximately NZD 1.3 billion). Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is expected by Inland Revenue whenever the annual aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions exceeds NZD 5 million, and Inland Revenue's compliance focus automatically attaches to 'significant enterprises' (turnover above approximately NZD 100 million).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulation statute (unlike the UAE or Bermuda). Substance is instead enforced through: (i) corporate tax residence under s YD 2 (incorporation in NZ alone establishes residence and worldwide taxation); (ii) the general anti-avoidance rule in s BG 1; (iii) the transfer pricing recharacterization provisions in Part 4A; and (iv) the 49% continuity test for loss carry-forward in Part IA. A NZ-incorporated shell or a company claiming a non-existent 'tax-exempt zone' status enjoys no exemption: it remains assessable at 28% on worldwide income, and claims to the contrary are void and treated as abusive tax avoidance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Manipulation (Offshore Development Center)

Statutory sanction: IR adjustment substituting an arm's-length consideration under the Income Tax Act 2007, Part 4A; shortfall penalty under s 141: 20% if lack of reasonable care, 40% for an unacceptable tax position, 150% for evasion; interest on underpaid tax. Unpaid principal debt of NZD 15,000+ may trigger a Departure Prohibition Order.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false IR4/IR10 returns with knowledge of non-arm's-length pricing face criminal liability as parties to tax evasion (Crimes Act 1961 s 252, imprisonment up to 15 years and/or fine). On insolvency of the NZ company caused by profit stripping, the Companies Act 1993 s 135 (reckless trading) allows liquidators and IRD to recover the shortfall from directors personally (civil restitution) and obtain disqualification for up to 5 years under s 383.
Tax Evasion & False R&D Credit Claims

Statutory sanction: Tax Administration Act 1994 s 143A: evasion-level shortfall penalty of 150% of the tax shortfall; late payment penalties and interest; criminal conviction under Crimes Act 1961 s 252 ('Every person who makes a false or misleading return... with intent to evade tax') with a maximum term of imprisonment of 15 years; confiscation of proceeds of crime under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

Director personal liability: Directors as principals or accessories face personal indictment, imprisonment up to 15 years, unlimited fines in the High Court, travel bans through departure prohibition (tax debt above NZD 15,000), and disqualification as a company director (Companies Act s 382). Civil restitution is recoverable against the director's personal assets for the tax shortfall attributable to their knowledge.
Shell Company / Sham 'Tax-Exempt Zone' Status

Statutory sanction: The tax-exempt classification is void ab initio; IRD assesses the company on worldwide income at 28% and may reconstruct the arrangement under s BG 1 (avoidance), with shortfall penalties up to 150%, and may disregard the separate existence of the shell in appropriate sham cases; NRWT at 30%/15% is assessed on any payments recharacterized as dividends.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced under the Companies Act 1993 ss 135–136 for recklessly allowing the company to trade while tax liabilities accrued; directors are jointly and severally liable for the company's unpaid tax to the extent of the loss caused; departure prohibition orders and criminal liability for aiding a sham arrangement (up to 15 years' imprisonment for evasion) apply.
