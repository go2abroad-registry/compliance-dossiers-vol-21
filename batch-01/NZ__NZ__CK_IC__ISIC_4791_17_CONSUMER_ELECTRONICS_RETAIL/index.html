Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Under section 180 of the Companies Act 1993, a New Zealand registered office must be a physical address, not a post office box; however, a virtual office service that provides a physical street address and a dedicated mail handling service may satisfy the registered office requirement. Despite this, Inland Revenue and major New Zealand banks (e.g., ANZ, ASB, Westpac) objectively assess commercial substance and are likely to reject a pure virtual office for an e-commerce company due to low operational substance and potential VAT/goods and services tax evasion. A serviced executive office with a physical meeting room, a registered agent, and documented records is strongly recommended to maintain the low AML risk tier and pass bank commercial substance due diligence.

Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Determine tax residence. A company is New Zealand-resident if incorporated in New Zealand or if its centre of strategic management and control is exercised in New Zealand; a non-resident International Company (IC) is taxable only on New Zealand-sourced income. Step 2: Apply for an IRD number. For a non-resident/foreign company, submit paper form IR742 (Request for a New Zealand IRD Number for a Non-Resident Company) together with certified copies of the certificate of incorporation (or foreign equivalent), constitutional documents, director and shareholder identification, registered office address, and evidence of the New Zealand business relationship; alternatively, instruct a registered New Zealand tax agent to file the application. The IRD number serves as the single statutory identifier for both income tax and GST. Step 3: Register for GST. Step 4: Obtain a New Zealand Customs exporter identifier. Register with the New Zealand Customs Service to obtain a customs client code / exporter code for the physical export of consumer electronics hardware. Step 5: Complete merchant/payment compliance evidence for Stripe and PayPal business accounts, linking the entity name, IRD number, and bank records so that export zero-rating and foreign-currency proceeds can later be substantiated.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime, but the following statutory elections and structural mechanisms are available: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under the Income Tax Act 2007 subpart HB, providing full pass-through taxation to shareholders; (2) grandfathered Qualifying Company election; (3) imputation group election permitting a single imputation credit account across a 100% common ownership group; (4) GST grouping election under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 s 55; (5) CFC grey-list exemption for companies resident in Australia, the United Kingdom, the United States, and other listed jurisdictions; (6) hybrid mismatch neutralisation elections under the Income Tax Act 2007 ss FH 1-FH 15 to prevent double non-taxation; and (7) an election or factual position to maintain non-residence where central management and control remain offshore. For the custom International Company model, the entity is structured as a non-resident-owned vehicle with no onshore permanent establishment, placing its foreign-sourced trading income outside the New Zealand source taxation net.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised, free-floating exchange rate regime with unrestricted convertibility of the New Zealand dollar (NZD) on both the current account and capital account. The country has accepted Article VIII obligations of the IMF Articles of Agreement, thereby warranting no exchange controls on payments and transfers for current international transactions. Capital inflows, dividend repatriation, profit remittance, and loan repayments to non-residents are not subject to foreign exchange licensing or prior Reserve Bank approval. The sole regulatory gate is the Overseas Investment Act 2005, which requires consent for certain 'sensitive' land, fishing quota, or significant business assets above statutory thresholds; ordinary e-commerce working capital injections and trade disbursements do not trigger OIO screening. Withholding taxes (non-resident withholding tax on dividends and interest, and royalties) represent the only fiscal friction on profit remittance, and these can be reduced under New Zealand's double tax agreements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory obligation in New Zealand to surrender export proceeds to the central bank or to any authorised dealer, and no mandatory conversion ratio exists. Exporters may retain foreign currency earnings in domestic or offshore foreign-currency accounts indefinitely, and may hold, lend, invest, or convert such proceeds at their commercial discretion. This policy stems from the abolition of the Exchange Control Act 1939 and New Zealand's adherence to IMF Article VIII; no filing, reporting, or repatriation timeline applies to export receipts for balance-of-payments purposes, although the proceeds must be reflected in the entity's New Zealand financial statements and tax returns for income measurement and transfer pricing documentation.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) and the New Zealand Overseas Investment Office (OIO) under Land Information New Zealand for foreign investment screening; prudential regulation of registered banks is the sole statutory remit of the Reserve Bank of New Zealand pursuant to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the non-bank deposit taker framework.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium-to-High for licensed New Zealand Financial Service Providers (FSPs) registered on the Financial Service Providers Register and supervised under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act). Offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., UK- or Lithuania-licensed EMIs) that are not registered with the New Zealand FSP register cannot lawfully provide payment services to NZ-resident customers, and NZ-registered banks will generally refuse to settle on behalf of unregistered EMIs. Practical viability is HIGH for established cross-border EMIs holding an NZ FSP licence and a New Zealand AML/CFT compliance officer, such as Airwallex New Zealand or Wise (TransferWise NZ), which maintain local NZD rails and segregated trust accounts; viability is LOW for unlicensed pure-offshore EMIs that rely on passported EU licences without local registration, as the Reserve Bank and Financial Markets Authority treat unregistered payment facilitation as an unlicensed financial service.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant inbound capital injection into the New Zealand operational account should be executed via SWIFT MT103 from the Cook Islands International Company's offshore bank to the NZ bank, using field 70 (Remittance Information) with the purpose code 'CAPIN' (capital injection) or 'INVCAP' (investment capital) and clear reference to the shareholder loan or equity contribution. The wire must be preceded by a capital verification letter from the NZ onshore accountant confirming the injection is for working capital of the online electronics retail business and is not a suspicious deposit. Profit and principal repatriation back to the offshore parent should be routed via the same NZ bank's international banking facility using SWIFT MT103 with purpose code 'DIVR' (dividend remittance) or 'LOANR' (loan repayment), supported by board resolutions, tax withholding certificates (RWT/NRWT), and transfer pricing documentation to evidence arm's-length interest rates. All inbound wires must be matched against the bank's sanctioned list and the AML/CFT electronic verification checks, and any remittance exceeding NZD 10,000 is automatically flagged in the bank's transaction monitoring system; the entity should pre-file a Customer Due Diligence Addendum with the relationship manager to avoid the standard 24–48 hour hold.Trade disbursements for consumer electronics procurement should be settled over SWIFT MT103 for all non-documentary open-account supplier payments, with field 70 specifying the commercial invoice number, purchase order reference, and the harmonised system code. For shipments requiring documentary security, a Commercial Letter of Credit (LC) must be issued via SWIFT MT700 by the NZ issuing bank, advised and confirmed through the supplier's bank, typically with a 30–90 day usance period. For domestic NZD clearing of collections from Stripe and PayPal (which settle in NZD to the NZ bank account), the NZClear high-value payment system operated by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand provides real-time gross settlement through the ES+ exchange settlement account; low-value retail e-commerce refunds and card settlement runs on the standard ACH batch rails. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% for all companies on taxable income; non-resident companies are taxed only on New Zealand-sourced income attributable to a New Zealand permanent establishment (Income Tax Act 2007, Schedule 1, Part A, cl 1; ss. YD 4 and RS 2-4).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% is the New Zealand statutory non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) rate on dividends paid by a New Zealand-resident company to a non-resident company (ITA 2007 s. 210 and Schedule 14). Where the dividend is fully imputed, a further 'fully imputed dividend' rate of 15% applies in certain scenarios; however, the standard non-treaty rate is 30%.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a standard 31 March balance date, the IR4 return and terminal tax payment are due by 7 July following year-end (standard filing) or 31 March of the following income year where a tax agent has a valid extension of time; provisional tax is paid in three instalments on 28 August, 15 January and 7 May (for March balance dates). Non-standard balance dates shift these dates proportionally.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Relevant GST return must be filed and GST paid by the 28th day of the month following the end of the taxable period (e.g., the 28th of September for the July-August period). Non-resident sellers are bound by the same deadlines.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand dollar threshold: a Local File (contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation) is mandatory where the New Zealand taxpayer's cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZD 10 million in the income year, or where related-party interest-bearing loans exceed NZD 1 million; a Master File is required where the multinational group's consolidated global revenue is NZD 1.1 billion or more (TAA ss. 61A-61E and IRD Transfer Pricing Guidelines). Even below these thresholds, the arm's length standard applies and basic records must be kept.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply offshore Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as in the UAE or Cook Islands International Company regimes. Instead, the relevant doctrines are: (i) central management and control (ITA 2007 s. YD 5) - a foreign-incorporated company becomes a New Zealand tax resident if its head office or centre of strategic management is in New Zealand, making it subject to tax on worldwide income at 28%; (ii) the New Zealand permanent establishment threshold for income tax on NZ-source trading income; (iii) the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA 2007 s. BG 1) which permits the Commissioner to counteract tax avoidance arrangements lacking commercial substance; and (iv) the business continuity/loss-offset rules. A Cook Islands IC that is managed in the Cook Islands (licensed resident trustee, genuine board minutes, substantive commercial rationale) will not be a NZ tax resident, but a letterbox entity with management exercised in Auckland is exposed to full NZ tax residence and penalty exposure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion / intentional failure to file IR4

Statutory sanction: On conviction: fine up to NZD 100,000 for a company (NZD 50,000 for an individual) and/or imprisonment up to 5 years (TAA s. 143A(2)). Civil shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax shortfall apply (s. 141E), plus UOMI on the outstanding tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and shadow directors who knowingly procure the filing of false statements for a shell company can be prosecuted personally under TAA s. 143A. The Companies Act 1993 s. 301 permits the court to order restitution from directors of a company that made tax evasion payments; New Zealand tax debts can be the basis for a director penalty notice under TAA s. 149 for PAYE/GST debts, and the Commissioner can apply for an order preventing a director from leaving New Zealand (border alert while a tax debt exceeds NZD 10,000).
Shell company / artificial offshore structure

Statutory sanction: Under the centre-of-management rule (ITA 2007 s. YD 5), the company becomes a NZ tax resident and is assessable on worldwide income for all open years. The GAAR (s. BG 1) allows the Commissioner to cancel any tax advantage, with reconstruction powers. Shortfall penalties can reach 100% (dominant purpose) or 150% (evasion); prosecution under s. 143A may follow.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil may be pierced where the company is found to be a mere puppet: directors are personally liable for unpaid NZ tax under the Companies Act 1993 (reckless trading s. 135, dishonest use of position s. 136) and TAA director penalty notices; immigration/airport interdiction and asset restraint orders apply to directors named in the assessments; imprisonment up to 5 years may be imposed.
GST non-compliance for offshore e-commerce

Statutory sanction: Non-registration does not absolve the seller: Inland Revenue will assess un-collected GST, impose late payment penalties (1% initial + 4% additional + 1% per month), UOMI, and shortfall penalties of up to 150% (s. 141). For a company, the maximum evasion fine is NZD 100,000.

Director personal liability: Directors can be made personally liable under TAA s. 149 for GST debts created during their tenure, and may face director penalties, bankruptcy/prohibition from managing entities under the Companies Act, border alerts for unpaid tax, and criminal conviction where GST evasion is proven (imprisonment up to 5 years).
Transfer pricing mispricing / profit shifting

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable income to an arm's length amount under ITA 2007 s. GC 6; denial of interest deductions for excessive debt under 'thin capitalisation' rules (s. FE 2); shortfall penalties up to 100% (unacceptable tax position) or 150% (evasion) where documents are not maintained; documentation failure penalty under TAA s. 61C? The IRD can also impose a 'benchmark penalty' on those who knowingly participate.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve artificial TP schemes can be personally assessable for penalties as 'directors of a company' under TAA ss. 149 and 141A-141K; the court may order asset recovery and award damages in a liquidator's claim (s. 301 Companies Act); criminal prosecution for evasion applies if the arrangement is fraudulent.
NRWT evasion / repatriation stripping

Statutory sanction: NRWT is assessable on the true source income (dividend, royalty or interest) plus shortfall penalties of up to 150% and UOMI; the NZ payer is personally liable to the Commissioner for the NRWT not withheld under TAA s. 157; the GAAR will reverse the arrangement. For non-deductible royalty payments, the deduction is disallowed and tax under the 'non-resident contractor' regime may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors who caused the company to pay without withholding are jointly and severally liable to the NRWT shortfall; travel bans can be imposed by the Commissioner for a determination debt; directors can be prosecuted for 'aiding and abetting' tax evasion (Crimes Act 1961 s. 66 combined with TAA s. 143A).
