Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by New Zealand law for a private company. Shares may be issued as nil-paid, partly paid or fully paid. Where share capital is subscribed, the consideration must be paid into the company's bank account in accordance with the constitution and evidenced in the share register; there is no statutory escrow account requirement for non-public companies. The board must ensure that share premium and capital accounts are maintained in accordance with Part 4 of the Companies Act 1993 and the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 does not apply given the private, non-public character of the issuance.

Funding & unlock path: No civil defence or occupational clearance is required for a purely virtual e-commerce operation. In the event the company leases office, showroom or fulfilment space, the following clearances apply: (1) Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) and compliance schedule under the Building Act 2004 for buildings with specified systems; (2) fire evacuation scheme approval and annual fire safety checks under the Fire and Emergency New Zealand Act 2017; (3) structural load inspection and certificate of occupancy from the territorial authority; and (4) workplace health and safety clearance under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, including a hazard register, worker engagement documentation, welfare facilities, and first aid equipment.

Paid-in capital rule: No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed by New Zealand law for a private company. Shares may be issued as nil-paid, partly paid or fully paid. Where share capital is subscribed, the consideration must be paid into the company's bank account in accordance with the constitution and evidenced in the share register; there is no statutory escrow account requirement for non-public companies. The board must ensure that share premium and capital accounts are maintained in accordance with Part 4 of the Companies Act 1993 and the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 does not apply given the private, non-public character of the issuance.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Tauhake) is the sole competent national tax authority of New Zealand. All tax registrations, filings, and communications are administered through the 'myIR' secure online portal at https://www.ird.govt.nz/myir. Customs export clearance is separately administered by the New Zealand Customs Service (NZCS) through the Trade Single Window (TSW) electronic lodgement system.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Determine the need for a New Zealand IRD number. A non-resident International Company (IC) must obtain an IRD number if it carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand, derives New Zealand-source income, holds New Zealand assets, becomes a GST-registered person, or appoints a New Zealand agent. Step 2: Apply for an IRD number using Inland Revenue form IR596 (Non-Resident Company Application) or IR742, filed electronically or by post. The company must certify non-resident status and provide its registered office, director details, and any New Zealand agent. Step 3: Obtain a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) via the NZBN register, which is a prerequisite for electronic interaction with government agencies but does not replace the IRD number. Step 4: Register for GST under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 using the myIR portal (or via the same application) if the entity makes taxable supplies in New Zealand or supplies remote services or low-value imported goods to New Zealand consumers and expects turnover to exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period; voluntary registration is available. Step 5: Upon approval, Inland Revenue issues an IRD number (8-9 digits), which also serves as the GST registration number; the entity is assigned a filing frequency (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly) based on expected turnover. Step 6: For non-resident suppliers of remote services or low-value imported goods, use the simplified myIR non-resident supplier registration path and, where required, appoint a GST agent resident in New Zealand.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box entity classification election; entity classification is determined by statute under the Income Tax Act 2007 and the Companies Act 1993. A non-resident International Company is taxed at entity level and is not eligible for pass-through treatment. Available statutory elections and mechanisms relevant to this entity include: (1) voluntary GST registration under the GST Act 1985 to claim input tax credits before reaching the NZD 60,000 threshold; (2) GST group registration under sections 55-57 of the GST Act 1985 to consolidate related entities; (3) GST return frequency election (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly) under section 16 of the GST Act 1985; (4) appointment of a joint and several GST agent for non-resident suppliers; (5) hybrid mismatch elections under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018 to align deduction timing and mitigate BEPS adjustments; (6) non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) election under section 32 of the Income Tax Act 2007 to apply reduced treaty rates, or the Approved Issuer Levy (AIL) election for offshore debt; and (7) no flow-through or transparent election exists for ordinary international companies, so entity-level taxation remains the default.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (compulsory registration threshold over any 12-month period for domestic taxable supplies and for non-resident suppliers of remote services or low-value imported goods; the threshold is calculated on total taxable supplies including zero-rated supplies)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: Export supplies of musical instruments and studio audio gear are zero-rated under section 11 of the GST Act 1985 where the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand within 28 days of the supply (or within 60 days with Inland Revenue approval), the supplier retains ownership and possession until export, and the goods are exported by the supplier or the supplier's agent. Services: remotely supplied digital audio services and other services performed remotely are zero-rated under section 11A where the services are supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand when the services are performed and who does not use or consume the services in New Zealand; the supplier must hold sufficient evidence of the recipient's non-resident status and offshore location. Goods imported into New Zealand (low-value imported goods valued at or below NZD 1,000) from offshore sellers are instead subject to GST at the border under the Goods and Services Tax (Low-Value Imported Goods) Order 2019; 0% export rate applies only to genuine exports leaving New Zealand permanently.

  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical export from New Zealand
  • Commercial invoice and packing list matching the export entry and the GST zero-rated supply
  • Inward Remittance Certificate and/or bank account credit advice evidencing foreign exchange repatriation

New Zealand does not impose a real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory e-invoicing, or digital stamping regime for GST. E-invoicing is voluntary and operates on the Peppol network under the New Zealand e-Invoicing framework administered by the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE); adoption is encouraged for suppliers to government agencies. GST returns must be filed electronically via myIR by the 28th day (or 30th day for certain filers) following the end of the taxable period. There is no API-based pre-clearance or mandatory digital tax stamp; however, the taxpayer must retain full records and issue tax invoices satisfying section 24 of the GST Act 1985, and non-resident suppliers must keep evidence of the consumer's residence, the supply type, and the NZD conversion method used.

Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, incorrect or fraudulent zero-rating export claims expose the taxpayer to: (1) shortfall penalties from 20% (lack of reasonable care) to 150% (abusive tax avoidance or evasion), plus use-of-money interest (currently 8.97% for underpayments and 11.53% for overdues); (2) retrospective reassessment and full GST clawback where export evidence is insufficient or the goods are later diverted into the New Zealand market; Inland Revenue may assess missing GST for up to 7 years, or 10 years in cases of fraud or evasion; (3) late filing penalties of NZD 50 to NZD 250 per taxable period and late payment penalties of 1% immediately plus an additional 4% after 7 days; (4) criminal liability under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for evasion or knowingly providing false information, with fines up to NZD 50,000 for individuals or NZD 250,000 for companies and imprisonment of up to 5 years; (5) New Zealand Customs may seize exported goods unlawfully returned and impose penalty duty equal to the GST forgone plus an additional 10% of the goods' value; and (6) participation in cross-border fraud schemes may trigger promoter penalties of up to NZD 10,000 per offence and publication of taxpayer details by Inland Revenue.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar is freely convertible and the exchange control regime is fully liberalised: the Exchange Control Act 1953 was repealed and no residual RBNZ licensing or foreign-exchange approval applies to current-account or capital-account transactions. Current-account convertibility (trade, services, dividends, interest, royalties) is unrestricted; capital-account convertibility (equity subscription, loan funding, reinvestment) is unrestricted for non-sensitive business activities. Profit remittance is permitted without central-bank consent; the only frictions are treaty-based non-resident withholding tax (typically 15% under the NZ-foreign DTA or 30% non-treaty), and the Overseas Investment Act 2005 screening that applies solely to acquisitions of sensitive land, fishing quota, or significant business assets—neither of which is engaged by an online musical-instrument/audio retail treasury merely holding cash and settling trade payments. The RBNZ prudential framework does not categorise cash or FX flows for sectoral prioritisation; no prior licence, monetary-authority licence, or exchange-control permit is required for the CK_IC entity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement exists in New Zealand. There is no mandatory conversion ratio, no compulsory repatriation timeline, and no requirement to convert export receipts into NZD. Exporters may hold foreign currency in domestic or offshore bank accounts indefinitely. The only formalities are statistical: the banking system reports cross-border transactions to the Reserve Bank via the periodical survey of financial institutions (CB statistical returns) and the Balance of Payments reporting framework, neither of which imposes any surrender or conversion obligation. For GST and income-tax purposes, export proceeds are translated into NZD at the time of supply or payment using the eligible NZD-to-FCY exchange rate published by the Inland Revenue or a bank rate benchmark; this is a valuation rule, not a surrender rule.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), acting with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and the Department of Internal Affairs for AML/CFT market-conduct supervision

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium—legal constraints: Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Revolut, Airwallex) are not licensed banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021, do not accept deposits as a New Zealand banking business, and cannot offer New Zealand domestic account numbers for inward NZD clearing (Inward Payments via the Retail Payments System / BECCS). Such EMIs may be registered as financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must comply with the AML/CFT Act 2009, but they lack the balance-sheet strength and local clearing-node guarantee required for a treasury-critical operating account; use for ancillary FX conversion or disbursements is viable for amounts below NZ$50,000 per transaction, but primary liquidity routing must remain with a RBNZ-registered incorporated bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For the CK_IC entity: (1) route the inbound wire as an MT103 SWIFT cross-border credit transfer from the shareholder's bank to the NZ bank operational account, strictly using a plain 'Equity Subscription / Capital Contribution' purpose standard (ICR payment classification under NZ BOP statistical reporting is automatically coded by the receiving bank as 'investment income' or 'direct investment capital contribution' — no RBNZ prior approval is required); (2) the received payment must be matched against a duly executed share subscription agreement or a board resolution of the IC authorising issuance of ordinary shares against the cash injection; (3) the NZ bank will require a copy of the incorporation certificate, the register of members, and a directors' resolution confirming the transaction is a genuine capital contribution—to fully satisfy source-of-funds verification; (4) for a foreign trust funding the IC, the trust deed and a trustee resolution must be lodged to demonstrate the beneficial-ownership chain, as the bank applies enhanced due diligence to trust-funded injections; (5) if the capital is later to be used for acquisition of sensitive New Zealand assets, the Overseas Investment Act 2005 screening must be cleared before the inbound transfer; but for pure treasury working capital funding this is not engaged.Primary clearing rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier payments (e.g., inventory purchases from musical instrument manufacturers in China, Japan, Germany, and the USA) denominated in USD, EUR, JPY, or CNY, with the SWIFT MT103 field 72 'Purpose' populated by the supplier's commercial invoice reference and the NZ bank's FX conversion executed on the same business day; (2) Commercial Letters of Credit (Irrevocable, Confirmed) for high-value procurement contracts above NZ$100,000, where the NZ issuing bank authenticates the LC and the beneficiary's confirming bank negotiates the presentation—this is the standard rail for large purchase orders of orchestral instruments, grand pianos, and studio consoles; (3) for card-acquirer settlements, Stripe and PayPal aggregate retail proceeds into the core NZ bank account via their respective payout cycles (daily or T+2), and the bank's treasury desk may then route batched trade payments abroad through the same MT103 rail; E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium—legal constraints: Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Wise, Revolut, Airwallex) are not licensed banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021, do not accept deposits as a New Zealand banking business, and cannot offer New Zealand domestic account numbers for inward NZD clearing (Inward Payments via the Retail Payments System / BECCS). Such EMIs may be registered as financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must comply with the AML/CFT Act 2009, but they lack the balance-sheet strength and local clearing-node guarantee required for a treasury-critical operating account; use for ancillary FX conversion or disbursements is viable for amounts below NZ$50,000 per transaction, but primary liquidity routing must remain with a RBNZ-registered incorporated bank.
