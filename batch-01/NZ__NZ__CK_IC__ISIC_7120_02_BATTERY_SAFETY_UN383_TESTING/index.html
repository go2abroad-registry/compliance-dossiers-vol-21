Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Te Tari Tāke) — myIR portal at ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity deriving New Zealand-source income or carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand must first obtain a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) through the Companies Office and then apply to Inland Revenue for a tax file number (IRD number). Non-resident companies use form IR596, while resident or carrying-on-business companies use form IR595; applications can be submitted electronically through myIR or by a registered tax agent. The application must include certificate of incorporation, registered office details, director information and, for non-resident applicants, evidence of the New Zealand tax agent or local address for service. Once the IRD number is assigned, the entity must file GST and income tax returns if turnover exceeds relevant thresholds or if NZ-source income is derived; a foreign entity with no NZ taxable presence is generally not required to register for an IRD number.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box election system. Eligible close companies may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status under subpart HA of the Income Tax Act 2007 to pass through tax attributes to shareholders. Certain foreign-owned companies may elect to be treated as non-resident or as exempt from tax on non-New Zealand-sourced income, and cross-border hybrid mismatch elections may be required or available under the OECD-aligned BEPS rules. No general tax-haven exemption is available; any specific statutory exemption must be claimed by filing the appropriate IRD return or election form.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated as an export if the supplier exports the goods within 28 days of the time of supply to a destination outside New Zealand and retains documentary evidence prescribed by section 11 of the GST Act. Services performed directly in connection with goods intended for export may also be zero-rated if provided to a non-resident outside New Zealand, provided the services are not supplied to a person in New Zealand and not for use in New Zealand. Technical testing and analysis services for exported battery goods may qualify, subject to the supplier holding sufficient evidence of destination, export, and payment.

  • Customs export declaration (CusDec) through New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical export
  • Commercial invoice and contract of supply
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank payment proof confirming foreign currency repatriation

New Zealand imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or government API invoice authorisation. E-invoicing via the PEPPOL network is voluntary. Suppliers must issue tax invoices that satisfy section 24 of the GST Act, including supplier name, GST number, invoice date, description of supply, and GST amount. The Inland Revenue may request electronic records during audit; no pre-clearance is required.

Inland Revenue audits export zero-rating claimants with particular attention to physical export timing, documentary evidence, and currency remittance. If goods are not exported within 28 days, or if documentation is insufficient, GST of 15% becomes payable and use-of-money interest runs from the original time of supply. Fraudulent or abusive export claims attract shortfall penalties of 20%, 40%, or 100% of the tax shortfall, plus criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act. The Commissioner may also claw back refunds previously issued and require repayment of any GST input credits claimed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely convertible for residents and non-residents without exchange-control approval. Profit remittances, dividends, interest, loan repayments, royalty payments, and capital repatriation are not subject to central bank licensing or administrative conversion limits. The only relevant filter is banking-channel due diligence, Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 compliance, taxation at source for certain outbound payments, and the Overseas Investment Act 2005 regime for acquisitions of sensitive land, significant business assets, or fishing quotas. No government approval is required for an International Company incorporated under a permitted foreign statute to open a treasury account in New Zealand or to inject equity capital from a non-resident parent.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement. There is no mandated timeline for converting foreign-currency export receipts into NZD and no mandatory conversion ratio. The default ratio is 0%, meaning an exporter may retain 100% of export receipts in an acceptable foreign-currency account or convert them to NZD according to commercial treasury policy and the exporter's banking relationship.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for ancillary payment collection and Multi-Currency treasury routing, but Low for acting as the principal corporate banking, escrow, or trade-finance account provider. Wise New Zealand Limited is not a registered bank under the Prudential Supervision of Banks in New Zealand framework; it operates as a financial service provider and payment intermediary. It may hold funds for payment transmission incidental to cross-border trading, but it cannot provide regulated banking products such as commercial letters of credit, banker's acceptances, or RTGS settlement. Offshore digital EMIs must comply with the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 if they provide services into New Zealand. Corporate treasury should use a licensed domestic bank as the primary account and use fintech rails solely for expedited low-value settlement or employee expense disbursements.

The IR4 return for a standard 31 March balance date is due by 7 July immediately following the end of the income year. Where the company uses a tax agent with a current extension schedule, the deadline is generally extended to 31 March of the following income year. Terminal tax payment is due on the 7th of the month following the balance date (typically 7 May for a March balance date) unless on a tax agent extension.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 (aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions in an income year). This triggers the requirement to prepare and contemporaneously retain transfer pricing documentation (Local File + Master File equivalent) supporting the arm's length character of the transactings.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime as seen in BVI/Cayman. However, the Income Tax Act 2007 requires genuine economic substance for a company to be treated as a New Zealand tax resident and to give effect to deductions. The GAAR (s BG 1) and the transfer pricing, CFC, and thin-capitalisation rules effectively require real local activity. A New Zealand company that is part of a tax-exempt offshore International Company structure with no employees, premises, or decision-making in New Zealand will be vulnerable to being re-characterised as an agent or conduit, and its income reallocated to the principal.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Concealment of Income

Statutory sanction: Criminal liability under Part 4 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 (s 143B): upon conviction, an individual is liable to a fine not exceeding NZ$1,000,000 and/or imprisonment not exceeding 5 years; a body corporate is liable to a fine not exceeding NZ$1,000,000. In addition, a shortfall penalty for evasion of up to 150% of the tax shortfall may be imposed.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in the evasion are personally liable for the criminal fine; they may also be ordered by the court to pay restitution of the unpaid tax. Inland Revenue may issue a border stop order (travel ban) for tax debts exceeding NZ$25,000 and directors may be barred from leaving New Zealand. Imprisonment up to 5 years can be imposed.
Sham / Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner of Inland Revenue may invoke s BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 to disregard the intervening entity or reallocate income to the New Zealand company. Transfer pricing adjustments and disallowed deductions can result in a tax shortfall, on which shortfall penalties of 20% (unacceptable tax position), 100% (abusive tax position), or 150% (evasion) may be applied. The company may also be struck off under the Companies Act 1993.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable under s 300 of the Companies Act 1993 for reckless trading if the company continues to operate while insolvent after the Commissioner's assessment. Courts may order civil compensation and disqualify the individual from managing companies for up to 10 years. A travel ban may be imposed for unpaid assessments.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue re-determines the arm's length price under subpart GC and may impose a transfer pricing adjustment. The resulting tax shortfall attracts shortfall penalties (20% for unacceptable tax position; 100% for abusive tax position) plus use-of-money interest from the date the tax would have been payable.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve the defective tax return are exposed to personal liability under the Tax Administration Act 1994 as 'wholly or partly responsible' for the shortfall penalty, provided they knew of the tax position. The penalties attach directly to the director's personal assets; travel bans can be issued for unpaid penalties exceeding NZ$25,000.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / Countering Financing of Terrorism (CFT) Failure

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, a reporting entity that breaches CDD, record-keeping, or suspicious activity reporting obligations is liable to a civil penalty up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate and NZ$50,000 for an individual. Where the conduct amounts to money laundering under the Crimes Act 1961 (s 243), an individual is liable to imprisonment up to 7 years and a fine up to NZ$2,000,000.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted as a principal party if they directed or facilitated the laundering. In addition to imprisonment, the High Court may make an assets forfeiture order under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. Directors may also be disqualified and their passports cancelled; a travel ban is available against them as a condition of bail.
