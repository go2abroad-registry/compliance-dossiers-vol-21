Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone — A Cook Islands-style International Company (CK_IC) that is non-resident, holds no New Zealand fixed establishment, and derives no New Zealand-sourced income is exempt from New Zealand income tax and may hold a limited-scope GST registration solely for zero-rated international marine bunkering supplies. By contrast, a New Zealand-resident company or a foreign company with a New Zealand permanent establishment is subject to Entity_Level_Taxation at the 28% corporate rate unless a Look-Through Company election is made.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime; entity classification is determined by domestic tax law. Available statutory elections: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under Form IR427 for close-held companies, conferring Pass_Through_Eligible treatment; (2) consolidated group and imputation group elections under the Income Tax Act 2007; (3) GST group registration and GST branch registration under sections 55 and 54 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (4) hybrid and branch mismatch defensive elections and adjustments under Subpart FH and section GB 52 of the Income Tax Act 2007 aligned with OECD BEPS Action 2; (5) optional zero-rating elections and secondhand goods elections relevant to marine stores and vessel supplies.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of actual or expected taxable supplies in any 12-month period (sections 51 and 52, GST Act 1985). A non-resident supplier of remote services or low-value imported goods to New Zealand consumers must register when supplies to NZ exceed NZD 60,000 per annum. Voluntary registration is available for marine bunkering suppliers to support zero-rated export supplies and input tax recovery.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under section 11(1)(m) of the GST Act 1985 for supplies of bunker fuel, lubricants, and ship stores delivered to the master or owner of a craft that is or will be engaged in international (non-New Zealand coastal) travel for reward, provided the goods are solely for use as stores during international travel; related port, towage, mooring, and maintenance services supplied to international craft are zero-rated under section 11A(2)(b). General export zero-rating under section 11(1)(a) applies where the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand, with Inland Revenue practice requiring physical export and documented shipment within 28 days of the time of supply. The supplier must hold contemporaneous evidence of export and of the vessel's international voyage itinerary; supplies to coastally-trading, fishing, or non-international vessels remain standard-rated at 15%.

  • Customs export declaration lodged with the New Zealand Customs Service (Integrated Cargo System export entry with approved exit status)
  • Bill of Lading or Sea Waybill evidencing discharge at an international port outside New Zealand
  • Bunker Delivery Note (BDN) signed by the master detailing quantity, grade, IMO number, and port of delivery
  • Vessel certificate of registry or IMO tonnage certificate evidencing the craft's international voyage status
  • Inward foreign currency remittance certificate and SWIFT MT103 wire confirmation evidencing receipt of export consideration from the charterer or shipowner
  • Voyage log or port itinerary demonstrating a foreign destination and confirming the craft is not engaged in New Zealand coastal trade

No mandatory e-invoicing, real-time fiscal clearance, or digital stamping regime exists in New Zealand. E-invoicing is voluntary and operates interoperably through the Peppol network administered by the NZ Peppol Authority. GST obligations are discharged via electronic returns in myIR without API-based invoice pre-clearance by Inland Revenue. Suppliers must nonetheless issue and retain GST invoices, including electronic format, recording recipient details, consideration, GST-exclusive value, and the statutory zero-rating basis for marine bunker supplies; the retained export evidence package operates as the functional substitute for fiscal clearance.

Inland Revenue may, upon audit, disregard export evidence and claw back the zero-rating, assessing GST at 15% on the full supply consideration plus use-of-money interest from the original due date under Parts 7 and 12 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Shortfall penalties under section 141 scale from 20% (failure to take reasonable care or unacceptable tax position) to 40% (gross carelessness), 100% (abusive tax position), and 150% (evasion). Deliberate fraudulent bunkering zero-rating claims expose the International Company and its officers to criminal prosecution under sections 143A and 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, carrying fines of up to NZD 50,000 per offence and, for individuals, imprisonment of up to five years; director and promoter liability may be imposed under section 149. Given the Medium_Risk_Enhanced AML tier applicable to ISIC_5222 marine bunkering, IRD and NZ Customs jointly scrutinize BDN-to-remittance matching, vessel AIS data, and same-vessel resupply patterns, and all substantiation records must be retained for seven years.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised and open capital and current account regime. The NZD is freely convertible for trade, investment, and profit remittance. There are no restrictions on capital account transactions, no inward/outward capital approval requirements, and no exchange controls applicable to non-resident entities. Profit remittances, dividends, and repatriations are not subject to exchange control approval; however, obtaining bank financing or maintaining a corporate account may be subject to AML/FATCA/CRS compliance and non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) or approved issuer levy (AIL) obligations on dividends, while no withholding tax applies to principal repayment or capital gains in the ordinary course.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export proceeds surrender rules, mandatory conversion ratios, or repatriation timelines in New Zealand. Exporters are free to hold foreign currency proceeds in offshore or onshore accounts; however, for statistical and AML reporting, funds credited to a New Zealand bank account must be accompanied by a valid transaction narrative and, where applicable, the New Zealand Customs Service export entry and underlying commercial documentation.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) in conjunction with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009; New Zealand maintains no separate foreign exchange control authority due to the freely floating New Zealand dollar (NZD).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low to Medium. Offshore digital EMI providers without a New Zealand registered branch cannot open a domestic settlement account in NZD for corporate use. Non-bank money service providers may be used for limited inbound/outbound USD or EUR transactions, but they face heightened AML/KYC and licensing constraints under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008 and are not a substitute for a full RBNZ-regulated current account for bunkering trade settlement or standby letters of credit.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Inland Revenue requires mandatory disclosure of cross-border related-party transactions on the IR4S where total transactions between the New Zealand business and overseas related parties exceed NZD 1 million in aggregate per income year, or where financing arrangements with overseas related parties exceed NZD 500,000 (including short-term trade credit) and are not at arm's length. Full transfer pricing documentation (Local File) must be prepared where the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions is NZD 1 million or more for goods/services, NZD 1 million for intangibles, and NZD 1 million for financing transactions specifically under New Zealand's Schedule 20 disclosure requirements. Important: New Zealand does not have statutory Local File/Master File thresholds identical to OECD Action 13 of NZD 1 million in all cases; the documentation obligations are driven by the IR4S disclosure schedule and the requirement to maintain contemporaneous documentation sufficient to show arm's length outcomes for all cross-border related-party arrangements. The New Zealand Master File obligation effectively applies to significant enterprises with revenue over NZD 100 million and related-party transactions over NZD 10 million in aggregate; an IC acting as a conduit for fuel procurement from the Cook Islands parent would be required to prepare and, if requested, submit a Master File within 30 days of the Information Request. In all cases, documentation must be prepared before the tax return filing date to be contemporaneous.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand applies a general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, which voids tax avoidance arrangements; a Cook Islands IC operating a bunkering business in New Zealand must demonstrate real commercial substance, including operational decision-making, physical fuel storage or logistics activity, local risk management, and the presence of employees or agents in New Zealand. Although New Zealand does not apply the OECD-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) of the Caribbean or UAE, the thin capitalisation rules (75% debt-to-asset safe harbour for non-resident-owned companies) and the related-party financing rules impose substance-based limitations. A shell company without local substance may be disregarded as a sham, and the GAAR may reconstruct the IC's financing, royalty, and service fee arrangements. The IC must be able to evidence: (i) a New Zealand bank account, (ii) local operational contracts, (iii) independent directors if structured as a Cook Islands entity, (iv) actual meetings held outside New Zealand, and (v) arm's length remuneration for any New Zealand agent. The 'economic substance doctrine' in the context of the New Zealand-Cook Islands DTA also arises through the limitation of benefits clause requiring the IC to be a bona fide resident with real continuous presence, not a conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion via Understatement of Bunkering Revenue

Statutory sanction: Under section 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994, evasion of tax is a criminal offence punishable on conviction by imprisonment for a term not exceeding five years and a fine of up to NZD 50,000 for the first offence, plus a fine of up to NZD 50,000 in respect of each subsequent offence. Civil shortfall penalties under section 141I can be up to 150% of the tax shortfall for evasion or an abusive tax position. Inland Revenue can also impose late payment penalties and use-of-money interest on the entire evaded base.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false tax returns, direct the concealment of sales, or benefit from the evasion may be prosecuted personally. Under section 149 of the Tax Administration Act, directors are jointly and severally liable with the company for tax liabilities incurred through the company's default where the default is attributable to the director's act or omission and the director failed to take reasonable steps to prevent it. Criminal conviction may bring imprisonment up to 5 years, travel bans under the Criminal Proceeds Recovery Act 2009, and confiscation of personal and corporate assets. Civil restitution includes full repayment of evaded tax, shortfall penalties of up to 150%, and disqualification from managing a company under the Companies Act 1993.
Shell Company / No Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (BG 1) allows Inland Revenue to reconstruct the arrangement and deny the tax benefit entirely; the IC may be assessed as if no intercompany fees were paid, resulting in tax at 28% on the full gross margin plus non-dissection penalties of up to 100% of the shortfall for taking an unacceptable tax position lacking a reasonable basis. Thin capitalisation recharacterisation may treat excessive debt as equity, disallowing interest deductions and imposing non-resident withholding tax plus approved issuer levy penalties. Repeated abuse may lead to deregistration of the New Zealand branch and denial of the DTA benefits retroactively.

Director personal liability: Directors of the IC and of any New Zealand subsidiary may be held personally liable under the companies anti-avoidance rules where the corporate form is a mere facade. The court can pierce the corporate veil under section 15 of the Companies Act 1993, holding directors personally liable for the reconstructed tax debt. Where the directors facilitate a scheme designed to avoid New Zealand tax, they can be charged with being party to tax evasion, incurring imprisonment up to 5 years and a NZD 50,000 fine. A travel ban may be issued while criminal proceeds investigations are pending.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Unjustified Service Fees

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may adjust the transaction to the arm's length price under Part 4 of the Income Tax Act 2007, increasing taxable income, disallowing the corresponding interest or expense, and applying a transfer pricing shortfall penalty under section 141AD of up to 150% of the resulting shortfall if a transfer pricing arrangement was not documented contemporaneously and in good faith. A 10% late payment penalty may accrue, and use-of-money interest compounds. If the arrangement has a tax avoidance more than merely incidental purpose, BG 1 applies and penalties escalate. IRD also has power to impose GST adjustments on cross-border related party low-value supplies.

Director personal liability: Directors who controlled pricing and knew of the inflated cross-border charges are jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax debt. A director can be personally disqualified by the Financial Markets Authority or Inland Revenue for up to 5 years; in egregious cases of fraudulent transfer pricing, criminal prosecution for obtaining a tax advantage may follow, carrying imprisonment up to 7 years under the Crimes Act 1961 or the Tax Administration Act, alongside a travel ban and asset freezing orders.
Failure to Withhold and Pay NRWT / DTA Abusive Claims

Statutory sanction: Under section 144 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, any person who fails to deduct and pay NRWT is liable to pay the full amount of the tax plus an initial penalty of 1% and incremental monthly penalties; in addition, a criminal fine of up to NZD 50,000 and imprisonment for up to 6 months may apply for willful non-payment. A false DTA residence certificate triggers shortfall penalties of up to 150% and for a fraudulent certificate, prosecution under the Crimes Act for forgery with a maximum penalty of 10 years' imprisonment.

Director personal liability: The directors of the paying company are personally liable under the PAYE and withholding tax provisions of section 120 of the Tax Administration Act, which imposes directors' civil penalties equal to the unpaid withholding tax plus 100% where the company failed to comply. Directors who personally impressed the false certificate face criminal liability, imprisonment, and a travel ban while subject to the criminal process.
AML / Sanctions Violation for Bunkering Operations

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, an IC carrying on business in New Zealand is a financial institution only if it is a reporting entity; if the bunkering company does not fall under the AML/CFT regime, the breach arises instead through the Crimes Act 1961 handling of proceeds of crime, with a maximum penalty of 7 years' imprisonment for money laundering, and the Proceeds of Crime Act 2015 can confiscate the entire corporate asset base, including vessels and storage tanks. Sanctions violations under the Russia Sanctions Act 2022 or UN Act 1946 can lead to fines up to NZD 1,000,000 and imprisonment up to 14 years.

Director personal liability: Directors who knew or should have known of the illicit vessel counterparties and failed to terminate the business relationship are personally liable for money laundering facilitation, subject to criminal prosecution, asset freezing, travel bans, and long-term disqualification from holding directorships. The corporate veil is pierced under the Companies Act 1993 where the company was a facade for the laundering activity.
