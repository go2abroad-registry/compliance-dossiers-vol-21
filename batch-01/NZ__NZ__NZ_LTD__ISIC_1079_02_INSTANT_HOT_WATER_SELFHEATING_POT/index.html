Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue, New Zealand); electronic portal: myIR (myIR.ird.govt.nz); company incorporation is administered separately by the New Zealand Companies Office (companies-register.companiesoffice.govt.nz).

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must first confirm whether it is carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand. If the entity is incorporated in New Zealand as an NZ Limited company, it is automatically allocated an Inland Revenue Department (IRD) number following incorporation at the New Zealand Companies Office, provided the incorporation application includes the election to obtain an IRD number. If not automatically allocated, the company must apply through the IRD's myIR portal using the 'Register for a Tax Number' service. A non-resident foreign company that is not incorporated in New Zealand may apply to the IRD for a non-resident IRD number via myIR or by submitting the IRD's standard non-resident company application with certified constitutional documents, proof of foreign jurisdiction registration, and the registered address of the principal or agent in New Zealand. The application must include the entity's legal name, date of incorporation, registered office, director details, and a physical New Zealand address for service if a taxable activity is present. The IRD will issue an eight-digit IRD number, which is used for income tax, GST, and employer obligations. Where the entity intends to supply goods in New Zealand, GST registration is applied for concurrently.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None. New Zealand has no check-the-box election. Taxation follows statutory residence and entity classification. A company incorporated in New Zealand is tax resident and is taxed at the entity level on worldwide income. For GST, optional registration and the payments basis or invoice basis accounting elections are available, but these are not entity classification elections. Hybrid mismatch rules under OECD BEPS are applied to deny deductions, but no elective hybrid mismatch election is available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 gross turnover in any 12-month period for supplies made within the scope of a taxable activity in New Zealand. A person who expects to exceed this threshold must register for GST; a non-resident is liable to register if carrying on a taxable activity in or from New Zealand. Voluntary registration is available if the turnover threshold is not met.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods that are physically exported from New Zealand before the expiry of any applicable export period, where the supplier or the supplier's agent provides satisfactory evidence of export. For this self-heating food manufacturing scope, qualifying exported self-heating pots and meal products must be removed permanently from New Zealand for supply to an overseas buyer and must not be entered into home consumption or New Zealand's GST-registered economy. Services supplied to a non-resident customer may be zero-rated as remote or offshore services where the customer is outside New Zealand when the services are performed and the supply qualifies as a remote service under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Zero-rating is substantiated by contemporaneous export documentation and foreign exchange repatriation records.

  • New Zealand Customs Service electronic Export Entry (Customs Export Declaration) confirming departure
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or the New Zealand banking equivalent, comprising bank foreign-exchange credit advice or SWIFT MT103 evidencing repatriation of payment from the overseas buyer
  • Container tracking report or port departure log showing the vessel name, sailing date, and terminal of the overseas destination

New Zealand does not operate a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or anti-fraud digital stamping platform. E-invoicing is optional and enabled through the New Zealand E-Invoicing Network using the Peppol standard. GST-registered persons must issue tax invoices containing the statutory content required under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, and must file GST returns electronically through myIR on a monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly basis. There is no requirement for an invoice to receive prior government tax API approval or digital stamping before issue.

If zero-rating is claimed but the goods or services do not meet the statutory export criteria or are not corroborated by the required export and foreign-exchange documentation, Inland Revenue will treat the supply as taxable at the standard GST rate of 15% and will restore the tax shortfall by assessment. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 can apply up to 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or similar acts. Use-of-money interest accrues daily from the original return date. Customs offences for false export declarations attract penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, including financial penalties and potential forfeiture. Deliberate fraudulent zero-rating can lead to criminal prosecution and director liability, and retrospective adjustments to prior-period GST returns are mandatory for any unsubstantiated zero-rating claim.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. The IMF Article VIII status means there are no restrictions on payments and transfers for international transactions. Current account items, including trade payments, service fees, royalties, and dividends, can be remitted without foreign exchange control approval. Capital account transactions are also unrestricted, but certain acquisitions of sensitive assets or significant business assets by foreign persons require consent under the Overseas Investment Act 2005. Profit repatriation is not exchange-controlled; however, dividends paid by the NZ company will be subject to New Zealand non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at 15% or a lower treaty rate under an applicable double tax agreement. The company must maintain imputation records to support any imputation credit attached, and must comply with thin capitalisation rules if non-resident shareholders provide excessive debt funding. No mandatory conversion or surrender is required for export proceeds, and there is no central bank exchange rate approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory obligation to surrender, convert, or repatriate export proceeds. Receipts in foreign currency may be retained in external bank accounts indefinitely and converted to NZD at commercial discretion. For tax purposes, an export business may adopt a foreign functional currency only if more than 80% of its business income is derived in that currency and the Inland Revenue approves the basis; otherwise profits must be translated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice and the Income Tax Act 2007. Exporters must retain underlying invoices, shipping documents, and foreign exchange records for seven years under tax and AML record-keeping rules, and any overseas receivables must be revalued annually at balance date using the prevailing exchange rate.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) is the central bank and prudential regulator. There is no separate foreign exchange administration; the RBNZ exercises statutory oversight over registered banks, payment systems, and the Financial Market Infrastructure regime. Foreign exchange transactions are not subject to licensing or prior approval, but cross-border cash movements of NZD 10,000 or more must be reported to NZ Customs under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-Low as a primary banking rail; acceptable as a supplementary payments channel only. New Zealand does not require a banking licence for non-deposit-taking money service businesses, but any entity providing financial services in New Zealand must be a registered financial service provider under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must comply with the AML/CFT Act. Offshore EMI platforms such as Wise or Airwallex can open NZD virtual accounts backed by one or more RBNZ-registered banks, but they cannot accept deposits or lend. The NZ company may use these platforms for low-value receipts and supplier disbursements; however, capital subscriptions, long-term treasury positions, and material trade settlements should be routed through Tier 1 licensed banks to ensure settlement assurance, clearing via SWIFT, and access to FX hedging under NZ banking master agreements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For equity subscriptions and shareholder loans, the standard rail is a SWIFT MT103 credit transfer to the company's NZD or USD operating account via the sending bank. The reference field must quote the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and the purpose code Equity Capital Contribution or Intercompany Loan. The RBNZ does not require exchange approval or surrender. The receiving bank will perform sanctions screening and may require the injection to be sourced only from the named shareholder account; third-party payments require an executed declaration and source-of-funds evidence. For amounts exceeding NZD 100,000 or where the transferring beneficial owner is a non-resident, the bank may request a board resolution, the signed loan agreement, and an overseas investment screening assessment if the capital injection is used to acquire significant business assets or sensitive land.The company should maintain a clearing hierarchy: first, SWIFT MT103 for individual supplier invoices and trade payables, with full invoice references and NZD equivalent; second, MT202 for treasury transfers between its own accounts, such as from a capital escrow account to an operating account; third, commercial letters of credit (UCP600) issued by the NZ bank in favour of overseas suppliers of raw materials, particularly for large exothermic reagent purchases, with presentations through confirmed or advising banks; and fourth, SWIFT gpi-enabled MT103 for urgent disbursements to China, Australia, and Southeast Asian buyers, using the reference ISIC 1079 trade settlement. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The New Zealand Inland Revenue has adopted the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines (2022) with administrative safe-harbour documentation thresholds. Contemporaneous comprehensive transfer-pricing documentation is expected where any of the following apply: (a) aggregate cross-border related-party financial transactions (loans and interest) exceed NZ$1,000,000; (b) aggregate other cross-border related-party transactions (goods, services, intangibles, and recharges) exceed NZ$2,000,000; or (c) the company is a New Zealand-resident ultimate parent of a multinational group that lodges a Country-by-Country Report, with the notification threshold set at consolidated group annual revenue of NZ$1,300,000,000. Master File and Local File documents are not mandatorily pro-forma filed in New Zealand, but the IRD may demand them in writing within 30 days, and the taxpayer has the burden of proving arm's length pricing. Domestic safe-harbour confirmation is not available for royalty or management-fee payments; they are deductible only if the relevant withholding tax is paid and the amount is at arm's length.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) statute of the type used in the UAE or Cayman Islands. The equivalent substance doctrine emerges from four mandates: (a) the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) at s BG 1 Income Tax Act 2007 invalidating arrangements whose purpose or effect is tax avoidance; (b) the deduction tests at s DA 1 requiring expenditure to be incurred in the course of a business capable of producing assessable income; (c) the thin capitalisation regime at s FE requiring a minimum 40% shareholder debt-to-asset ratio for companies with more than 50% non-resident ownership, any excess interest disallowed; and (d) the OECD MLI Principal Purpose Test incorporated in treaty application. A shell company with no active manufacturing, sales, or decision-making presence cannot credibly support intra-group re-invoicing, royalty, or interest deductions, and will be attacked under the GAAR and denied treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent or Misleading Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under s 143A and s 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994: imprisonment for up to 5 years and/ or a fine of up to NZ$50,000 per offence. The civil shortfall penalty regime imposes 150% of the tax shortfall for evasion or an arrangement with a dominant purpose of tax evasion (s 141D); in addition, 20–40% penalties apply for lack of reasonable care or gross carelessness. UOMI at the underpayment rate accrues from the statutory date to payment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorise, engage in, or profit from evasion face personal prosecution as principals under s 143A; courts may order reparation and confiscation under the Sentencing Act 2002; the Companies Act 1993 permits disqualification for up to 5 years; Inland Revenue may obtain a departure prohibition order preventing directors from leaving New Zealand pending settlement; civil joint and several liability arises for the unpaid tax under s 90 where the debts are PAYE or GST; creditor-voidable preferences may be unwound by the commissioner as a creditor.
Repatriation Non-Compliance / Unpaid NRWT and DTA Abuse

Statutory sanction: The payer-company is personally liable to pay the unrecovered NRWT (30% dividends / 15% interest / 15% royalties, or 2% AIL where validly elected); late payment penalties and UOMI accrue; the unpaid tax is fully assessable and the dividend or interest deduction for the payer (where applicable) may be forfeited; criminal penalties under s 143B apply for wilful non-remittance, with fines up to NZ$15,000 and imprisonment up to 3 years.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the full amount of NRWT withheld but not remitted, under s 90 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, after the IRD issues a director penalty notice and no settlement occurs within 30 days; travel bans are routinely sought where a director has facilitated asset-stripping; fraudulent dividend distributions to directors in breach of the Companies Act create a debt due to the company which the Commissioner may recover via a liquidator, together with restitution and imprisonment.
Shell Company Structures / Sham Transactions / Treaty-Shopping

Statutory sanction: The arrangement is void against the Commissioner (s BG 1 and s GB 1), with profit reallocation to the economic substance jurisdiction; the company loses all deduction for intercompany payments, the taxable income is reconstructed, and shortfall penalties of 20–150% apply. If the arrangement involves dishonest signatures or backdated board minutes, criminal offences apply under the Crimes Act 1961 and the Companies Act 1993, with fines and imprisonment up to 5 years. Treaty relief is denied under the PPT, with recaptured NRWT.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false minutes, declarations, or financial statements are personally liable for fraudulent misrepresentation to creditors and the Commissioner; they may be personally assessed for the tax shortfall under s 141 (assisting) and s 90 (collection) where the company is stripped of assets; courts may issue restraining orders over personal property and passports; disqualification under s 382 of the Companies Act 1993, and criminal conviction on an evasion or fraud count carry mandatory travel and banking restrictions.
AML and Banking / Wire-Transfer Reputational Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, an entity engaging in financial services without registration is liable to a fine up to NZ$1,000,000 for an entity and NZ$100,000 for an individual; if the entity is a Reporting Entity, failure to conduct CDD, wire-transfer verification, or suspicious transaction reporting (noting the low-risk standard tier for self-heating food manufacturing) carries fines up to NZ$100,000 per breach; banks may additionally freeze accounts and terminate banking relationships, and funds may be liable to forfeiture under the Proceeds of Crime Act 1991 where the flow of funds constitutes money laundering or the proceeds of a serious offence.

Director personal liability: Directors of a non-Reporting Entity that intentionally or recklessly engages in remittance or currency exchange activities without registration are personally liable for the fines; bank account signatories facing a money-laundering investigation are subject to restraint orders under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, asset freezing, and possible imprisonment for up to 7 years for laundering proceeds under s 243 of the Crimes Act 1961; travel bans may be imposed under the Extradition Act 1999 where cross-border funds are at stake.
