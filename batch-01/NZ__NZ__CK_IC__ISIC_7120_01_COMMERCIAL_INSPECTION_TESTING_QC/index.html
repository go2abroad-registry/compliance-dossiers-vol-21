Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD / Te Tari Taake), Wellington, New Zealand — principal online portal 'myIR' (https://www.ird.govt.nz); business registration coordinated via the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) registry (https://www.nzbn.govt.nz) and the New Zealand Companies Office (https://www.companiesoffice.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

Step 1 — Incorporate a New Zealand International Company (IC) with the NZ Companies Office or, if a foreign-incorporated entity, register as an overseas company; obtain the NZBN as the common Master Data identifier. Step 2 — Apply for an IRD number (New Zealand tax file number) through myIR using the non-resident company application form (IR596) or via the myIR business account; the IRD number serves as the statutory TIN for income tax and GST purposes. Step 3 — Register for GST using the GST registration form (GST101A or online via myIR) if annual taxable supplies exceed NZD 60,000, or voluntarily where a refund position is expected; non-residents supplying 'remote services' to NZ consumers must register under the GST (Cross-border) rules regardless of turnover. Step 4 — Where applicable, file an election for 'Exempt Foreign Company' status (form IR453) with the IRD within the statutory deadline (generally by the first two income years) to maintain a tax-exempt offshore profile. Step 5 — Register as an employer (IRD employer number) only if hiring NZ-based staff; otherwise no PAYE obligations arise. Step 6 — Open a NZ or offshore bank account and notify the IRD of the appointed New Zealand agent for service of notices.

Tax ID type & application path: Tax_Exempt_Zone — The CK International Company (CK_IC) is treated as a New Zealand foreign company that, if it elects and continues to qualify under section EX 21B of the Income Tax Act 2007, is an 'Exempt Foreign Company' (EFC): all shareholders are non-resident, the company is not controlled from New Zealand, it derives no New Zealand-sourced income, and it carries on no business in New Zealand. As an EFC, the entity is wholly outside the New Zealand income tax net, effective as a statutory tax-exempt offshore zone, while remaining a limited liability legal person. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone — The CK International Company (CK_IC) is treated as a New Zealand foreign company that, if it elects and continues to qualify under section EX 21B of the Income Tax Act 2007, is an 'Exempt Foreign Company' (EFC): all shareholders are non-resident, the company is not controlled from New Zealand, it derives no New Zealand-sourced income, and it carries on no business in New Zealand. As an EFC, the entity is wholly outside the New Zealand income tax net, effective as a statutory tax-exempt offshore zone, while remaining a limited liability legal person. Absent a valid EFC election, the default rule is that a company incorporated in New Zealand is tax-resident and therefore taxed on its worldwide income ('Entity_Level_Taxation' would then apply); however, for the CK_IC structure the intended and achieved status is Tax_Exempt_Zone via the EFC regime.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not have a 'check-the-box' regime. The relevant statutory elections for the CK_IC are: (i) Exempt Foreign Company election under sections EX 21A–EX 21B, Income Tax Act 2007 (filed on IR453), allowing a non-resident-owned IC to be treated as tax-exempt on non-New Zealand income; (ii) Hybrid and branch mismatch elections under Part FM of the Income Tax Act 2007 (introduced under BEPS), including the hybrid financial instrument exclusion and the surrogate branch election, to neutralise mismatches in double-tax relief; (iii) GST grouping election under section 55 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, if the IC is part of a GST group with NZ-resident members; (iv) Voluntary GST registration under section 51(3) of the GST Act, permitting the IC to register for GST even below the NZD 60,000 threshold to zero-rate export supplies and recover input tax; and (v) GST taxable-period elections (1-month, 2-month, or 6-month filing cycles) aligned with the anticipated refund profile.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (compulsory registration once annual taxable supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period, or reasonably expected to exceed in the next 12 months, per section 51 GSTA 1985). Voluntary registration permitted from the first dollar of taxable supplies (section 51(3)). Non-resident suppliers of 'remote services' (electronically supplied services, telecommunication services, and offshore services received by NZ-resident consumers as defined in section 8B GSTA 1985) are subject to a distinct statutory registration obligation with no de-minimis threshold, using the 'sole supply' rule for offshore-to-offshore business-to-business supplies to avoid double registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The CK_IC may zero-rate its supplies of on-site factory pre-shipment inspection, testing, and quality-control services under one of two mutually exclusive limbs of the GSTA 1985: (1) Goods — where the inspection/testing itself is incidental to the supply of exported tangible goods, zero-rating applies under section 11(1)(a) provided the goods are physically exported by the supplier or on the supplier's behalf within the statutory timeframe (in practice, within 12 months or as evidenced by the Customs export entry). (2) Services — where the CK_IC supplies services to a non-resident client who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, and the services are performed wholly or predominantly outside New Zealand, the supply is zero-rated under sections 11(1)(b) and 11(1)(c) (the 'offshore services' rule). For on-site factory inspection performed at an offshore factory (e.g., a manufacturing plant in the People's Republic of China), where the CK_IC contracts with a non-resident principal (e.g., an overseas importer, foreign government agency, or supranational buyer), and the inspection report is delivered to the non-resident client offshore, the supply qualifies for zero-rating provided none of the section 11(1)(c) exceptions apply (i.e., the services are not directly in connection with land in New Zealand, not directly in connection with goods in New Zealand, and not supplied to an individual physically present in New Zealand). Where the CK_IC's NZ trading entity performs inspection on NZ soil for goods to be exported, zero-rating may nonetheless be available under section 11(1)(b) if the recipient is a non-resident and the services relate to goods being exported by the recipient; the IRD applies the 'directly in connection with goods' test strictly. The CK_IC must maintain contemporaneous documentation proving the non-resident status of the counterparty (e.g., certificate of incorporation, tax residence certificate, and bank details), the physical location of the factory, and the offshore delivery of the report.

  • NZ Customs Service Export Declaration (Trade Single Window entry) — evidencing the outward movement of the goods, including the Customs Unique Identifier and the declared export value
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill — showing the consignor as the CK_IC or its agent, the consignee as the offshore customer, and the destination port/airport outside New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / Foreign Exchange Transaction Record — bank proof of receipt of the export settlement in NZD or convertible foreign currency, referencing the relevant export invoice
  • Executed inspection and testing services contract — identifying the non-resident client, the offshore factory premises, the scope of the pre-shipment quality-control work, and the delivery terms of the inspection report
  • Detailed inspection report and photographic evidence — including GPS-stamped factory coordinates, date-stamped photos of the tested goods, batch/serial numbers, and the certifying inspector's credentials
  • Certificate of Origin and/or Supplier's Declarations — where the goods are commodities subject to preferential origin rules, supporting the physical flow from the offshore factory to the importing jurisdiction
  • Invoicing pack — zero-rated GST invoice (clearly marked '0% GST — Exported Services' or '0% GST — Export of Goods'), together with all purchase orders, correspondence, and the foreign buyer's tax identification number (TIN/VAT ID)

New Zealand imposes no real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, and no continuous transaction control (CTC) mandate. E-invoicing is voluntary and is implemented under the Peppol framework (NZ e-Invoice standard, adopted by the Digital Business Council); the IRD does not operate an API-based real-time clearance gateway for individual invoices. GST returns are filed electronically via myIR on a 1-month, 2-month, or 6-month cycle, with payment and filing required by the 28th day of the following month (or the 7th day of the following month for interest/penalty purposes on deferred payment). The myIR portal supports bulk electronic filing (EDIFACT/XML) but does not pre-validate individual invoices for VAT purposes; instead, IRD conducts risk-based post-return audit mining of invoice-level data. The practical effect for the CK_IC is that paper/PDF invoices remain fully valid, but a Peppol-conformant e-invoicing protocol is recommended for audit transparency and to support the zero-rating evidence trail.

The Inland Revenue Department applies heightened audit scrutiny to zero-rated export claims by offshore-focussed entities such as the CK_IC, particularly where (i) the supplier cannot demonstrate physical export of goods or offshore performance of services, (ii) the counterparty is a related party or a zero-tax jurisdiction resident, (iii) the consideration is not repatriated within 180 days of the invoice date, or (iv) the documentation bundle is missing any mandatory component. Where the IRD determines that zero-rating was applied incorrectly or the substantive conditions were never met, it will issue a default GST assessment converting the supply to the standard rate (15%), raising the net GST liability retrospectively from the tax period in which the supply was originally returned, and charging use-of-money interest (presently 9.53% per annum under the IRD prescribed rate on underpayments) from the original due date. In addition, the Tax Administration Act 1994 imposes tiered shortfall penalties: 20% of the resulting tax shortfall for failing to take reasonable care; 40% for gross carelessness; 100% for an 'abusive tax position' (i.e., a position taken with a dominant purpose of avoiding tax that is not reasonably arguable); and 150% for evasion or an arrangement with the dominant purpose of defeating the intent of the GST Act. Where the CK_IC, its directors, or its agents knowingly submit false or misleading export zero-rating claims, the IRD may prosecute criminally under section 143A TAA 1994 (maximum imprisonment of 5 years and/or fine of NZD 50,000 for individuals, with an additional automatic penalty equal to the unpaid tax), and under section 143B the corporate entity is liable to a fine not exceeding NZD 100,000. The IRD also has power under Part 9 TAA 1994 to issue a 'tax avoidance arrangement' determination, striking down the purported export structure and any associated GST refund, together with a 100% shortfall penalty for an abusive tax position where the arrangement is void as against the IRD. Furthermore, where the 180-day FX repatriation deadline is missed without a demonstrable commercial cause (e.g., documentary credit delay, bank error, or contractual milestone linked to the final acceptance certificate), the IRD can recharacterise the transaction as a domestic supply, regardless of the underlying export evidence, and claw back the zero-rated status for the entire transaction. All refunds of input tax arising from zero-rated export positions are separately subject to an IRD 'integrity check' before release, and the commissioner may withhold a refund pending completion of a full records inspection under section 46 TAA 1994.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. New Zealand abolished formal exchange controls in 1984, and there are no legal restrictions, approvals, or licensing requirements imposed by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand on the conversion of NZD into foreign currency or on the repatriation of dividends, capital, or service fees. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) separately regulates financial market conduct. Profit remittance is subject exclusively to tax compliance obligations (e.g., Non-Resident Withholding Tax on distributions if applicable), not to currency control authorization. For an exempt International Company with substantive inspection and testing operations in New Zealand, no overseas investment consent is required for ordinary cross-border service remittances; however, if a non-resident acquires significant land or sensitive business assets in NZ, the Overseas Investment Act 2013 consent regime could apply, which is not triggered by routine banking or FX activity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion requirements on export proceeds. An exporter of on-site factory pre-shipment inspection, testing, and quality control services may retain, invest, hedge, or disburse foreign currency receipts offshore without any obligation to convert them into NZD within a specified timeline. Banks will nevertheless apply standard anti-money laundering transaction monitoring and may request supporting contracts and invoices to evidence the commercial substance of each inbound transfer.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

High feasibility with regulatory caveats. Wise (formerly TransferWise), Airwallex (NZ), OFX, and Convera are registered as Financial Service Providers on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register (FSP) and are subject to the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act 2009) as reporting entities. Wise is expressly targeted in the payment rails and remains the preferred fintech channel because it offers multi-currency settlement, local NZD bank account details, and pragmatic KYC documentation for cross-border service businesses. However, a fintech EMI cannot solely substitute for a Tier 1 commercial bank requirement: the entity's status as a tax-exempt International Company will be reviewed as a higher-risk customer by EMIs, requiring the same underlying economic-substance evidence and beneficial ownership disclosure. Full functionality (SWIFT outbound, commercial LC capacity) is unavailable via fintech EMIs; they are viable only for operational funding and operational disbursements, not for escrow or trade finance.

Root cause: Tax-exempt International Company status classified as a tax haven or high-risk zone flag during bank on-boarding and CDD/EDD review

Defense evidence: Full economic-substance package: executed client inspection/testing agreements with Asian agrifood importers, on-site factory audit contracts, comprehensive business plan, staffing payroll records, office lease, local licence or certification (e.g., ISO 17020 or export inspection licences), profit-and-loss projections, and a written legal opinion from a New Zealand-qualified tax counsel confirming the exempt status derives from NZ specific legislation and not from a no-tax jurisdiction.
Undisclosed or partially identified ultimate beneficial owners (UBOs) residing outside New Zealand, triggering ownership-chain red flags and correspondent-bank restrictions

Root cause: Undisclosed or partially identified ultimate beneficial owners (UBOs) residing outside New Zealand, triggering ownership-chain red flags and correspondent-bank restrictions

Defense evidence: Complete UBO chain to the final natural persons: certified passports or national identity cards, proof of residential address (utility bill or bank statement not older than 3 months), each shareholder's and director's curriculum vitae and business biography, a trust deed where applicable, a register of significant controllers filed with the NZ Companies Office, and statutory declarations of source of wealth and source of funds.
High-risk industry-class mismatch between invoicing description (inspection/testing) and expected payment flows from procurement-heavy overseas clients that may resemble trade-based money laundering

Root cause: High-risk industry-class mismatch between invoicing description (inspection/testing) and expected payment flows from procurement-heavy overseas clients that may resemble trade-based money laundering

Defense evidence: Signed service agreements with clear fee schedules, purchase orders, pre-shipment inspection reports and certificates of conformity issued per shipment, supporting photos and audit trails, exact-match contract-to-invoice amounts, invoices with accurate ISIC-based service descriptions, and independent confirmation from the counterparty bank that its client is a legitimate manufacturer or importer.
Inability to evidence the commercial rationale for a tax-exempt structure combined with rapid cross-border wire volumes, triggering SAR filing by the reporting entity

Root cause: Inability to evidence the commercial rationale for a tax-exempt structure combined with rapid cross-border wire volumes, triggering SAR filing by the reporting entity

Defense evidence: Group structure chart, intercompany service contracts with arm's length pricing, operational substance in New Zealand (staff, premises, insurances), correspondence from a recognised New Zealand corporate services provider, historical settlement track record with verifiable audit trail, and quarterly reconciliation of inspection revenue against time sheets and factory visits.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

New Zealand has no mandatory purpose-code regime for wire transfers; payment instructions are processed via SWIFT MT103 (ISO 20022 in the NZD local clearing context) with BBAN/IBAN-style account references. For optimal reconciliation, the remitter should populate the wire reference with the ISIC-coded service description (e.g., 'FACTORY INSPECTION QC SERVICES', Contract/PO number) and the transaction narrative should state 'Trade-related business services under ISIC 7120'. The beneficial owner's name, the beneficiary bank branch SWIFT code, and the NZ account number in the format used by the local exchange settlement (e.g., ANZ/NZ bank prefix, branch and account fields) must be accurate. Inbound FX conversions from USD, EUR, CNY to NZD should be executed through the receiving bank's spot desk or Wise's mid-market conversion; no Reserve Bank approval is required. For share capital injection, the corporate law identifier in the payment narrative must reference the board resolution; capital is freely credited to the operational account.For fee payments to overseas subcontractors, laboratories, and auditors, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 via the transmitting NZ bank's correspondent network (typically via Australian or US correspondent banks for USD, EU banks for EUR, and Chinese RMB via CNH clearing in Hong Kong). For payment of NZD-denominated invoices within New Zealand, the clearing rail is the Reserve Bank-owned Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) used by the immediate payment system and the Settlement Before Interchange (SBI) network. For any trade settlement where the inspection/testing agency acts as an intermediary purchasing goods for resale (not standard for ISIC 7120), a commercial Letter of Credit (LC) may be arranged through the issuing bank with SWIFT MT700/MT707 messages; however, for the core fee-for-service model, MT103 electronic transfers and Wise's automated wholesale settlement are sufficient. Outbound transfers require upholding screening against NZ sanctions lists and the bank's internal AML risk appetite.FX Spot Contracts (same-day or T+2 settlement in NZD)Outright FX Forwards (standard deliverable forwards up to 12 months, freely permitted and tradeable in the NZD market)FX Swaps (swap of principal at inception and reversal at maturity, used for liquidity funding)Currency Options (European-style vanilla call/put options for asymmetric risk protection)Cross-Currency Swaps (CCS/CRS licensed by the FMA and permitted under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 where prudential requirements are met)Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) are legally permissible in New Zealand but are economically unnecessary because NZD is freely deliverable; regulators prefer deliverable instruments to avoid any question of speculative positioning; NDFs may only be offered by a registered derivatives issuer under the FMC Act

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: High feasibility with regulatory caveats. Wise (formerly TransferWise), Airwallex (NZ), OFX, and Convera are registered as Financial Service Providers on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register (FSP) and are subject to the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act 2009) as reporting entities. Wise is expressly targeted in the payment rails and remains the preferred fintech channel because it offers multi-currency settlement, local NZD bank account details, and pragmatic KYC documentation for cross-border service businesses. However, a fintech EMI cannot solely substitute for a Tier 1 commercial bank requirement: the entity's status as a tax-exempt International Company will be reviewed as a higher-risk customer by EMIs, requiring the same underlying economic-substance evidence and beneficial ownership disclosure. Full functionality (SWIFT outbound, commercial LC capacity) is unavailable via fintech EMIs; they are viable only for operational funding and operational disbursements, not for escrow or trade finance.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% domestic Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) on gross dividends paid by a New Zealand-resident company. Treaty relief generally reduces the rate to 15%, and to 5% for a corporate shareholder beneficially owning at least 10% of voting power under most New Zealand DTAs. For a non-resident exempt IC, distributions sourced outside New Zealand are outside NRWT; a distribution sourced from New Zealand could be caught at the domestic rate absent DTA relief.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZ$10 million annual aggregate cross-border related-party transactions triggers Inland Revenue's contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation expectation. Mandatory Country-by-Country reporting applies to multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of NZ$1.3 billion or more. Master File and Local File are not filed annually but must be produced within 30 days of Inland Revenue request in a transfer pricing risk review.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand tax law does not operate a separate economic substance filing regime for ordinary companies; corporate residence is determined by incorporation in New Zealand or where the company's head office, centre of management, or director control is in New Zealand. The General Anti-Avoidance Rule in section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 may recharacterise arrangements that lack commercial substance and are undertaken for a tax avoidance purpose. For the tax-exempt IC, economic substance must be maintained in its home jurisdiction: physical office, resident directors, employees, core business decision-making, and appropriate books and records. If an IC is managed and controlled from New Zealand, it is treated as New Zealand tax resident, losing its exempt-zone status and becoming taxable on worldwide income at 28%.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Filing

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty of 150% of the tax underpaid under section 141E of the Tax Administration Act 1994; criminal prosecution with fines up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment of up to 5 years; use-of-money interest and late payment penalties apply; assessment time bar extended indefinitely in cases of evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns or direct the evasion are jointly and severally liable for the tax shortfall where the company is a façade; personal prosecution, director disqualification, asset restraint orders, and travel bans may be imposed by the court.
Shell Company and Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Recharacterisation of the IC as a New Zealand tax resident under the head-office and management control rules; worldwide income taxed at 28%; invalidation of the exempt-zone tax position under GAAR; shortfall penalties up to 150%; deregistration or strike-off by the Companies Office; cancellation of export or professional licences.

Director personal liability: Directors can have the corporate veil pierced where the IC is used as a sham; they may be held personally liable for unpaid tax, ordered to make restitution, subjected to travel bans, and prosecuted criminally for fraudulent insolvency or tax evasion.
Transfer Pricing and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may make an arm's-length adjustment and reallocate assessable income; shortfall penalties of 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for a dominant position, and 150% for intentional evasion; use-of-money interest; CbC reporting penalties up to NZ$100,000 for non-compliance; director penalty notices may attach to unpaid corporate tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who are parties to artificial profit-shifting arrangements may be personally liable for shortfall penalties, subject to disqualification, restrained from leaving New Zealand by court order, and prosecuted for money laundering or tax fraud where proceeds are moved offshore.
AML/CFT Breach and Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 of up to NZ$2 million per breach for a body corporate; individual fines up to NZ$200,000; criminal money laundering offence under the Crimes Act 1961 punishable by up to 7 years imprisonment; asset forfeiture.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly permit the movement of criminal proceeds face personal criminal liability, significant fines, confiscation orders, travel bans, and imprisonment; professional licences required for statutory inspection and QC activities may be revoked.
Repatriation and Withholding Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Anti-avoidance reassessment imposing 28% corporate tax plus NRWT at 30% on deemed dividends or at 15% on deemed interest/royalties; penalties of 20%, 40%, or 150%; use-of-money interest; refund claims for treaty relief denied if the recipient lacks substantive ownership or beneficial entitlement.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed false declarations or caused the repatriation structure to misstate its tax character can be held personally liable, face civil restitution, departure prohibition orders, and imprisonment for deliberate tax fraud.
