Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies to a New Zealand International Company (CK_IC); the company must issue and fully pay at least one ordinary share, ordinarily NZD 1.00, before the incorporation application is filed. Paid-up capital in excess of the issued consideration may be held in the company's bank account; no escrow account is mandated, but commencement of bonded warehouse operation requires the company to maintain adequate working capital and, where Customs so requires, furnish a bank bond or cash security to the New Zealand Customs Service.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Te Tari Taake) - the national tax authority of New Zealand; online registration and compliance portal: myIR (https://myir.ird.govt.nz). Corporate registration is administered by the New Zealand Companies Office (Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment), which issues the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN); customs bonded-zone licensing and export clearance are administered by the New Zealand Customs Service under the Customs and Excise Act 2018.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Register the entity at the New Zealand Companies Office (as a new New Zealand company or as an overseas company) and obtain the NZBN; this registration is integrated with Inland Revenue for tax purposes. Step 2 - Obtain the statutory IRD number (the functional TIN/GST reference): the online incorporation form permits simultaneous IRD-number creation, and non-resident companies may alternatively file form IR596 (Non-Resident Company Registration) through myIR. Step 3 - Register for GST in myIR once the taxable supply turnover exceeds or is expected to exceed NZD 60,000 in a rolling 12-month period; voluntary registration is available to exporters and zero-rated suppliers, including bonded-depot operators. Step 4 - Obtain a New Zealand Customs client code and apply to the New Zealand Customs Service for a transitional-facility / bonded-warehouse licence under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, the statutory precondition for operating a free-trade-zone bonded depot. Step 5 - Enrol for export declarations via the Trade Single Window / ESCR platform. No minimum capital is required, and 100% non-resident ownership is permitted.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Tax_Exempt_Zone (customs bonded-zone/free-trade-zone classification: GST and customs duty are suspended at the clearance staging point; the entity remains a New Zealand-resident limited-liability company subject to entity-level corporate income tax at 28% unless a specific Income Tax Act 2007 exemption is elected)

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box election. Available statutory elections for an International Company operating a bonded depot include: (i) consolidated group election under subpart FM of the Income Tax Act 2007 (wholly-owned group companies treated as a single entity for income tax); (ii) imputation credit account election and related subpart OB elections; (iii) tax loss grouping elections (subpart IA of the Income Tax Act 2007); (iv) GST group registration election (s 55 Goods and Services Tax Act 1985) and GST branch registration election (s 54); (v) hybrid mismatch rule co-ordination elections (subpart FH and subpart GZ of the Income Tax Act 2007) to prevent unintended double non-taxation; and (vi) customs licensing elections under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 (e.g., election to operate the facility as a transitional facility or bonded warehouse with approved storage arrangements). The entity-level default is full NZ taxation at 28%; no general income tax exemption is available solely by reason of operating in a bonded zone, and exporters claim relief via zero-rating and input credit rules rather than a corporate-level tax exemption.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (annual turnover threshold measured over a rolling 12-month period; mandatory once met or expected to be met; voluntary registration permitted for zero-rated exporters and bonded-depot operators)

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under s 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, zero-rating at 0% GST applies to: (a) goods exported by the supplier to a place outside New Zealand within 28 days after the time of supply; (b) goods supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand and where the goods leave New Zealand before being used or available for local consumption; and (c) services supplied to a non-resident who is not in New Zealand at the time of supply, provided the services do not relate directly to land or goods physically in New Zealand (or such NZ goods are subsequently exported) - the so-called active/passive test. A bonded-warehouse / free-trade-zone depot operator may zero-rate services rendered in respect of international cargo held under customs control where the goods are subsequently exported and the complete documentary chain is maintained. The supplier bears the burden of proof; merely intending to export, or having an address overseas, is insufficient without timely physical export evidence.

  • Customs Export Declaration (New Zealand Customs Service export entry filed via the Trade Single Window / ESCR)
  • Bill of Lading (or Air Waybill) evidencing physical departure of the goods from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank SWIFT MT103/MT202 payment confirmation or bank remittance advice showing receipt of the export sale proceeds; this reflects the 180-day documentary standard, as New Zealand has no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline)

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control (CTC), digital stamping, or fiscal API pre-clearance exists in New Zealand. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and implemented through the PEPPOL-based NZ e-Invoicing network. Inland Revenue requires only that GST returns and prescribed invoice information be made available on request; taxable periods are typically monthly (large taxpayers), two-monthly (default), or six-monthly (eligible smaller businesses). GST returns are filed electronically via myIR; no per-invoice government clearance is required.

Export zero-rating is subject to joint audit scrutiny by Inland Revenue (GST refund and zero-rating reviews) and the New Zealand Customs Service (export verification under the Customs and Excise Act 2018). If zero-rating is denied because the documentation is deficient, false, or the export never physically occurred, IRD will reassess the supply at 15% GST, clawing back the unpaid tax retrospectively, and will add use-of-money interest (s 120 Tax Administration Act 1994) and shortfall penalties (s 141 TAA): 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. The ordinary reassessment time bar is four years, extended to seven years for specified circumstances and unlimited for fraud or evasion. Directors and controllers can be jointly and severally liable for unpaid GST (s 149 TAA). Criminal penalties for evasion or false export declarations include fines up to NZD 50,000 and/or imprisonment up to five years (ss 143A and 143B TAA; Customs and Excise Act 2018), and Customs may impose monetary penalties, seize or forfeit the goods, and suspend or revoke the bonded-warehouse / transitional-facility licence.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible on both the current account and the capital account. New Zealand maintains no exchange-control approvals for trade settlements, dividend remittances, loan servicing, capital injections, or profit repatriation. There is no central-bank permit for the conversion of export receipts or the acquisition of foreign currency. Remittance hurdles are exclusively fiscal and documentary: (i) non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) applies at 15% on dividends and interest and 5% on royalties, subject to double-tax-agreement relief on an application of economic-beneficial-ownership rules; (ii) GST is chargeable on local taxable supplies at 15%, with mandatory registration where turnover exceeds NZD 60,000; and (iii) the Overseas Investment Act 2005 requires consent where a non-resident acquires or holds a 25% or greater substantial interest in significant business assets with a transaction value or asset value exceeding the legislated exemption thresholds (currently NZD 100 million for most non-residents outside elevated OECD/CPTPP thresholds), or where sensitive land is involved. A Cook Islands International Company conducting bonded-warehouse operations in a New Zealand Customs free-trade zone will in the ordinary course sit below those OIA thresholds, and because the entity is an externally incorporated non-resident company, profit remittance to shareholders is unblocked; only NRWT compliance at payment time remains a hard gate.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None apply. New Zealand legislation imposes no statutory timeline for the repatriation of export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio into NZD, and no obligation to maintain foreign-currency balances with domestic registered banks. Export receipts may be retained in any currency in any bank account anywhere in the world, and conversion to NZD is entirely at the corporate treasury's discretion. The sole regulatory overlay is the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, which obliges reporting entities that receive or transfer funds in New Zealand to report prescribed cross-border electronic transactions and suspicious activity where individual transactions meet or exceed NZD 15,000, or where structured transacting is detected.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), the central bank and prudential regulator established by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021 (which superseded the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989). Foreign-exchange administration is not vested in a separate exchange-control bureau; the only inward-investment screening is conducted under the Overseas Investment Act 2005, administered through the Toitū Te Whenua Land Information New Zealand (LINZ) Overseas Investment Office function. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) supervises conduct and market licensing under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, including derivative issuer and financial advice regimes that govern treasury hedging products.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium feasibility with significant legal constraints. Offshore digital electronic-money institutions (EMIs) and non-bank payment service providers may service the CK_IC treasury only if registered on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008, enrolled in an approved dispute-resolution scheme, and independently supervised for AML/CFT obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Pure offshore EMIs that merely route funds into New Zealand without local registration cannot access the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) or the Payments NZ Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS); access to those clearing rails is restricted to registered banks and approved settlement institutions. Accordingly, offshore EMIs are viable only as low-value operational float wallets and for disbursement automation; they are not viable for capital escrow accounts, customs-guarantee bonding, documentary credit issuance, or licensed derivative hedging. A prudent treasury taxonomy therefore treats fintech EMI onboarding as 'Medium' for legal feasibility but 'Low' for strategic utility in the bonded-depot context.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Shareholder equity contributions must be routed as SWIFT MT103 customer-credit transfers from the Cook Islands executing bank to the New Zealand receiving bank's nostro concentration account, using the ISO 20022 purpose code 'EQUI' (equity contribution) or the legacy SWIFT Category-8 purpose 'G' (general) with the field-70 narrative citing the company's identity and the purpose 'equity contribution for free-trade-zone bonded depot capital plant'. The receiving Tier-1 bank will require a source-of-funds questionnaire, a certified board resolution from the registered agent, and a funds-flow diagram demonstrating that monies originate from identifiable shareholder accounts. Where the inbound capital is destined for a Customs licence-bond or statutory guarantee, the bank will tag the funds to the capital escrow ledger until the Customs call-bid is executed. No exchange-control approval or export-surrender certificate is required for the conversion into NZD; conversion is executed as an open-market spot trade with value date T+0 or T+1, settling ultimately through the Reserve Bank's Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) in central-bank money. If the capital injection exceeds the Overseas Investment Act 2005 significant-business-assets thresholds in combination with a 25% or greater substantial interest, an Overseas Investment Office consent condition would be a precondition to account activation; the standard bonded-depot capital injection, being below those thresholds, remains OIA-exempt and does not require ministerial authorisation.Bonded-depot treasury disbursements are executed across three simultaneous clearing rails. First, SWIFT MT103 irrevocable customer credit transfers for high-frequency, low-value supplier and logistics payments, originated in New Zealand's institutional channel and settled cross-border via correspondent banks with real-time gross settlement in the destination jurisdictions. Second, Commercial Letters of Credit issued by the New Zealand corporate bank under ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600), with Incoterms 2020 embedded in each documentary credit application, accommodating 30-to-90-day tenors for containerised agricultural, fisheries, and food inventory sourced from ASEAN, Pacific, and European counterparties; documents are negotiated against the IC's warehouse receipts and duty-deferred customs entries. Third, New Zealand local clearing through the Payments NZ Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) for Customs duty and GST payments to the New Zealand Customs Service, port and stevedoring tariffs, rail and trucking freight claims, and Customs bond guarantee servicing; BECS debit batches settle on a deferred-net basis through ESAS on T+1. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility with significant legal constraints. Offshore digital electronic-money institutions (EMIs) and non-bank payment service providers may service the CK_IC treasury only if registered on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008, enrolled in an approved dispute-resolution scheme, and independently supervised for AML/CFT obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Pure offshore EMIs that merely route funds into New Zealand without local registration cannot access the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) or the Payments NZ Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS); access to those clearing rails is restricted to registered banks and approved settlement institutions. Accordingly, offshore EMIs are viable only as low-value operational float wallets and for disbursement automation; they are not viable for capital escrow accounts, customs-guarantee bonding, documentary credit issuance, or licensed derivative hedging. A prudent treasury taxonomy therefore treats fintech EMI onboarding as 'Medium' for legal feasibility but 'Low' for strategic utility in the bonded-depot context.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% flat corporate income tax rate under the Income Tax Act 2007

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on gross dividends paid by a New Zealand company to non-residents. Under many double tax agreements the rate is reduced to 15% for portfolio dividends and 5% for substantial corporate shareholdings.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The Inland Revenue requires contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation where aggregate annual cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZ$1,000,000. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required where the multinational group has consolidated group revenue of NZ$1,200,000,000 or more (aligned with the OECD CbC threshold of EUR 750,000,000).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply standalone Economic Substance Regulations of the type used in BVI/Cayman. However, the general anti-avoidance rule in the Income Tax Act 2007 denies tax advantages from arrangements that lack commercial substance and have a dominant tax-avoidance purpose. A bonded depot company must demonstrate real substance in New Zealand: premises, employees, management, business records, bank accounts and actual commercial activity. Treaty relief, loss continuity and free-zone benefits can be denied if the entity is found to be a shell or conduit without substantive operations.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False or Misleading Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal liability under section 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994, punishable by imprisonment for up to five years. Administrative shortfall penalties may be up to 150% of the resulting tax shortfall where evasion is established, plus use-of-money interest from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly participate in or authorise the conduct may be personally prosecuted and imprisoned. Civil proceedings may be brought to recover the tax shortfall against directors, and serious cases can lead to travel bans, restraint orders and disqualification from managing companies.
Abusive Tax Avoidance / GAAR

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may reconstruct the arrangement under the general anti-avoidance rule. A shortfall penalty of up to 100% applies for an abusive tax position, plus interest and late payment penalties. Treaty benefits may be denied under the limitation of benefit / principal purpose tests.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false or misleading documents, or who direct the avoidance structure, can be personally liable for resultant tax obligations if the company is stripped of assets. Courts may pierce the corporate veil where the company is a mere facade, exposing directors to civil recovery and penalty orders.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Tax adjustments to reflect arm's-length conditions under the Income Tax Act 2007. Shortfall penalties may be imposed: 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 100% for abusive or evasive positions, plus interest. Inland Revenue may also issue pecuniary penalty orders for deliberate transfer pricing non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable if they knowingly provided false transfer pricing documentation or failed to ensure the company's tax obligations were met. In insolvency scenarios, directors can face voidable transaction and reckless trading liability under the Companies Act 1993.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may disregard the entity as a sham, deny treaty benefits, reconstruct the arrangement, and impose shortfall penalties up to 100% of the tax avoided. The company may also be struck off under the Companies Act 1993 for failure to carry on business.

Director personal liability: Criminal prosecution may extend to directors, shadow directors, and nominee shareholders. Directors can be made personally liable for restitution of tax shortfalls, and the court may order forfeiture of assets, travel bans, and imprisonment where the sham involved dishonesty.
Bonded Depot / Customs and AML Violations

Statutory sanction: Penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 1996 including forfeiture of goods, customs penalties, and fines. AML/CFT breaches under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 can attract civil penalties of up to NZ$2,000,000 for a body corporate and criminal fines. Money laundering offences under the Crimes Act 1961 carry imprisonment of up to seven years.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers are personally exposed to criminal prosecution for money laundering and for knowingly engaging in customs fraud. The courts may impose personal fines, confiscation orders, travel restrictions, and custodial sentences. Directors responsible for compliance failures can also be separately sanctioned under the AML/CFT supervised-entity regime.
