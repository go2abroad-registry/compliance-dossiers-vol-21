Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department of New Zealand (Inland Revenue) via the myIR online portal (https://www.ird.govt.nz/myir); for export and customs clearance, co-administration by the New Zealand Customs Service using the Trade Single Window.

E-government filing portal

Under the Tax Administration Act 1994 and the Companies Act 1993, a New Zealand incorporated NZ_LTD obtains its IRD number via the Companies Office registration process, which shares the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and incorporation details with Inland Revenue, after which the Commissioner issues the IRD number. For a foreign non-resident company not incorporated in New Zealand, the entity must first determine whether it is carrying on a taxable activity or has a New Zealand permanent establishment; if so, it must apply on myIR through the Commissioner's prescribed electronic application for a non-resident company IRD number. The application must include: (i) the certificate of incorporation or equivalent foreign corporate registration deed, (ii) constitutional documents or articles of association, (iii) a board resolution appointing a New Zealand agent, branch manager or tax representative where applicable, (iv) certified passport and tax residence declarations for all directors and ultimate beneficial owners, (v) a New Zealand business address and bank account mandate, and (vi) evidence of the parent company or shareholder register. Once verified, Inland Revenue issues the IRD number and the entity is placed on the income tax register. For GST, the entity must separately apply for GST registration on myIR under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 if the taxable turnover threshold is met or if voluntary registration is needed for refunds or zero-rating; non-resident suppliers of remote services to New Zealand residents use the simplified non-resident GST registration channel.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in a 12-month period under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, including certain non-resident remote services supplied to New Zealand residents; registration is mandatory once the historical or prospective 12-month turnover exceeds the threshold, and voluntary registration is available where the Commissioner is satisfied that the entity carries on a taxable activity.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 when a registered person supplies goods and the goods are physically exported from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply. Services are zero-rated under section 11A when supplied to a non-resident recipient outside New Zealand, provided the services are not directly in connection with land in New Zealand, are not physically performed in New Zealand, and the recipient is outside New Zealand at the time of supply. For the specialty tea salon and lounge, on-site retail food and beverage supply is standard-rated at 15% GST; export sales of loose-leaf tea, ceramics and tea ware can be zero-rated as goods if physical export and all statutory evidence are completed within the 28-day rule. There is no separate tourist GST refund administration; rather, suppliers may zero-rate goods that are exported as accompanied baggage only when the prescribed export evidence is obtained and retained.

  • Export declaration or export entry lodged electronically with the New Zealand Customs Service through the Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing dispatch from New Zealand and destination abroad
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the exported tea, ceramics or goods and their value
  • Bank credit advice, SWIFT MT103, or foreign exchange receipt showing the export proceeds received in the New Zealand bank account (the functional equivalent of an inward remittance certificate)
  • Proof of non-resident status of the recipient for any zero-rated service supply, including the customer's name, overseas address and tax residency declaration

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory invoice stamping, or anti-fraud digital stamp regime exists in New Zealand. GST returns must be filed electronically through myIR. Standard GST invoices must satisfy section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 by showing the supplier name and GST number, invoice date, description, consideration and GST amount. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and is transmitted over the New Zealand Peppol network; there is no mandatory API-based fiscal clearance.

Inland Revenue may retrospectively assess output GST at 15% on any supply previously zero-rated if the supporting export and proceeds evidence is not maintained, with the general four-year reassessment period under the Tax Administration Act 1994, and no time limit where there is fraud or evasion. Shortfall penalties under Part 7 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply at 20% for failure to take reasonable care or an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion; use-of-money interest is also charged from the original due date. Knowingly false or fraudulent export zero-rating claims can give rise to criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994, and directors or responsible officers may be held personally liable where the false GST return was knowingly caused or permitted. To sustain export zero-rating, the complete customs declaration bundle, transport documentation and foreign exchange credit evidence must therefore remain available to Inland Revenue for audit inspection, with the business policy requiring all proceeds to be reconciled within 180 days of export.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised and freely floating foreign exchange regime. There are no exchange controls, no mandatory conversion requirements, and no restrictions on current account payments for trade in goods and services, interest, dividends, royalties, or capital account movements such as direct investment, portfolio investment, loans, and repatriation of share capital. The New Zealand dollar is fully convertible on both the current and capital accounts. No permission from the Reserve Bank of New Zealand is required for cross-border transfers, and no prior approval is needed for foreign investors to establish or fund a limited liability company, except for transactions involving sensitive land, significant business assets, or fishing quota under the Overseas Investment Act 2005. Profit remittances by a non-resident shareholder are tax-deductible if subject to non-resident withholding tax and are freely transferable without surrender requirements. Exporters are not required to surrender export receipts, and there is no FX rationing, no multiple exchange rate regime, and no settlement restriction on converting NZD back to home currency. The practical impediment is not regulation but standard bank de-risking due to AML/CFT compliance; therefore documentary evidence of source-of-funds and beneficial ownership must be robust. Overall, convertibility is high and the external accounts are fully open, allowing a specialty tea salon to move capital and operational funds in and out of the country without central bank repurchase or surrender hurdles.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory export proceeds surrender rule in New Zealand. Exporters may retain 100% of foreign currency receipts in offshore or onshore accounts, may freely convert proceeds to NZD, or may use them directly for third-party trade settlement, provided the underlying transactions are accurately declared to Customs New Zealand and supported by commercial invoices for AML and Goods and Services Tax (GST) purposes.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) under the delegated foreign exchange administration framework of the Foreign Exchange Control Regulations 1977, administered jointly with the New Zealand Treasury

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) are legally constrained in New Zealand because deposit-taking and issuing of demand liabilities require registration as a bank under the Prudential Regulation Framework of the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. A general-purpose EMI license comparable to the UK or Singapore model does not exist. Offshore EMIs may only act as payment facilitators or prepaid instrument providers if they partner with a licensed New Zealand bank, but they cannot independently hold corporate treasury balances, provide full settlement accounts, or issue overdraft facilities. Their practical viability is Low for primary receipt of share capital and for critical clearing rails, but Medium as an auxiliary payment gateway for e-commerce orders if the underlying cash settlement is held at a tier-1 local bank. For this treasury blueprint, the specialty tea salon must open a primary corporate operating account at a locally incorporated bank and may use EMIs only for low-value card processing or merchant aggregator services, never for capital escrow, supplier settlement, or FX conversion.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound share capital should be wired via SWIFT MT103 from the beneficial owner's personal or corporate bank account to the company's NZD capital escrow account at an RBNZ-registered commercial bank. The wire must include the ISO 20022 purpose code or a narrative expressly identifying the transfer as 'share subscription consideration', 'equity contribution', or 'loan to subsidiary', along with the New Zealand Companies Register company number. For values exceeding NZD 50,000, banks require a completed Source of Funds and Source of Wealth declaration. If the capital originates from China, Hong Kong, or other jurisdictions with FX reporting systems, the remitter must confirm the local bank registration and purpose code so that the funds are not mischaracterised as trade payment. After receipt, the bank may hold the capital in escrow until the company issues share certificates and files the shareholder register with the Companies Office. A New Zealand chartered accountant may issue a capital confirmation report supported by real-time bank statements, the board resolution, and correspondent bank MT103 trail. For loans from shareholders, the loan agreement must be notarised and the interest rate should be arm's length to avoid thin capitalisation and withholding tax on royalties or interest. Any physical cash injection is prohibited under AML/CFT.Outbound trade settlements for specialty tea purchases, custom ceramic ware, packaging, and logistics services should be executed primarily through SWIFT MT103 for high-value transactions where the supplier requires direct bank-to-bank credit. For tea imports from origin markets with established trade relationships, an irrevocable at-sight documentary letter of credit (L/C) with a confirmed correspondent bank provides security to the seller and an audit trail for clearing. For repeat medium-volume shipments, documentary collections (CAD or D/P) may reduce transaction costs but provide weaker working capital protection; therefore they are acceptable only for trusted counterparties with no prior discrepancy history. All trade disbursements must be supported by commercial invoices, packing lists, certificates of origin, and, where required for agricultural goods, phytosanitary certificates and food safety documentation. Payment to a related party or to a third-party commission agent is treated as high-risk and will require the same evidential standard as inbound capital. For this treasury blueprint, the specialty tea salon must open a primary corporate operating account at a locally incorporated bank and may use EMIs only for low-value card processing or merchant aggregator services, never for capital escrow, supplier settlement, or FX conversion.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZ$100 million group-wide revenue threshold; cross-border related-party goods/services transactions exceeding NZ$10 million, or cross-border related-party financing transactions exceeding NZ$5 million, trigger the requirement to maintain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation including a Master File and Local File.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to offshore financial centres. However, the IRD applies general anti-avoidance rules under the Income Tax Act 2007, requires substantive economic activity for tax residence and permanent establishment analysis, and insists that treaty benefits are available only to beneficial owners with genuine business substance. A shell company holding assets or receiving passive income without staff, premises, or business activity in New Zealand will be vulnerable to anti-avoidance reconstruction and treaty denial.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty equal to 150% of the evaded tax, plus use-of-money interest. Criminal conviction under Section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 can result in a fine of up to NZ$250,000 and imprisonment for up to five years.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false returns or knowingly participate in tax evasion face personal criminal liability, disqualification from managing companies, and orders for reparation or restitution. The corporate veil may be pierced where business is carried on reckantly under the Companies Act 1993.
Abusive Tax Avoidance

Statutory sanction: IRD may invoke the general anti-avoidance rule (s BG1 and s GA1 of the Income Tax Act 2007), disregard the offending arrangement, reconstruct taxable income, and impose a 100% shortfall penalty for abusive tax avoidance, plus an additional 20% for taking an unacceptable tax position.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly enter into or promote an abusive tax arrangement can be held personally liable for the resulting tax shortfall and penalties. Courts may order civil restitution and impose travel bans pending tax fraud or money laundering investigations.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: IRD may adjust profits to the arm's length standard, deny deductions, and impose shortfall penalties of 40% for an unacceptable tax position or 100% for abusive tax avoidance, plus interest. Certain penalties may be reduced if contemporaneous documentation is supplied on request.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign transfer pricing statements known to be false or who cause the company to be used as an instrument of profit shifting can face personal civil penalties and, in egregious cases, criminal prosecution for evasion of tax.
Shell Company and AML/CTF Abuse

Statutory sanction: AML/CTF breaches can result in fines up to NZ$2 million per entity and civil penalty orders. IRD may disregard the shell arrangement for tax purposes, deny treaty benefits, and impose shortfall penalties. Money laundering offences may lead to imprisonment for up to seven years.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted under AML/CTF legislation if they knowingly permit suspicious transactions without reporting. Freezing orders, travel bans, extradition, and disqualification from holding directorships are available where the company is used as a vehicle for financial crime.
Chronic Non-Filing and Default Assessment

Statutory sanction: IRD may issue default assessments under section 107B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, impose late filing penalties, late payment penalties, and use-of-money interest, and register a charge over company assets. Persistent non-filing can lead to strike-off and deregistration of the company.

Director personal liability: Directors can be made personally liable for unpaid GST and PAYE withheld from employees if they allowed the company to trade while unable to meet those obligations. Illegal trading provisions under the Companies Act 1993 expose directors to personal liability and disqualification.
