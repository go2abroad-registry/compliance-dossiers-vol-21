Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None required under New Zealand law. The Companies Act 1993 imposes no minimum issued or paid-up share capital and there is no escrowed or blocked capital account rule. Shares may have no par value and may be issued for consideration in cash, intangible property, or services, provided the board records the allotment and ensures the solvency test is satisfied before any distributions, share redemptions, or capital reductions.

Funding & unlock path: Not Applicable for the virtual-only retail operation. The company must nonetheless comply with the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 by ensuring a safe remote or office working environment, providing first-aid facilities, reporting notifiable injuries and illnesses, and maintaining a health and safety policy appropriate to a small e-commerce operation. No Civil Defence fire safety certificate, structural load inspection, or workplace safety approval is required for home offices or small administrative premises. If the company later opens a physical showroom or warehouse, it will need a building warrant of fitness, fire evacuation plan, Emergency Lighting and Automatic Fire Alarm certification where required, and compliance with the Building Act 2004 and approved fire safety codes.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD/Inland Revenue) is the national tax authority; online portal is myIR (myIR.ird.govt.nz). Company legal registration is through the New Zealand Companies Office (companies-register.companiesoffice.govt.nz), an operational arm of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment. Customs matters require registration with the New Zealand Customs Service and use of its Trade Single Window electronic border system.

E-government filing portal

For an NZ limited company owned by non-residents: (1) incorporate with the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993, receiving a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and certificate of incorporation; (2) apply to Inland Revenue for an IRD number by using the myIR business registration and non-resident company application pathway, submitting the certificate of incorporation, constitution, registered office, director and ultimate shareholder/control information, and confirming the New Zealand agent or address details required for non-resident-owned entities; the IRD number is the single statutory TIN for income tax, GST and other obligations; (3) register for GST in myIR once taxable supplies are expected to exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period, or voluntarily if carrying on a taxable activity; Inland Revenue may require an overseas-owned company to provide security or evidence of business substance; (4) for export of physical goods, obtain a Customs Client Code from the New Zealand Customs Service and, for each consignment, lodge the electronic Customs Export Entry/export declaration through the Trade Single Window at or before departure.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box regime. A closely held qualifying company may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, but an LTC may not have non-resident shareholders, so this non-resident-owned NZ Ltd cannot use the election. VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 per 12-month period; mandatory registration is triggered once total taxable supplies of goods or services connected with or made in New Zealand exceed this threshold, and voluntary registration is available to any person carrying on a taxable activity.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated at 0% GST where the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand, or supplies goods to a non-resident recipient who removes or arranges removal of the goods from New Zealand, provided the goods are not consumed or used in New Zealand and the statutory export evidence is available at the time of filing and the goods are physically exported within the statutory period, generally within 28 days of the date of supply under section 11(1) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. For a fast-fashion e-commerce retailer, the relevant zero-rating base is the cross-border sale of physical apparel and footwear exported directly by the supplier to the overseas customer; qualifying remote/digital services supplied to a non-resident recipient outside New Zealand are likewise zero-rated under the place-of-supply and services-to-non-resident rules in the GST Act where the services are not supplied through a New Zealand establishment or to a person in New Zealand.

  • Electronic Customs Export Declaration/Export Entry lodged with the New Zealand Customs Service through the Trade Single Window, with departure confirmation or customs endorsement
  • Bill of Lading for sea freight, Air Waybill for air freight, or courier export manifest for e-commerce parcels, showing the New Zealand consignor and the foreign consignee/delivery destination
  • Commercial invoice and packing list corresponding to the exported goods and the GST tax invoice
  • Bank inward remittance evidence such as SWIFT MT103/MT202 or a bank foreign-exchange transaction advice proving receipt of payment from the non-resident buyer; New Zealand does not issue a statutory Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC)
  • Internal dispatch and order records showing the date of supply and the date of export, sufficient to demonstrate the goods left New Zealand within the statutory zero-rating period

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory API stamping, or anti-fraud digital stamping applies in New Zealand. E-invoicing is voluntary and follows the Peppol interoperability framework (PEPPOL BIS 3.0). A GST-registered supplier must issue a GST tax invoice containing the particulars required by section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, including supplier identity, GST registration number, date of supply, description, quantity, price, and for zero-rated exports the substantive zero-rating rationale. Export declarations are made to the New Zealand Customs Service independently of GST invoicing; Inland Revenue does not pre-clear invoices.

Inland Revenue may audit export zero-rating by inspecting customs export records, transport documents, bank remittance evidence and commercial contracts. If a zero-rating claim cannot be substantiated, GST at 15% is reassessed on the supply and use-of-money interest is charged from the original return due date. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 include up to 20% for lack of reasonable care or an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or similar conduct. Failure to retain the mandatory export and payment documentation can cause retrospective clawback of zero-rating, reassessment, penalties, and in serious cases criminal prosecution for GST evasion. New Zealand has no fixed 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, but absence of bank remittance evidence is a significant audit red flag and will impair the reliability of the export zero-rating documentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a freely convertible currency across both the current account and capital account. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not impose exchange controls, mandatory conversion requirements, or withholding on current-account transactions such as trade payments, service fees, royalties, and dividends. Profits and repatriated capital may be remitted without foreign exchange licensing or prior central bank approval, subject only to normal commercial bank anti-money-laundering verification and tax withholding obligations such as resident withholding tax on dividends and non-resident withholding tax on interest. Capital-account transactions, including direct equity injections, share subscription proceeds, and shareholder loans, are also unrestricted; however, acquisitions that would confer control over sensitive assets, such as farm land or material fisheries, may require consent under the Overseas Investment Act. A fast-fashion retail e-commerce entity does not typically require this level of approval because its underlying operations are commercial property leases and digital assets. Commercial banks handle inward and outward remittances under documented economic purpose, performing standard compliance screening. The absence of a surrender requirement means exporters and retailers can hold NZD and foreign currency balances in accounts freely; no foreign exchange balance sheets need to be declared to the Reserve Bank. Exchange rates are determined by free market flows, and the Reserve Bank's remit is monetary stability, not currency rationing. Banks are also permitted to operate multi-currency accounts for eligible customers, enabling treasury managers to hedge and retain export earnings in foreign currency without regulatory friction. Overall, the regime is on the extremely liberal end of the spectrum, with only sanctions screening and genuine verification obligations serving as the de facto guardrails.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export foreign currency receipts under New Zealand law. Exporters and e-commerce merchants may hold 100% of their export proceeds in any foreign currency for an indefinite period, or convert immediately to NZD at a negotiated commercial rate. Banking arrangements may require a signed declaration on the intended purpose of foreign currency balances, and the Inland Revenue Act requires revenue recognition in NZD for tax reporting, but no surrender or repatriation mandate exists in either the Reserve Bank of New Zealand or Customs and Excise regulations. Therefore, for this turbo-fast-fashion entity, treasury policy can freely retain USD and EUR receivables for just-in-time inventory settlement without central bank conversion obligations.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Putea Matua) acting as the central bank, prudential regulator for registered banks, and formal administrator for the Exchange Settlement Account System, with supplementary foreign investment screening for sensitive land and fishing quota under the Overseas Investment Office

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Fintech electronic money institutions are viable and not legally barred in New Zealand, provided they are registered on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and licensed in their home jurisdiction as a bank or non-bank deposit taker. For this low-risk fast-fashion apparel retail entity, EMIs such as Wise Business, Airwallex, or Payoneer can be legitimately employed as auxiliary rails for multi-currency payout to influencers, unpaid customs deposits, and cross-border card acquiring settlement (especially when linked to Shopify Payments). However, a principal treasury relationship with a Tier-1 domestic bank is strongly recommended. EMIs are typically not authorised to provide overdraft facilities, professional FX hedging, or trust-grade capital escrow services. They also generally lack the physical branch infrastructure to complete complex KYC on discretionary shareholders, and their deposit insurance coverage is not provided by New Zealand's statutory scheme. Therefore, the feasibility rating is High for ancillary foreign-exchange and payment settlement use, but Low for serving as the core banking relationship intended to hold statutory capital and facilitate audited capital verification. All offshore EMI accounts should be ring-fenced, with daily sweep sweeps into the Tier-1 NZD account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For an equity injection from an overseas parent or investor into the New Zealand operational company, the sender should execute a SWIFT MT103 with the beneficiary being the NZ Ltd's Tier-1 account at a domestic bank. The field 70 (remittance information) must clearly state 'Share Capital Injection, Invoice Ref #, Contract #' to satisfy both the Reserve Bank and the bank's transaction monitoring team. All incoming foreign-exchange wires are cleared over the ESAS system for high-value NZD settlements, while the paying bank will convert the funds at the prevailing wholesale exchange rate. No foreign exchange approval is necessary, but the bank will request the underlying subscription agreement for reporting. After settlement, the NZ company's auditor will prepare a capital verification certificate referencing the bank's confirmation of funds, the Companies Office share registry update, and any Schedule 24 foreign investment waiver if applicable. Funds should be parked in a separate Capital Escrow Account to facilitate traceability, then transferred into the operational account only after capital verification is complete. Any subsequent withdrawal for working capital must be documented in the board minutes as use of capital in the commercial supply chain.The principal clearing rails for trade settlements will be SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier payments in USD/RMB/EUR for garment manufacturing, through the NZ bank's correspondent network. For high-value procurements (above USD 25,000), a commercial letter of credit (L/C) issued by the NZ bank on an irrevocable basis provides supplier security, with a typical tenor of 15-30 days against presentation of clean shipping documents. For medium-value and repeat shipments, documentary collections (D/P or D/A) are advisable to lower bank fees and retain payment risk with the exporter. Fast-fashion settlement through Shopify Payments and Klarna BNPL is a separate rail: funds are collected by the payment processor as the acquiring bank, net settled into the NZ entity's settlement account on a T+1 or T+2 basis. For outbound refunds to customers, the merchant may use automated ACH/Payto rails on NZ-led bank accounts, but for cross-border refunds, a SWIFT MT103 is required. We recommend establishing an escrow buffer account with a target of 8% of average quarterly card volumes to manage chargeback clawbacks without squeezing core liquidity. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Fintech electronic money institutions are viable and not legally barred in New Zealand, provided they are registered on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and licensed in their home jurisdiction as a bank or non-bank deposit taker. For this low-risk fast-fashion apparel retail entity, EMIs such as Wise Business, Airwallex, or Payoneer can be legitimately employed as auxiliary rails for multi-currency payout to influencers, unpaid customs deposits, and cross-border card acquiring settlement (especially when linked to Shopify Payments). However, a principal treasury relationship with a Tier-1 domestic bank is strongly recommended. EMIs are typically not authorised to provide overdraft facilities, professional FX hedging, or trust-grade capital escrow services. They also generally lack the physical branch infrastructure to complete complex KYC on discretionary shareholders, and their deposit insurance coverage is not provided by New Zealand's statutory scheme. Therefore, the feasibility rating is High for ancillary foreign-exchange and payment settlement use, but Low for serving as the core banking relationship intended to hold statutory capital and facilitate audited capital verification. All offshore EMI accounts should be ring-fenced, with daily sweep sweeps into the Tier-1 NZD account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 60 million annual turnover: prepare Transfer Pricing Documentation (Local File) for cross-border related-party transactions. Country-by-Country Reporting threshold: consolidated group revenue of NZD 1.15 billion (EUR 750 million equivalent). Mandatory contemporaneous documentation for related-party transactions exceeding NZD 10 million in aggregate per income year.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not adopt Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations. Substance is instead tested through: (i) the general anti-avoidance rule in BG 1 ITA 2007, which recharacterises artificial arrangements; (ii) the permanent establishment concept under GB 1; (iii) the 49% continuity rule for loss utilisation; and (iv) the management-and-control test for corporate residency. For a fast-fashion e-commerce company, physical presence, local directors, employees, inventory warehousing, and decision-making in NZ are dispositive commercial substance evidence.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (TAA 1994 ss 143A, 143B)

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax; criminal conviction with a fine of up to NZD 50,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years; additional fine of up to three times the tax evaded. Publication of the conviction is also common.

Director personal liability: Under s 37 TAA, directors may be personally liable for the company's unpaid PAYE, GST, and withholding taxes. Criminal liability may be imposed on directing minds. Courts may pierce the corporate veil to order restitution.
Shell Company / Sham Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Recharacterisation by Inland Revenue under BG 1/GB 1: all income attributed to the foreign controlling entity, loss offsets denied, and a 40% abusive tax position shortfall penalty under s 141D.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed declarations of solvency without reasonable grounds are jointly and severally liable under ss 137-138 Companies Act 1994. Courts can order directors to compensate the company for diverted assets.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Transfer-pricing adjustment to the arm's-length price; shortfall penalty of 20% (or 40% where the position is not more likely than not to be correct); use-of-money interest accrues from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly execute the offshore pricing scheme face personal penalties under s 143B TAA. Inland Revenue may lift the corporate veil to pursue the foreign parent for outstanding tax debts.
AML / Misleading Financial Reporting

Statutory sanction: Fines up to NZD 200,000 under the Financial Reporting Act 2013 and Companies Act 1994 for false statements. Under the AML/CFT Act 2009, if the BNPL or payment activity requires a licence, penalties reach NZD 2 million per offence for the company, and up to 5 years imprisonment for officers.

Director personal liability: Directors may be disqualified from management for up to 10 years. For reckless trading or fraudulent trading, directors are personally liable to creditors for the full amount of the company's debts.
