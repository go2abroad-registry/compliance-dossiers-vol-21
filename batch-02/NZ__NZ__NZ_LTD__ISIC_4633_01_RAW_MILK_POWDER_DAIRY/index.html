15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period for supplies made in NZ and for remote services supplied by offshore suppliers to NZ resident consumers; once the threshold is met or exceeded, registration is mandatory under sections 51 and 54B of the GST Act 1985, with a statutory right to claim input tax from the effective date of registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Section 11(1) of the GST Act 1985 zero-rates the supply if the goods are exported by the supplier (or by a carrier at the supplier's direction) and the supplier holds appropriate evidence that the goods have been physically removed from New Zealand; where the export is not completed before the time of supply, the goods must leave New Zealand no later than 28 days after the time of supply. For qualifying services supplied by a NZ supplier to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply, zero-rating is available under section 11(1)(m) unless the services are supplied directly in connection with NZ land or physically performed in NZ. For a raw milk powder commodity seller, the sale is zero-rated as a supply of exported goods only if legal title and risk transfer are consistent with the trade terms, the goods are shipped by the NZ_LTD to a foreign buyer, an NZ Customs export declaration is accepted, and evidence of departure is retained in the GST record.

  • New Zealand Customs Service export entry in the Trade Single Window (electronic export declaration) showing the NZ exporter of record, HS code, quantity, FOB/invoice value, and proof that Customs clearance was granted for physical departure from New Zealand
  • Original negotiable bill of lading or sea/air waybill evidencing loading at a NZ port/airport, foreign discharge destination, dated shipping date, and consignee outside New Zealand; the 28-day export deadline must be visible in the shipment date
  • Commercial invoice and packing list cross-referenced to the GST invoice, IRD number, and export entry reference, with incoterms and Peppol or EDIFACT reference where applicable
  • Container gate-out or terminal receipt, Customs cargo report, or overseas port arrival document proving the goods were not diverted for domestic sale
  • Bank-issued SWIFT MT103, TT credit advice, or documentary credit utilisation advice from a New Zealand registered bank, evidencing receipt of export proceeds in foreign currency; no New Zealand FIRC exists because New Zealand does not operate exchange controls
  • If the dairy product is regulated, Ministry for Primary Industries (MPI) registration and clearance documents, the exporter's MPI dairy exporter registration, and any official assurance certificate required for the destination market

New Zealand has no real-time tax clearance, API pre-clearance, or anti-fraud digital stamping mandate. E-invoicing is voluntary and operates through the IRD-approved Peppol BIS 3.0 network for interoperability; government agencies are required to accept e-invoices. GST-registered suppliers must issue GST tax invoices meeting sections 24-24E of the GST Act 1985, retain these records in New Zealand to establish time of supply, and file periodic GST returns and payments via myIR. There is no statutory requirement to transmit invoice-level data to IRD before or at invoice issuance.

IRD requires the exporter to retain full export evidence for each zero-rated supply and will disallow the zero-rating if any statutory condition is not met, re-assessing GST at 15% on the full supply price. The unextinguished GST becomes a tax shortfall plus use-of-money interest from the original GST due date. Tax Administration Act 1994 shortfall penalties apply: 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 50% for an abusive tax position, and 100% for evasion, with available reductions of up to 75% for prompt voluntary disclosure. The statutory assessment time-bar is four years from the filing date, with no time bar where evasion or fraud exists. Additionally, false export declarations to NZ Customs can be prosecuted under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 and dishonesty offences under the Crimes Act 1961. New Zealand does not impose exchange-control repatriation deadlines; the critical statutory deadline for export zero-rating is that goods must be physically exported or delivered to a carrier for export within 28 days after the time of supply.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised current and capital account framework under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the Foreign Investment Regime. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely convertible with no exchange control restrictions, no surrender requirements, and no prior approval for current account transactions or capital movements. Profit remittance, dividend distributions, and proceeds from the liquidation of investments may be repatriated without restriction, subject only to the standard company law solvency test and tax clearance from Inland Revenue for non-resident withholding tax on dividends and royalties. Anti-money laundering and countering financing of terrorism (AML/CFT) obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 apply to all reporting entities, meaning banks require full evidentiary transparency but do not impose FX surrender or conversion ratios. The only practical hurdles are documentary in nature: banks need to verify the underlying trade, beneficial ownership, and source of funds to satisfy due diligence. Capital account transactions, such as intercompany loans or equity injections, are reportable for balance-of-payments statistics but not subject to permission. Therefore, the jurisdiction poses minimal FX regulatory friction for a dairy commodity trading entity, provided the entity maintains proper tax residency, commercial substance, and audited financial records.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory surrender requirements or mandatory conversion timelines for export foreign currency receipts in New Zealand. Exporters may retain proceeds in NZD or any freely convertible foreign currency, hold them in offshore or onshore accounts, and repatriate them at their own discretion, subject only to bank AML/CFT screening and the reporting of transactions above NZD 10,000 for suspicious matter reporting.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI (electronic money institution) accounts are not prohibited by New Zealand law, but their viability is constrained. A New Zealand-incorporated limited company may open an account with a non-resident EMI only if the EMI holds a valid license in its own jurisdiction and the service provider is not a New Zealand reporting entity. However, a dairy commodity trader of raw milk powder requires full banking services: trade finance instruments (documentary letters of credit), SWIFT MT103 wires, and FX hedging. Offshore EMIs generally cannot issue or confirm commercial LCs, cannot provide NZD settlement through the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) operated by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, and may not be recognised as an eligible bank by foreign counterparties or New Zealand clearing systems. Furthermore, most credible EMIs are reluctant to service a physically delinked trading entity without a local banking relationship, due to correspondent bank de-risking. Therefore, EMI viability is rated Low as a primary clearing rail and Medium only as a supplementary payment corridor for small operational disbursements, provided the EMI performs full KYC and maintains segregated client funds.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections into the New Zealand limited company must be structured either as paid-up share capital or as shareholder loans with a formal loan agreement. For share capital, the subscription funds should be wired via SWIFT MT103 from the disclosing shareholder's own bank account in the same legal name as the shareholder, using payment purpose code S101 (Issuance of shares) or S301 (Inward direct investment) as recommended by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's balance of payments coding manual. The bank will require a board resolution authorising the allotment, a share register update, and confirmation that the funds are not derived from criminal activity. For capital verification under the Companies Act 1993, the company must file an annual return with the New Zealand Companies Office, and the bank's opening deposit must match the authorised share capital. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Offshore digital EMI (electronic money institution) accounts are not prohibited by New Zealand law, but their viability is constrained. A New Zealand-incorporated limited company may open an account with a non-resident EMI only if the EMI holds a valid license in its own jurisdiction and the service provider is not a New Zealand reporting entity. However, a dairy commodity trader of raw milk powder requires full banking services: trade finance instruments (documentary letters of credit), SWIFT MT103 wires, and FX hedging. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10,000,000 aggregate cross-border related-party transactions for goods, services, and intangibles; CbCR and Master File obligations apply to multinational groups with group revenue exceeding EUR 750,000,000. Financing arrangements must separately satisfy thin capitalisation and transfer pricing substance thresholds.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply a Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations register. Instead, substance is enforced through tax residence, PE definitions, the general anti-avoidance rule (Income Tax Act 2007 section BG 1), transfer pricing provisions, and DTA beneficial ownership requirements. For NZ_LTD, Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) must actually occur in New Zealand: negotiating milk powder purchase contracts, taking delivery risk, managing quality specifications, issuing invoices, funding trade finance, and bearing freight and currency risk. A shell company that merely routes profits while decisions are made overseas will be disregarded or recharacterised.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Sections 143A-143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994: evasion is criminally punishable by imprisonment for up to 5 years and a fine of up to NZD 50,000. The tax shortfall penalty is up to 150% of the evaded tax, plus use-of-money interest and late payment penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false returns or direct fraudulent invoicing face personal civil liability for the company's losses and tax shortfalls under the Companies Act 1993, including reckless trading and insolvent trading provisions. Criminal prosecution can lead to jail, director disqualification, asset confiscation, and a court-imposed travel ban equivalent to blacklisting.
Profit Repatriation Without Withholding Tax / DTA Abuse

Statutory sanction: The payer is personally liable to pay the unpaid NRWT plus late payment penalties. The Tax Administration Act 1994 imposes shortfall penalties up to 150% for the resulting tax shortfall. If the conduit is designed to defeat the purpose of the DTA, the arrangement is void under the general anti-avoidance rule and NRWT becomes fully payable at the 30% or 15% statutory rate.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable as withholding agents for the unpaid NRWT and penalties. The corporate veil is pierced where funds are paid away to shareholders while trade creditors or the Commissioner of Inland Revenue remain unpaid. Directors face civil asset restitution, disqualification, travel blacklisting, and imprisonment for up to 5 years if the non-payment is a deliberate evasion.
Shell Company / Absence of Economic Substance / AML Failure

Statutory sanction: The arrangement is void for tax avoidance under Income Tax Act 2007 section BG 1, with a shortfall penalty of up to 100% under Tax Administration Act 1994 section 141D for taking a tax position with a dominant purpose of tax avoidance. Non-compliance with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 attracts civil penalties, remedial orders, and suspension of the reporting entity's licence.

Director personal liability: The court may pierce the corporate veil and hold directors personally liable for the company's liabilities, including unpaid tax, if the company is a mere sham or used for dishonest asset protection. Directors may be disqualified under the Companies Act 1993, asset-tracing orders may be made against non-resident directors, and criminal convictions under the Crimes Act 1961 for dishonest use of documents can result in imprisonment and permanent blacklisting from travel or business roles.
