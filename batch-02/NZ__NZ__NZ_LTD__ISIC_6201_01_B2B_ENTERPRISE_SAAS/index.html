A virtual office or commercial mailbox is permissible as a registered office only if it is a physical New Zealand street address and not a postal box, and it must be maintained at all times under Companies Act 1993. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand, electronic portal: myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must first determine if it has a New Zealand tax presence. For GST purposes, a non-resident can register for GST voluntarily if it carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand, or compulsorily if its taxable supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period. The entity must complete the IRD's 'Non-resident company registration' application (IR 596) or apply online via myIR. Required documents include: certified copy of the certificate of incorporation, proof of registered office address, details of directors and shareholders, and a New Zealand physical or postal address for service (or an appointed agent). The IRD will issue an IRD number (tax file number) and a separate GST registration number (if applicable). For income tax, a non-resident company is automatically registered for income tax upon incorporation in New Zealand; for foreign companies, registration is required if they derive New Zealand-sourced income. The application must be submitted with supporting evidence of the entity's existence and beneficial ownership.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to the supply of goods exported from New Zealand, provided the goods are exported within 28 days of the time of supply and the supplier retains evidence of export. For services, zero-rating applies to services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply, and the services are not supplied to a person in New Zealand. For B2B enterprise SaaS, if the customer is a non-resident and the services are performed outside New Zealand (or the benefit is received outside New Zealand), the supply may be zero-rated. The supplier must obtain and retain sufficient evidence of the customer's non-resident status (e.g., business address, IP address, contract details).

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry) - New Zealand Customs Service (NZCS) export declaration (CUSR) or approved electronic equivalent
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill (proof of physical export for goods)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank statement showing foreign exchange receipt from the overseas customer
  • Commercial invoice and contract/purchase order
  • Evidence of payment (e.g., SWIFT confirmation) and any other documentation required by the IRD to substantiate the export

New Zealand does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance or electronic invoicing for GST. However, all GST-registered businesses must issue tax invoices that comply with the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Tax invoices must contain specific details (e.g., supplier name, GST number, date, description, amount). For B2B transactions, a buyer-created tax invoice may be used if agreed. There is no government-mandated API or digital stamping system. The IRD uses risk-based audits and data matching; businesses must retain records for at least 7 years.

If the IRD determines that zero-rating was claimed without proper substantiation, the supplier is liable for the unpaid GST (15%) plus interest and penalties under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and the Tax Administration Act 1994. Penalties include: late payment penalties (up to 10% of the tax), shortfall penalties (up to 150% of the tax for evasion), and criminal prosecution for fraudulent claims. The IRD may claw back GST refunds and impose a 'use of money' interest from the original due date. Additionally, if the goods are not exported within the statutory timeframe, the supply is recharacterized as taxable and GST becomes due.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates an open, free-floating exchange rate system with no general foreign-exchange controls. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible on both current and capital account. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not require pre-approval for trade-related settlements, dividend remittances, repatriation of shareholder loans, or B2B SaaS licensing royalties. The only significant gateways are prudential due diligence by registered banks and, for direct investment involving sensitive land, fishing quota, or large non-sensitive assets, the Overseas Investment Office under the Overseas Investment Act 2005. Capital injected into a New Zealand company may be converted to NZD on the spot market or held in foreign currency. Profit remittance is unrestricted after normal corporate tax compliance; dividends require a shareholder resolution and may attract non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) or an approved issuer levy (AIL) on interest, but no FX conversion limit. Exporters retain full discretion to hold or convert export receipts, and banks will typically require only an FX purpose code and underlying contract documentation for audit trail. Overall, New Zealand is a low-FX-risk jurisdiction for corporate treasury because there is no surrender, rationing, or central-bank allocation regime.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement exists. There is no mandatory timeline, minimum conversion ratio, or Reserve Bank of New Zealand obligation affecting export proceeds. Export receipts may be kept in the currency of invoice, sold on the spot or forward market, or used directly to settle trade payables. For balance-of-payments statistics, exporters may be selected for survey by Statistics New Zealand, but this does not restrict conversion. The only practical requirements are to maintain accurate ledgers for GST, corporate tax, and transfer-pricing compliance, and to use a commercially reasonable exchange rate for New Zealand tax returns.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Putea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Payment fintechs such as Wise and Stripe are regulated in New Zealand as financial service providers under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. They can legally collect B2B SaaS subscription revenue and perform some FX conversion, but they are not registered banks. Their viability for Stage 4 treasury purposes is Medium as a secondary collection rail and Low as the primary settlement backbone. The defining legal constraint is that an EMI cannot establish a true New Zealand clearing account or provide native import and export letters of credit, NZD currency hedges, or borrowing facilities. In addition, major Tier-1 banks are cautious about receiving fund flows intermediated by offshore EMIs; if an NZ_LTD account receives large inbound capital from a Wise balance or a non-bank money-service business, the bank may issue a prolonged source-of-funds request or suspend the account until the EMI transaction statement and underlying contract is provided. A disciplined structuring approach is therefore to use EMI rails solely for low-ticket subscription collections and to run all intercompany financing, capital injection, supplier settlement, and FX hedging through the Tier-1 bank relation.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The domestic non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) rate on gross dividends paid to non-resident shareholders is 30% where the dividend is not fully imputed, and 15% where the dividend carries a full imputation credit. Under most New Zealand double tax agreements, the rate is reduced to 15% for portfolio shareholdings and to 5% for substantial shareholdings of at least 10% of the voting power (the precise rate depends on the applicable treaty).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 for cross-border related-party services and NZD 3,000,000 for non-service related-party transactions. A contemporaneous transfer pricing Local File and Master File are required for any multinational group with cross-border related-party transactions above those thresholds. Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) is mandatory for New Zealand resident ultimate parents of multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of NZD 1.125 billion or more; lower-tier NZ subsidiaries of foreign groups above the same revenue threshold must also file a CbCR return if the ultimate parent is not otherwise reporting to New Zealand. The Commissioner may require broader documentation where the annual value of transactions is significant.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) regime equivalent to the UAE or other offshore financial centres. However, the general anti-avoidance rule in section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, combined with the OECD Principal Purpose Test applied through the Multilateral Instrument (MLI), requires that any entity claiming NZ tax residence, treaty benefits, or reliance on conduit arrangements possess real economic substance. For a B2B SaaS entity, the Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) must be exercised actually in New Zealand: strateg ic software development, server-side product engineering, SaaS contract negotiation, data hosting accountability, and day-to-day technical support. If these CIGA are conducted by the foreign parent while the NZ LTD is reduced to a shell invoicing vehicle, the IRD may reattribute the profits to the foreign principal under sections GC 21 and GB 42, disregard the artificial arrangement, deny treaty relief under the Principal Purpose Test, and impose shortfall penalties of up to 40% for an abusive tax position.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Tax Return Filing

Statutory sanction: Under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, any person who evades or attempts to evade tax by signing a false return or keeping false accounts is liable on conviction to a fine of up to NZ$50,000 and/or imprisonment for up to five years. Under section 141A, a shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax is imposed; the Commissioner may also assess use-of-money interest from the balance date and recover all unpaid tax, penalties, and interest. The company itself is subject to the same penalties, and the Inland Revenue is empowered to issue a formal investigation and compel documents under sections 16–19 of the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign, authorise, or cause the false IR4 to be filed are exposed to personal criminal prosecution as principals or parties to the evasion. The corporate veil is pierced under section 149 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, which imposes joint and several liability on directors for the company's unpaid deductions and GST; in evasion cases, the Court may also apply general piercing principles where the company is used as a mere facade. The director may be subjected to a departure prohibition order under section 157, preventing them from leaving New Zealand until the tax liability is secured, and a dishonesty assessment may lead to disqualification under the Companies Act 1994 and a bar on acting as a director of any New Zealand company. Civil asset recovery can attach to the director's personal property if the evade d tax is traced to the individual, and a criminal conviction for tax evasion carries a custodial sentence of up to five years.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse and Failure of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The arrangement is vulnerable to annulment under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule in section BG 1 and the specific reconstruction power in section GC 21 of the Income Tax Act 2007. The Commissioner will disregard the shell structure, reattribute the SaaS revenue to the parent, disallow intercompany deductions, and reassess the correct non-resident withholding tax on all distributions as if the treaty relief had been denied. A shortfall penalty of up to 40% is imposed for an abusive tax position (section 141D), and if the abuse contains false or misleading information provided to the IRD, the 150% evasion penalty under section 143B may apply. Under the MLI's Principal Purpose Test, treaty benefits are denied entirely for the arrangement.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly set up and operate a shell NZ LTD for treaty shopping are personally assessed under section 149 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 as persons involved in the company's tax default. The corporate veil will be pierced where the company is found to be a mere instrumentality of the offshore parent; directors may be ordered to make civil restitution of the diverted profits, be disqualified from managing companies under the Companies Act 1994, and be prohibited from leaving New Zealand via a departure prohibition order if unpaid tax remains. Criminal liability for aiding and abetting an offence under section 143B can result in imprisonment; the Inland Revenue also has the power to levy personal assets of directors who are found to be the controlling mind of the sham.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: Under sections GC 13 and GC 14 of the Income Tax Act 2007, the IRD will adjust the related-party consideration to the arm's length amount. Thin capitalisation rules under subpart FE disallow interest deductions on the excessive debt portion, and the transfer pricing adjustment may be imposed using the best method available to the Commissioner. A shortfall penalty of 40% is applied for taking an unacceptable tax position under section 141A, and the taxpayer is liable for use-of-money interest. If the documentation was fabricated or the taxpayer deliberately failed to maintain records, the non-compliance escalates to the 150% evasion penalty and criminal liability under section 143B.

Director personal liability: Directors are responsible for ensuring that contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation exists for each cross-border controlled transaction. Where documentation is absent and the Commissioner makes an adjustment, the burden of proof shifts against the company, and directors who approved the non-arm's-length pricing may be personally assessed under the limited-liability piercing provisions in section 149 if the tax default is attributable to their direction. Civil restitution may be ordered against directors who benefited from the stripped profits; departure prohibition orders under section 157 may be issued; and directors who certified false intercompany invoices or misrepresented interest rates to the IRD can be criminally prosecuted for tax evasion, exposing them to fines up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment up to five years.
