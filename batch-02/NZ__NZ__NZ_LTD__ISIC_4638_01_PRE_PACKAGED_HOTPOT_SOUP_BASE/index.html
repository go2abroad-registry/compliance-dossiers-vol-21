Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue (IRD) — government department responsible for tax administration; online portal is myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz) integrated with the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) register.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned or newly incorporated New Zealand limited company must first obtain an NZBN through the Companies Office registration process. The company then applies for an IRD number using the IR596 form (Non-resident company) or completes online IRD registration through myIR. The company must provide the certificate of incorporation, registered office and director details, New Zealand physical address, and expected business activity descriptions. The IRD number is issued promptly once the application and supporting documents are verified, and the company must register for GST separately once registration thresholds are met or voluntarily.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; resident New Zealand company taxed on worldwide income under the Income Tax Act 2007 (no territorial limitation); non-resident owners are subject to withholding tax or attributable rules only on distributions and certain income streams.

Available election forms

No US-style 'check-the-box' election exists. Closely held New Zealand companies may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status on IR488, allowing tax transparency for up to five individuals who are New Zealand residents and not beneficiaries of a foreign trust. Hybrid mismatch elective positions are restricted and must follow the Hybrid and Mismatch Rules in Part FH of the Income Tax Act 2007; there is no general override election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 — a person carrying on a taxable activity must register for GST if total taxable supplies supplied in New Zealand exceeded or will exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period; voluntary registration is permitted where supplies are made periodically.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST-registered exporter may zero-rate supplies of goods that are physically exported from New Zealand in the course or furtherance of a taxable activity, including pre-packaged hotpot soup base bricks sold to overseas buyers. The supplier must retain evidence of export (customs outward entry, carrier manifest, bill of lading/airway bill) showing the goods left New Zealand within 28 days after the time of supply. Zero-rating also applies to services performed for a non-resident person where the services are not connected to New Zealand land, provided the recipient is outside New Zealand at the time of supply.

  • Customs Export Declaration / New Zealand Customs outward entry (ECSI)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing export from New Zealand
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Proof of payment / inward remittance certificate where applicable
  • Carrier manifest / export cargo report evidencing physical departure from New Zealand

No real-time fiscal clearance, API stamping, or digital wet seal is mandated. New Zealand supports voluntary Peppol e-invoicing and direct data exchange with myIR for GST returns, income tax returns, and employer obligations. A registered supplier must issue a valid tax invoice within 28 days of supply for taxable supplies over NZD 50; invoices must meet the GST Act record-keeping requirements, but no government pre-approval is required.

Inland Revenue may disallow zero-rating and assess GST at 15% plus use-of-money interest if export documentation is inadequate or goods did not physically leave New Zealand. Shortfall penalties apply under the Tax Administration Act 1994 at rates up to 150% for abusive evasion, with seven-year reassessment periods for fraudulent claims and four-year periods for non-fraudulent omissions. False export declarations attract additional penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, including forfeiture, monetary penalties, and criminal sanctions. There is no statutory New Zealand FX repatriation deadline, but unreasonably delayed or low-priced offshore receipts may be recharacterised under transfer pricing and cross-border pricing rules.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility and has accepted IMF Article VIII obligations. There is no exchange-control legislation, no currency rationing, and no prior central-bank approval requirement for any lawful cross-border payment or transfer of funds denominated in New Zealand dollars or any foreign currency. The New Zealand dollar is a freely floating major currency, and resident and non-resident entities may freely open, fund, and operate foreign-currency accounts with licensed banks. Profit remittance to non-resident shareholders is unrestricted and carries no profit-remittance clearance or government permit; the only imposts are statutory non-resident withholding tax (currently 30% on dividends, generally reduced to 15% under applicable double-tax treaties such as NZ-China and NZ-Singapore) and the OECD-consistent transfer-pricing documentation obligations for intercompany cross-border payments. No hinrance exists for dividend distribution, foreign-loan servicing, or intercompany royalty payments beyond the company being in good tax standing with Inland Revenue and satisfying the bank's AML/CFT due-diligence protocols.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender, compulsory repatriation, or mandatory conversion obligation on export proceeds. Exporters may lawfully retain 100% of their foreign-currency earnings in licensed New Zealand bank accounts, hold offshore foreign-currency accounts, and settle international trade wholly in USD, CNY, EUR, or any freely convertible currency. There is no statutory timeline by which proceeds must be brought onshore or exchanged into NZD, and no conversion ratio applies. The only regulatory reporting obligations are statistical: exporters must lodge monthly Overseas Trade Statistics (export entry data via the New Zealand Customs Service/Juice portal to Statistics New Zealand) within 10 working days after the end of each reporting month, including commodity description, quantity, FOB value, destination country, and HS code, in order to support the national balance-of-payments compilation. Banks are separately delegated to collect international-transaction data under the RBNZ/Statistics NZ balance-of-payments framework and will record the SWIFT purpose code on each cross-border wire; no surrender rule is triggered by that recording.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua). The RBNZ is the sole central banking authority responsible for monetary policy, currency issuance, financial system stability, and the oversight of licensed banks in New Zealand. There is no separate foreign-exchange administration body; foreign-exchange dealing activity is conducted by registered banks operating under the Reserve Bank's prudential supervision. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) oversees financial market conduct and OTC derivative dealing under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, and the Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) supervises AML/CFT compliance under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. The Overseas Investment Office (OIO), hosted by Land Information New Zealand, administers the Overseas Investment Act 2005 only for screening of sensitive land and significant business assets; it does not control FX flows.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium. New Zealand's liberal exchange-control environment means offshore electronic-money institutions (EMIs) and payment-service providers such as Wise, Airwallex, and Payoneer can lawfully transmit cross-border proceeds, and no RBNZ exchange-control approval is required for their use. However, the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 requires any person providing financial services in New Zealand to be registered on the Financial Services Provider Register (FSPR) unless an exemption applies (for example, the same-group exemption, the licensed-bank exemption, or the foreign-licensee exemption under regulations). Offshore EMIs that are not separately registered in New Zealand may operate only through a licensed New Zealand partner bank or a duly licensed FSP. For a Low-Risk Standard wholesale exporter of pre-packaged hotpot soup base, an offshore EMI is viable for inbound settlement of small-ticket SWIFT-alternative transfers and for cross-border receivables collection in USD/CNY, but is not viable for documentary Letter of Credit handling, capital escrow, or formal trade-finance facilities, which remain resident in tier-1 licensed banks. Legal constraint: where the EMI holds customer funds, the funds must be safeguarded and the entity must hold an e-money or funds-service licence in its home jurisdiction (UK FCA, MAS, AUSTRAC-registered), and the NZ exporter must complete its own counterparty CDD and sanctions screening to avoid AML/CFT liability.

However, the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 requires any person providing financial services in New Zealand to be registered on the Financial Services Provider Register (FSPR) unless an exemption applies (for example, the same-group exemption, the licensed-bank exemption, or the foreign-licensee exemption under regulations). Offshore EMIs that are not separately registered in New Zealand may operate only through a licensed New Zealand partner bank or a duly licensed FSP. For a Low-Risk Standard wholesale exporter of pre-packaged hotpot soup base, an offshore EMI is viable for inbound settlement of small-ticket SWIFT-alternative transfers and for cross-border receivables collection in USD/CNY, but is not viable for documentary Letter of Credit handling, capital escrow, or formal trade-finance facilities, which remain resident in tier-1 licensed banks. Legal constraint: where the EMI holds customer funds, the funds must be safeguarded and the entity must hold an e-money or funds-service licence in its home jurisdiction (UK FCA, MAS, AUSTRAC-registered), and the NZ exporter must complete its own counterparty CDD and sanctions screening to avoid AML/CFT liability.
