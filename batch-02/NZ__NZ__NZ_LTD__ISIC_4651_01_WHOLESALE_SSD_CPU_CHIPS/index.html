Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; the NZ_LTD is a New Zealand resident company by virtue of incorporation in New Zealand under section YD 2 of the Income Tax Act 2007, and is therefore taxable on its worldwide income at the corporate rate, with the New Zealand foreign active income exemption and branch equivalent rules applying to certain offshore business income; it is not a pass-through entity as a default classification.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box classification system. The relevant statutory elections for a closely held trading entity are: (1) the Look-Through Company (LTC) election under sections HB 1 to HB 4 of the Income Tax Act 2007, available only where every look-through owner is an individual, a trustee, or another LTC and no owner is a company, and therefore not available where the NZ_LTD has a corporate shareholder; (2) the GST group registration election under Part 5 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, which consolidates related companies into one GST group; (3) the GST payment basis election from accrual to cash basis where annual taxable supplies do not exceed the prescribed threshold; (4) the GST six-monthly filing election for smaller taxpayers; and (5) the transitional and deemed-settlement elections under the financial arrangement rules where applicable. Hybrid mismatch rules under sections FH 1 to FH 15 of the Income Tax Act 2007 apply mandatorily; they cannot be dis-applied by election, though taxpayers may elect to restructure intercompany funding to neutralise mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period (compulsory registration for taxable supplies made in New Zealand); a person who does not exceed the threshold may voluntarily register. Where the entity makes only remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers as a non-resident, registration is also compulsory once total supplies to New Zealand exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods: the NZ_LTD may zero-rate SSD drives and server processor chips where the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand within 28 days of the supply, the goods are exported in substantially the same state as supplied, and the supplier obtains and retains proof of export. Services: zero-rating applies where the services are supplied to a recipient who is a non-resident and not in New Zealand at the time the services are performed, and the services are not services that directly relate to land or moveable personal property situated in New Zealand at the time of supply. For an open-market spot trader, the typical qualifying transactions are direct resale shipments from New Zealand inventory to overseas buyers on EXW, FOB, or CIF terms, where title and risk pass overseas and physical export is achieved; sales that are merely booked in New Zealand but delivered from an offshore warehouse are outside the New Zealand GST net altogether, and the NZ_LTD should support that offshore supply chain position with contractual evidence of the place of supply.

E-invoicing is voluntary and operates exclusively through the Peppol network framework. However, every GST-registered supplier must issue a tax invoice for taxable supplies exceeding NZD 50 including GST; the tax invoice must show the words 'Tax Invoice', the supplier's name and GST number, the date of issue, a description of the goods or services, the quantity or volume, the amount payable, and for supplies exceeding NZD 1,000 the name and address of the recipient. Zero-rated export invoices must additionally contain a statement that the supply is zero-rated and identify the goods and shipping evidence supporting export.

IRD audits export zero-rating claims with a focus on proof of physical export, the 28-day export window, and commercial substance of the foreign customer relationship. A failure to retain the full documentation bundle for seven years exposes the taxpayer to disallowance of zero-rating, resulting in an output tax assessment at 15% GST on the full export consideration, together with use-of-money interest running from the original due date. Penalties are imposed under the Tax Administration Act 1994 as follows: 20% of the tax shortfall for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or an arrangement with a dominant purpose of defeating the intent of the GST Act; a false or misleading export declaration may also constitute an offence under the Customs and Excise Act 2018. Where proceeds are not repatriated within 180 days and no genuine foreign currency receivable exists, IRD may recharacterise the transaction as a non-export, clawing back the GST input credits historically claimed on the related inventory and assessing shortfall penalties plus increased UOMI. For fraudulent zero-rating the directors may face personal liability under the director penalty regime and criminal prosecution with imprisonment of up to five years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar is freely convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. There are no general exchange control restrictions on inbound capital injection, profit repatriation, dividend payment, or shareholder loan repayment. Dividends paid to non-resident shareholders are subject to New Zealand non-resident withholding tax at 15% or any applicable lower treaty rate. Capital account investments may be subject to screening under the Overseas Investment Act only if they involve particular sensitive land, fishing quota, or significant business assets; ordinary semiconductor wholesale trading capital does not require pre-approval. No central bank authorization is required for remittance of trading profits or capital returns.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender requirement for export proceeds. There is no mandatory timeline for repatriation and no mandatory conversion ratio into New Zealand dollars. Exporters may retain receipts in foreign currency, convert freely at market rates, or hold in overseas accounts. Export transactions remain subject to AML/CFT record-keeping and reporting obligations, but there is no Reserve Bank requirement to surrender foreign exchange earnings.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a payment facilitation channel but Low as a substitute for a New Zealand bank account. Offshore digital electronic money institutions are not authorized deposit-taking institutions in New Zealand and cannot provide the same custodial and escrow safeguards as a registered bank. They may offer virtual IBANs or payment processing, but are legally constrained by the need to comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, and the Payments System rules. Client funds held by an offshore EMI are not protected under the RBNZ banking deposit exclusion unless the EMI is registered and licensed in an acceptable jurisdiction. For capital escrow and high-value trade settlement, a tier-one New Zealand bank is mandatory.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or loan capital must be routed by SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfer to the New Zealand company's domestic account. Remittance instruction must clearly state the purpose as direct investment share capital subscription or shareholder loan contribution, including the New Zealand Business Number and Company Number of the recipient entity. New Zealand banks will verify source of funds, ultimate beneficial ownership, and tax residency before crediting capital. No Reserve Bank approval is required. The wire should reference the relevant banking purpose code for equity injection or direct investment, such as 'EQUI' or the bank's internal BOP reporting code, and should include: 'SHARE CAPITAL INJECTION - DIRECT INVESTMENT - NZ COMPANY NUMBER [NZBN]'. The instructing bank must conduct correspondent banking due diligence and confirm that the sending entity holds evidence of the capital contribution resolution and the NZ bank's inward remittance compliance approval.Outbound trade payments must be executed through regulated clearing rails: SWIFT MT103 for open-account settlement and telegraphic transfer of trade invoices; SWIFT MT700 for documentary commercial letters of credit issued by the New Zealand bank in favor of overseas suppliers; and escrow inspection wires released only after presentation of an independent inspection certificate. For domestic settlement leg, the New Zealand clearing system operates through the ESAS settlement environment. For cross-border settlement, correspondent banks use SWIFT gpi for tracking. Each outbound payment must include the underlying invoice reference, ISIC 4651 trade description, and be supported by the trade contract. They may offer virtual IBANs or payment processing, but are legally constrained by the need to comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, and the Payments System rules. Client funds held by an offshore EMI are not protected under the RBNZ banking deposit exclusion unless the EMI is registered and licensed in an acceptable jurisdiction. For capital escrow and high-value trade settlement, a tier-one New Zealand bank is mandatory.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory NZ domestic rate: 30% for any dividend paid by a New Zealand company to a non-resident (under the Income Tax Act 2007, Part N). However, if the company maintains an imputation credit account, the effective rate on 'fully imputed' dividends paid to a non-resident is 28% NRWT (the imputation credit portion is not subject to NRWT; the cash dividend is deemed grossed up and NRWT at 30% applies, but the imputation credit offsets the company tax, historically yielding a combined 28% shareholder tax on a fully imputed dividend). For treaty countries, the DTA commonly reduces NRWT to 15% (or 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders holding 10% or more, depending on the DTA).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand transfer pricing documentation thresholds (effective from 1 July 2022): A Master File is required if the multinational group has consolidated group revenue of at least NZD $100 million and the New Zealand entity (or group) has cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZD $50 million in that income year. A Local File is required if the group has NZD $100 million or more in global revenue and the New Zealand entity has cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZD $10 million in that income year. For enterprises below these thresholds, contemporaneous documentation is not mandatory but is strongly encouraged and reduces or eliminates penalty risk. The 'significant enterprise' threshold for the 4-year reassessment period is NZD $100 million global revenue and NZD $10 million related-party transactions with New Zealand.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a separate 'Economic Substance Regulations' regime, but the Income Tax Act 2007 contains a comprehensive general anti-avoidance rule (s BG 1) and specific CFC and transfer pricing rules that effectively require real economic substance for treaty claims and loss utilisation. A wholesale trading company engaged in SSD/CPU chip brokerage must demonstrate meaningful operational substance in New Zealand: physical office or warehouse, employees involved in procurement and sales, the assumption of inventory risk, and the performance of management and decision-making functions. Failure to do so may result in recharacterisation under the 'economic substance doctrine', denial of tax residence benefits, and the imposition of penalties under the international tax authority's powers.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Intent

Statutory sanction: Under s 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994, evasion or similar offences carry a fine of up to NZD $50,000 for a company; under s 143B, fraudulent evasion can lead to imprisonment of up to 5 years for individuals. Inland Revenue also imposes a shortfall penalty of 150% of the tax evaded (s 141D) and may assess for up to 8 years back for fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate or authorise evasion can be prosecuted personally and sentenced to up to 5 years' imprisonment. The director may also be jointly and severally liable for civil penalties, and the corporate veil will be pierced where the company is a sham. The court may issue a travel ban and order restitution of unpaid taxes.
Transfer Pricing / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may adjust the taxpayer's income to reflect arm's length prices (s GC 6-14 ITA 2007). A shortfall penalty of up to 100% of the resulting tax shortfall may apply if the taxpayer had a dominant purpose of tax avoidance (s 141E). The 8-year reassessment period applies for transfer pricing cases where inadequate disclosure is made.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign incorrect transfer pricing documentation or are party to a scheme that is voided may be personally liable for penalties if they are knowing participants. The director could face disqualification, travel bans, and, where the arrangement constitutes criminal tax evasion, imprisonment.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (s BG 1) may void the arrangement, and Inland Revenue can reattribute the income to the real economic owner. Under the Companies Act 1993, a company that does not carry on business can be struck off. Treaties may be denied if the company is not the 'beneficial owner' or lacks any real business purpose.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company can be personally liable for enabling the company to operate in breach of the Companies Act (trading recklessly, s 135), and can be prosecuted for tax offences if they sign false returns. They may be banned from acting as directors and subject to imprisonment.
AML/CFT Non-Compliance in Cross-Border Trade

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties under the AML/CFT Act 2009 can reach NZD $2 million for a body corporate and NZD $200,000 for an individual. Criminal penalties for wilful breach can be imprisonment up to 2 years and fines of up to NZD $300,000 per offence. The Financial Markets Authority is the regulator for non-bank sector.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers can be personally liable for AML/CFT breaches. The Regulations impose personal liability on the 'senior manager' of a reporting entity. A director who knowingly permits the company to process funds for a sanctioned person or without AML checks could face debarment and imprisonment.
False Invoicing / Fraudulent Documentation

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, the penalty can be 150% of the shortfall for evasion; for false GST reporting, a 50% penalty for gross carelessness and 100% for abusive tax position. Forgeries can also attract criminal liability under the Crimes Act 1961 for forgery, with up to 10 years' imprisonment.

Director personal liability: A director involved in issuing false invoices is criminally liable and may be fined and imprisoned. The director's assets can be traced and restrained under criminal proceeds recovery legislation. The director may also be disqualified from managing companies for a period fixed by the court.
Failure to Register and Remit GST

Statutory sanction: Late registration penalties: an immediate penalty of 75% of the unpaid GST if the failure is fraudulent; otherwise, a 5% penalty and 1% per month incremental interest. Inland Revenue may assess GST on the full taxable supply value, and prosecution can occur where a person fails to register with an intent to defraud.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for unpaid GST under the director penalty regime (Part 11A of the Tax Administration Act 1994) if the company has failed to pay GST and the director did not take reasonable steps to ensure payment. The penalty is 100% of the unpaid GST plus interest, and a director can be pursued directly without the corporate veil.
