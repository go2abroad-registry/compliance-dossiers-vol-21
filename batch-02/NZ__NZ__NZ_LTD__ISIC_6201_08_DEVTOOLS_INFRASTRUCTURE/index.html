Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A New Zealand registered office must be a physical street address within New Zealand open for service of documents; a virtual office or commercial mail-forwarding box is not sufficient. For an ISIC 6201 cloud infrastructure company, the Inland Revenue Department and major banks reject pure virtual offices for GST registration and corporate account opening because there is no physical premises, no local records, no key staff, and no verifiable director presence. A serviced-office physical address with a lease, meeting rooms, management, and utility bills is acceptable as registered office for tax purposes, but the IRD will assess commercial substance by actual activity, payroll, source of income, bank transactions, and export structure. Thus a virtual mailbox must be replaced by a managed office space or director-approved physical address before incorporation.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None under New Zealand law. The Companies Act 1993 abolishes par value, authorised capital and minimum paid-up capital. A company may be incorporated with zero paid-up capital or with a nominal amount such as NZ$1.00. Shares may be issued for cash, property or services if the board considers the consideration to be fair value. No blocked escrow account or minimum capital deposit is required by the Registrar.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from New Zealand are zero-rated for GST if they are exported within 28 days of supply. Services supplied to a non-resident are zero-rated if they are not supplied in New Zealand and are not directly related to land or goods in New Zealand. For digital services, the place of supply rules apply, and zero-rating is available if the recipient is a non-resident and the service is not performed in New Zealand.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry)
  • Bill of Lading
  • Proof of Export (e.g., shipping documents)
  • Bank statement showing receipt of foreign currency

No real-time fiscal clearance mandate. Tax invoices must be issued for taxable supplies, and records must be maintained. There is no mandatory e-invoicing or digital stamping.

If export zero-rating is claimed without proper documentation or is found to be fraudulent, the GST will be assessed, and penalties under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 apply, including shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax, and criminal prosecution for fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand has unrestricted current and capital account convertibility. The exchange control regime was abolished in 1984 and no reinstatement has occurred. The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is a free-floating G10 currency with no domestic surrender or conversion mandate. Profit remittances, intercompany dividends, loan repayments, and capital repatriation may be effected freely via any registered bank without RBNZ or FMA authorization. The only statutory friction is the Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) at 15% on dividends, 15% on interest (or reduced treaty rate with applicable DTA), which is a tax compliance obligation rather than an FX restriction. No CFIUS-style inbound screening, no negative interest rate or capital buffer restrictions on foreign-owned NZ entities, and no requirement to localize USD revenue into NZD within any statutory window.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rules exist in New Zealand. Exporters of software, SaaS subscriptions, and API monitoring infrastructure services may retain 100% of foreign currency export receipts in offshore USD, EUR, or any other convertible currency accounts indefinitely. There is no mandatory repatriation timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no requirement to sell export proceeds to an authorized dealer. The only reporting obligation is standard trade statistics reporting to Statistics New Zealand (Overseas Trade Index) and the IRD's international financial reporting for balance of payments purposes when applicable, which are statistical rather than approval-based.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Putea Matua) in coordination with the Financial Markets Authority for AML/CFT supervision; New Zealand maintains a fully liberalized cross-border capital and foreign exchange regime with no central bank exchange control approval requirements.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High for inbound collections and outbound USD/EUR/GBP disbursements. Stripe Treasury and Stripe Billing integrate natively with the NZ entity's operations given the target payment rails, providing virtual account issuance with substantial sub-account structure through a US partner bank but without NZD settlement; Wise Business and Airwallex are fully viable for NZ-incorporated firms and provide domestic NZD bank account numbers via a local NZ banking partner (typically BNZ or Westpac) while enabling borderless multi-currency wallets. Offshore EMIs such as Mercury (US) are only viable where a separate US-disregarded LLC is established to hold the US account; the NZ Ltd itself cannot open a Mercury account directly without a US connecting entity. Legal constraints: under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989, EMIs are not licensed banks and may not hold client funds as 'deposits'; they must segregate client money through a licensed NZ bank or registered custodian, and reliance solely on an unregulated offshore EMI is not acceptable for statutory capital escrow purposes because an EMI does not offer the same RBNZ protection under the Deposit Takers Act 2023.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan injections must be executed via SWIFT MT103 (International Wire Transfer) from the investor's bank to the NZ company's Operational Account at an NZ tier-1 bank. The wire instruction shall include the beneficiary NZ company name and NZBN, and the payment reference shall specify 'Equity Contribution' or 'Shareholder Loan.' New Zealand does not require a foreign investment approval for a DevOps cloud tools company below the $200 million significant business asset threshold under the Overseas Investment Act 2005, but the receiving bank must complete sanctions screening and the company must provide its Certificate of Incorporation, board resolution, shareholder register, and the investor's bank verification. For anti-money laundering compliance, aggregate cash transactions over NZD 10,000 are reportable; wires of equity from institutional investors are not subject to the cash transaction threshold but the bank may file a suspicious transaction report if funding sources are opaque. The IRD requires the company to maintain accurate shareholder records and report any foreign shareholding on the annual return; benefit/ownership changes exceeding 25% must be filed with the Companies Office within 20 working days. The funds are then available for treasury deployment without any exchange control approval or mandatory conversion.The primary clearing rails for outbound trade settlements are: (1) SWIFT MT103 for intercompany vendor payments and foreign supplier invoices to the US, EU, and APAC technology partners; (2) the New Zealand Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) operated under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2006 for domestic NZD disbursements to local subprocessors and payroll; (3) Commercial Letters of Credit (UCP 600) only where a major hardware procurement is involved, which is rare for a pure DevOps software infrastructure company; and (4) ACH via the fintech rails for recurring international SaaS licensing to the US parent or technology partner, routed through either Mercury ACH (via a US-connected entity) or Stripe Payouts. Legal constraints: under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989, EMIs are not licensed banks and may not hold client funds as 'deposits'; they must segregate client money through a licensed NZ bank or registered custodian, and reliance solely on an unregulated offshore EMI is not acceptable for statutory capital escrow purposes because an EMI does not offer the same RBNZ protection under the Deposit Takers Act 2023.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand has no de minimis statutory documentation threshold requiring Local File or Master File preparation for all taxpayers, but the Inland Revenue's transfer pricing guidelines (and OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines) apply. Mandatory documentation requirements are triggered by: (1) cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZ$10 million in aggregate in an income year for non-financial transactions; or (2) cross-border related-party financing arrangements exceeding NZ$1 million in aggregate. For multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of NZ$1.125 billion or more, Country-by-Country reporting (CbCR) is mandatory if the ultimate parent files in New Zealand or the group is required to file CbCR under a parent jurisdiction and the New Zealand entity is the surrogate parent or constituent entity. Record-keeping must support arm's-length pricing and be available upon request.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain formal Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as found in certain offshore financial centres; however, the application of transfer pricing rules, controlled foreign company (CFC) rules, thin capitalisation rules, the general anti-avoidance rule (section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007), and the 'anti-conduit' rules require that any New Zealand limited company demonstrates real economic substance, managerial control and decision-making in New Zealand. A shell company with no local business activity, no directors' meetings in New Zealand, and no substantive operations risks being treated as having a permanent establishment elsewhere or being subject to reconstruction under the anti-avoidance provisions. New Zealand's tax treaties allocate taxing rights based on place of effective management, so a company whose effective management is outside New Zealand may be treated as non-resident and lose access to domestic concessions, or if it lacks substance, the jurisdiction of effective management may impose tax. The company must maintain proper books, records, and board minutes both for tax purposes and for Companies Act compliance; failure to do so can result in fine and deregistration.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Avoidance (Income Tax Act 2007, ss 141, 143; Crimes Act 1961)

Statutory sanction: For tax evasion: a person is liable on conviction to imprisonment for up to 5 years and a fine of up to NZ$50,000 for an individual or up to NZ$250,000 for a company, plus the full amount of evaded tax and shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax shortfall (ranging from 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, to 150% for evasion). For tax avoidance: shortfall penalty of 40% plus an increase of 100% if the dominant purpose is to defeat the intent of the Act (total 100% for abusive tax position). Inland Revenue may also seek publication of the taxpayer's name and details.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly assist in, authorise, or participate in tax evasion or avoidance can be proceeded against personally under the Tax Administration Act 1994 (ss 143, 147). The corporate veil may be pierced where the company is a sham or fraud is involved; directors may be personally liable for unpaid tax under the Companies Act 1993 (e.g., s 300 for fraudulent trading) and for tax shortfalls arising from their own culpable conduct. Directors can face imprisonment (up to 5 years), confiscation orders, and travel bans or border alert submissions made by Inland Revenue to prevent departure from New Zealand for failure to pay tax debts or respond to investigation demands (Tax Administration Act 1994, s 157). Courts can also issue asset freeze orders and civil penalty orders against directors.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance (Tax Administration Act 1994 and Income Tax Act 2007 anti-avoidance rules; Companies Act 1993)

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue can disregard the corporate structure, reallocate income and deductions to the real economic owner, impose an abusive tax position shortfall penalty of up to 100% of the tax shortfall plus interest, and, where the company is a sham, the corporate veil may be lifted. The Registrar of Companies may initiate deregistration or place the company in liquidation for failure to meet the 'genuine presence' requirements under the Companies Act (e.g., no registered office, no director resident in New Zealand or Australia, or failure to maintain accounting records). Directors' duties under the Companies Act may be enforced with penalties of up to NZ$200,000 for failure to comply with record-keeping.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company face civil liability for the company's debts if they allow the company to trade recklessly or fraudulently (Company Act 1993, s 135 (reckless trading) and s 136 (duty not to incur obligations); s 300 for fraudulent trading). Directors can be personally liable for all or part of the company's liabilities. Inland Revenue can also require any director to pay tax shortfalls where the shortfall arose because the director not only failed to exercise reasonable care, but did so knowingly. Travel bans under s 157 apply where tax is unpaid. Persistent non-compliance may lead to disqualification from managing companies for up to five years (Companies Act 1993, s 383).
AML/CFT Failures and Structuring Dirty Money (Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009; Financial Transactions Reporting Act 1996 (historical); Crimes Act 1961 ss 243, 244)

Statutory sanction: For money laundering under the Crimes Act 1961: imprisonment of up to 7 years (knowingly dealing with property derived from serious crime) or up to 5 years for aiding. Under the AML/CFT Act 2009, a reporting entity that breaches a compliance obligation is liable for a civil penalty of up to NZ$4 million for a body corporate and NZ$400,000 for an individual, plus a further penalty of up to NZ$100,000 for each day the breach continues. The Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 allows profit forfeiture orders and unexplained wealth orders. For a low-risk standard business, a breach is still possible if it holds a financial service provider licence or is deemed a financial institution for FATCA/CRS purposes.

Director personal liability: Directors can be prosecuted for money laundering if they knowingly facilitated the offence. They can be personally liable for AML/CFT civil penalties if they directed or participated in the breach. Travel bans and property restraint orders are available under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 to prevent dissipation of assets pending investigation. Directors convicted on indictment of money laundering can be sentenced to up to 7 years in prison and are disqualified from being a director (Companies Act 1993 s 382) if the offence is committed in connection with the management of the company.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Thin Capitalisation (Income Tax Act 2007 ss GC 1-GC 20; CIR v Chevron rulings)

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may reprice intercompany transactions using the arm's-length standard, disallow excess interest deductions, recharacterise debt as equity, impose transfer pricing adjustments that increase assessable income, and apply shortfall penalties: 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, up to 100% for an abusive tax position, plus use-of-money interest. There is no statutory cap on the additional tax assessed. For large multinationals, non-compliance can also trigger a specific transfer pricing penalty of up to NZ$50,000 where the taxpayer fails to maintain documentation or provide it on time.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false transfer pricing disclosures or approve documentation they know to be materially false can be personally liable for penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 143 (false statements) and face fines up to NZ$50,000. They can also be personally liable for tax shortfalls if the corporate veil is pierced for sham or fraud. Directors may be prohibited from leaving New Zealand under s 157 while tax debts are outstanding. If the company is insolvent as a result of profit stripping, directors may be personally liable under Companies Act 1993 s 135 (reckless trading) to the extent of the company's debts, including Inland Revenue's claim.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax and Imputation Credit Frauds (Income Tax Act 2007 ss RE 1-RE 22; Tax Administration Act 1994)

Statutory sanction: The payer is personally liable to pay the unreported NRWT as a debt to the Crown, plus penalties: a late filing penalty of NZ$250/NZ$500 and daily penalties, a late payment penalty of 1% immediately and 4% after 6 days, monthly 1% thereafter, and use-of-money interest. Shortfall penalties for an unacceptable tax position or evasion apply if the failure is deliberate: from 20% to 150% of the NRWT shortfall. A failure to account for imputation credit debits may lead to a debit balance in the imputation credit account, requiring payment of tax of 28% of the debit balance plus penalties. Knowing failure to withhold is an offence under s 143 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 (fine up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years).

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the company's NRWT liabilities if they are directors at the time the liability arises and the company fails to withhold or pay, under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 149A (company tax reporting obligations, personal liability of directors). The Commissioner can recover the outstanding tax from directors without first exhausting remedies against the company. Imprisonment up to 5 years applies for knowingly filing a false tax credit or withholding declaration. Travel bans, freezing orders, and disqualification from directorship can be imposed.
