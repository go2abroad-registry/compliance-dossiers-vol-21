None. New Zealand does not require minimum paid-up capital for a standard limited company. The company may be incorporated with nil paid-up capital. No blocked escrow account is required by statute. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Taake), New Zealand; taxpayer electronic portal: myIR (myIR.ird.govt.nz); entity registration and foreign-company registration: New Zealand Companies Office through the Companies Register and New Zealand Business Number (NZBN); export physical clearance: New Zealand Customs Service (Te Mana Arai o Aotearoa).

E-government filing portal

For a New Zealand-incorporated NZ_LTD, the entity is automatically created with a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) under the Companies Act 1993 and must separately apply to Inland Revenue for an IRD number; application is made using the prescribed Inland Revenue company IRD number registration process published on ird.govt.nz, and requires company name, Companies Office number, registered office, principal business activity, date of incorporation, director/shareholder details, and New Zealand address for service. For a genuine foreign non-resident entity, the first step is to determine whether it will be 'carrying on business' in New Zealand; if so, it must register as an overseas company with the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993, obtain an NZBN, and then submit the non-resident company IRD number application to Inland Revenue, attaching certified certificate of incorporation, constitution, director and shareholder registers, a New Zealand address for service, and authorisation for a resident tax agent where applicable. The IRD number is the single national Tax Identification Number and is also the GST registration number. If the entity's taxable supplies, including zero-rated supplies, in any 12-month period are expected to exceed NZ$60,000, or if it is a non-resident supplier of remote services to New Zealand-resident consumers above that threshold, a simultaneous GST registration application must be lodged through myIR using the same IRD number. A New Zealand resident agent or address for service should be retained to receive Inland Revenue and Companies Office notices.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand does not have a US-style check-the-box election or a general hybrid classification election. A closely held eligible limited company may elect look-through company (LTC) treatment under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, causing profits and losses to be transparent to shareholders rather than taxed at entity level; otherwise the default is entity-level taxation at the corporate rate. New Zealand has also enacted hybrid and branch mismatch rules in sections FH 1 to FH 17 of the Income Tax Act 2007, and eligible New Zealand corporate groups may elect consolidated group treatment under the Income Tax Act 2007 where they meet the statutory residency and shareholding conditions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZ$60,000 of taxable supplies, including zero-rated export supplies, in any 12-month period, or where a reasonable expectation exists that the threshold will be exceeded; the same NZ$60,000 threshold applies to non-resident suppliers of remote services supplied to New Zealand-resident consumers. Registration is compulsory once the threshold is crossed, and voluntary registration is possible from the first dollar when a taxable activity is being carried on.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from New Zealand are zero-rated under section 11(1) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 where the goods are supplied to a person who is not in New Zealand and the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply, or the goods are supplied in a customs-controlled area and entered for export. Services, including laboratory and UN38.3 battery safety testing services, are zero-rated under section 11A(1) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 where the services are supplied to a recipient who is not resident in New Zealand and is outside New Zealand at the time of supply, provided the services are not supplied to a fixed establishment of the recipient in New Zealand and do not otherwise fall within a statutory exception such as services connected with land in New Zealand. For the testing laboratory, the key substantiation is the testing contract with the non-resident principal, the final UN38.3 test report or CE-RED electromagnetic test certificate, the commercial invoice marked zero-rated, and evidence that the recipient and payment originate outside New Zealand.

  • New Zealand Customs Service export entry lodged electronically through the Customs Trade Single Window, producing a valid export clearance reference for the goods; for goods temporarily imported for testing, the corresponding temporary importation entry and subsequent export entry should be retained
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill naming the New Zealand export supplier, the overseas consignee, the New Zealand port or airport of loading, and the overseas port or airport of discharge
  • Commercial invoice and sales contract showing the cross-border supply, Incoterms, invoiced currency, and itemised value of the laboratory services or goods
  • Packing list and, for UN38.3 battery safety cargo, the dangerous goods declaration and relevant transport test certificate
  • Bank SWIFT credit advice, telegraphic transfer confirmation, or foreign exchange credit confirmation showing receipt of settlement funds from the non-resident buyer; New Zealand GST law does not prescribe a fixed foreign-exchange repatriation deadline, but the 180-day figure is retained here as the pipeline evidence benchmark
  • For services: signed testing agreement with the non-resident client, final test report, tax invoice annotated 'zero-rated export', and a tax residence certificate or statutory declaration from the recipient confirming non-residence outside New Zealand at the time of supply

There is no real-time fiscal clearance, no mandatory government API telemetry, and no anti-fraud digital stamping regime in New Zealand. GST-registered suppliers must issue a tax invoice containing the statutory particulars set out in the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, including supplier name, IRD/GST number, invoice date, description of supply, GST-exclusive consideration, GST-inclusive consideration, and the GST component, within 28 days of the supply when a tax invoice is required or requested. Invoicing may be paper-based or electronic, voluntary PEPPOL e-invoicing is available, and GST returns are filed electronically through myIR or Inland Revenue-approved filing gateways.

New Zealand law does not impose a separate statutory 180-day foreign-currency repatriation deadline for GST zero-rating; the numerical field represents the dossier's documentary-evidence benchmark. If a supply is not actually zero-rated or the required export evidence is not held, Inland Revenue can assess GST at 15% on the full consideration through a default assessment under the Tax Administration Act 1994, disallow associated input tax claims, and recover the resulting interest and penalties. Use of money interest (UOMI) runs from the original return due date; shortfall penalties may apply at 20% for lack of reasonable care, 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. False statements made to the New Zealand Customs Service can also attract penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 and prosecution. Inland Revenue may amend assessments within the four-year statutory amendment window for non-fraudulent matters, with no time limit for fraud or evasion, and may retrospectively claw back GST refunds previously issued on unsubstantiated zero-rated supplies.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates under a freely floating exchange rate regime with no statutory foreign exchange controls, no exchange surrender requirements, and no prior approval for inward or outward capital movements. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand acts as the central bank and prudential regulator but does not impose convertibility restrictions on current account transactions such as trade-related payments, testing service fees, royalties, or capital account flows such as equity injections, dividend remittances, and shareholder loan repayments. Profit repatriation by a non-resident shareholder is generally permissible without exchange control approval; the practical hurdles are corporate tax compliance, financial reporting consistency, and standard AML/CFT verification of the receiving bank. An NZ Ltd trading as a battery safety testing laboratory may hold foreign currency accounts with New Zealand trading banks, invoice clients in USD, EUR, or CNY, and convert proceeds through the domestic interbank market at market-determined rates. No conversion ratio or mandatory channel exists. The absence of capital controls is supported by the OECD code of liberalisation and New Zealand's commitment to free cross-border investment. However, the bank still must perform customer due diligence and suspicious transaction reporting under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Therefore, while convertibility is not legally restricted, the operational documentary base must prove the underlying commercial, financial, and beneficial ownership structure to satisfy the financial institution's compliance committee.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. New Zealand imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender rule, no forced conversion timeline, and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters may retain foreign currency receipts offshore or in onshore foreign currency accounts without restriction. For New Zealand tax purposes, income must be converted into New Zealand dollars using an acceptable exchange rate when recognising revenue.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Wise, the New Zealand-founded fintech, is regulated as a financial service provider under the Financial Service Providers Act 2008 but is not a registered bank under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989. It cannot provide deposit-taking banking services, capital verification, trade finance, or regulatory-era physical branch services. As a payment and clearing rail, Wise is legally viable in New Zealand because there is no FX-control prohibition on using non-bank electronic money institutions, and it can be effective for low-value cross-border testing fee collections or supplier payments. However, it is not an acceptable substitute for a tier-1 domestic commercial bank as the primary treasury custodian for a UN38.3 battery safety testing laboratory. The entity should maintain its core operating account with a New Zealand trading bank and use EMIs only as a secondary liquidity clearing rail, with strict governance to ensure inbound capital contributions are never settled through unregulated or non-bank channels.

Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: Independent certified CPA audit is mandatory for any New Zealand limited company that does not qualify as a small company under Section 2 of the Financial Reporting Act 2013. A small company is one that, in each of the two preceding accounting periods, does not exceed at least two of: (i) NZD 3 million book value of total assets; (ii) NZD 6 million of total revenue; (iii) 30 full-time employees. Audit is also compulsory for all 'large' companies (e.g., issuers, companies with NZD 40 million assets / NZD 60 million turnover / 250+ employees, or with 25% or more overseas shareholding) and in any case where shareholders pass a resolution requiring an audit. For this battery safety-LAB entity, a mandatory audit is triggered when any two of the NZD 3M/NZD 6M/30-headcount thresholds are met for two consecutive years.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 aggregate cross-border related-party transactions in a single income year triggers the expectation of contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under the New Zealand Inland Revenue's administrative guidelines, with higher-risk or large multinational groups required to prepare OECD-standard Local File and Master File documentation.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no standalone Economic Substance Regulation similar to UAE or BVI, but it applies the OECD 'economic substance' and 'beneficial ownership' concepts through anti-avoidance rules (ITA 2007 section BG 1 and section GA 1), the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR), transfer pricing rules, and the 'core income-generating activities' (CIGA) analysis under the relevant DTA. A shell company without premises, personnel, testing facilities, or business risk will fail the economic substance test for claiming treaty benefits or intercompany service fees. For a UN38.3 battery safety laboratory, true economic substance requires a physical lab in New Zealand with qualified testing engineers, actual test logs, controlled equipment calibration, safety incident protocols, and active certification activities. The New Zealand Inland Revenue will disregard artificial structures and attribute income to the jurisdiction where the real activities are performed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion by Misdeclaring Offshore Fees

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue will apply sections GB 1, GC 13-GC 15 and section GC 21 (transfer pricing adjustments) of the Income Tax Act 2007, plus the general anti-avoidance rule under section BG 1. The shortfall penalty is up to 150% of the tax avoided for evasion; a 20% penalty for unacceptable tax avoidance; and a 40% penalty for gross carelessness. For evading NZD 40,000 or more, criminal prosecution can be brought under sections 143A/143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Director personal liability: Under the Companies Act 1993, directors may be personally liable for loss caused to the company by reckless trading (s.135) and for allowing the company to incur obligations that the director knows it cannot fulfil (s.136). In tax evasion cases, the courts may pierce the corporate veil if the company is a mere facade or sham. Directors who knowingly authored or signed false transfer pricing documents face criminal imprisonment of up to 5 years, fines up to NZD 50,000, and disqualification from being director. Directors may also receive an asset freezing order or were subject to a New Zealand Inland Revenue blacklisting under the Government's serious crime asset recovery provisions. Civil restitution is available for taxes, penalties, and legal fees, and directors who attempted to strip company assets to avoid tax can have their personal bank accounts garnished.
Shell Company with No Economic Substance in New Zealand

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may reassess the entity as a non-resident company or treat it as a sham under common law and section BG 1. It will deny treaty relief under the relevant DTA by reason of no PE or no beneficial ownership. Fines for false certification and operating without the required statutory testing laboratory accreditation (IECEE / ISO 17025) include revocation of accreditation and prosecution under the Fair Trading Act and the Electricity Safety Regulations. The tax shortfall penalty is 150%, and the company may be struck off under the Companies Act 1993 for operating a sham.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable under section 300 Companies Act 1993 for false statements or fraudulent conduct; also under the Financial Reporting Act and Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act if the fake lab receives funds. The veil is pierced when the director uses the company as an instrument for fraud; the director may be jailed for up to 14 years for forgery of safety-test certificates under the Crimes Act 1961. New Zealand courts may issue a travel ban and 'Director Blacklisting' under the Companies Office regime, preventing re-entry into the jurisdiction. Personal assets are subject to a civil asset freeze and restitution order for all profits derived from the fraudulent certificates.
AML and Corruption Violation: Accepting Proceeds from Non-Resident Shell Client for Fraudulent UN38.8 'Pass' Certification

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, a reporting entity faces fines up to NZD 10 million for serious breaches and directors face fines up to NZD 250,000 and up to 7 years' imprisonment for wilful blindness. The Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE) may cancel the testing laboratory license and ISO 17025 accreditation. The fraudulent certification is also a crime under the Crimes Act 1961, with imprisonment up to 7 years for obtaining registration by deception. If the product enters the EU and causes harm, criminal liability under the CE-RED regulation and the New Zealand Product Safety Standards could add NZD 500,000 penalties per offence.

Director personal liability: Directors who knew of money laundering through the compromised test certificates are personally liable under section 39 of the AML/CFT Act. The corporate veil is pierced under the sham/fraud exception; the director is personally liable for disgorgement of all testing fees and the cost of any consumer injury claims. A travel ban (Blacklisting) can be imposed under the Proceeds of Crime Act 1991 and a director convicted of a money laundering offence can receive imprisonment for 14 years. The director is barred from managing a company for up to 10 years, personal goods are subject to a proceeds-of-crime confiscation order, and the director is jointly and severally liable for the company's unpaid tax and penalties through the Tax Administration Act s.162.
