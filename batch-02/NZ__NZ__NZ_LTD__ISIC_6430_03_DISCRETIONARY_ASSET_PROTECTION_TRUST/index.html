Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies to a New Zealand private limited company; incorporation is lawful with nil capital. Shares may be issued unpaid or for promissory consideration as resolved by the board. No escrow bank account rule is imposed by the Companies Act 1993 for ordinary limited companies; capitalisation is a matter of board discretion documented in the first board resolution.

Funding & unlock path: Fire Safety Certificate issued under the Fire and Emergency New Zealand Act 2017, including compliance with NZS 4503 Building Evacuation Procedures; Building Warrant of Fitness and structural load inspection under the Building Act 2004 for the registered office premises; and an occupational health and safety clearance under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, covering workplace risk assessment, emergency egress, first aid provisions, and a statement of fitness for office-based fiduciary operations.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand - online portal: myIR (https://myir.ird.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity establishing a New Zealand limited company must obtain an IRD number for the company as part of incorporation and company registration through the New Zealand Companies Office. The Companies Office will automatically notify the IRD upon incorporation; the company then receives a unique IRD number. If an IRD number is not automatically issued or for a non-resident entity needing direct registration, file the prescribed application form (IR742 for companies and other entities) with supporting constitutional documentation, proof of directorship, and registered agent information. For GST purposes, the company must separately register under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 once taxable supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month rolling period; registration is also possible voluntarily on a 12-month expected-turnover basis. Non-resident persons or companies with isolated NZ-sourced transactions may apply for an IRD number via Non-Resident Land Withholding Tax or RWT application forms, but a fully incorporated NZ limited company standardly receives its IRD number within 1-2 business days of Companies Office confirmation. All IRD registrations must be completed through the myIR portal or authorised intermediary, and the company must maintain a New Zealand registered office and local agent if management is located offshore.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a check-the-box regime. A limited company is conclusively a separate taxable entity with entity-level taxation at the corporate rate. The principal elective mechanism available to qualifying small close-held companies is the Look-Through Company (LTC) election, which permits a maximum of five shareholders who are individuals or qualifying trusts to be taxed on a pass-through basis while retaining limited liability; the LTC must meet the eligibility criteria under sections HA 1 to HA 21 of the Income Tax Act 2007. No election exists to classify a company as a disregarded entity for New Zealand income tax. Trust-elected tax structuring is separate: a discretionary trust can elect to end date its income year or appoint fiduciary income to beneficiaries, but the corporate administrator (NZ_LTD) remains entity-taxed. Hybrid mismatch and branch mismatch rules operate automatically under sections FH 1 to FH 16 of the Income Tax Act 2007, denying deductions for imported hybrid outcomes; they are not elective but can be mitigated by structured documentation showing consistency of foreign entity classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period; non-resident remote-service providers to New Zealand consumers are also required to register if their NZ remote services exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period; suppliers of raw food and certain investment services are subject to special de minimis thresholds under the Special Uplift rule.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies primarily to the export of physical goods from New Zealand under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, provided the supplier has sufficient proof of physical exportation and retention of ownership by the non-resident recipient. Services are zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident person who is not in New Zealand at the time the services are performed and where the services are not supplied directly to a resident New Zealand person who uses or intends to use the services in New Zealand for the purpose of their taxable activity. Additionally, services connected with land situated outside New Zealand, and certain work on movable personal property physically located outside New Zealand, may be zero-rated. For the corporate administrator of an offshore discretionary asset-protection trust, the critical condition is that the recipient of Fiduciary Administration Services is the offshore trustee or trust itself, a non-resident entity, and that the administrative services are not procured by any New Zealand-resident beneficiary or settlor carrying on a taxable activity. The supply must satisfy the 'personal performance' and 'direct benefit' tests. Charitable and deemed zero-rated categories under section 11 also include exported goods destroyed at exportation, and temporary exports returning unaltered. Zero-rating is only available if statutory documentary evidence is retained within the required period, generally before the date of the return; otherwise the standard 15% GST applies.

  • New Zealand Customs Service Electronic Export Declaration (ESE) or an approved departure record for goods exported
  • Bill of Lading, Airway Bill or other internationally recognized transport document evidencing export
  • Commercial Invoice or tax invoice stating the supply is zero-rated under section 11 of the GST Act 1985 and identifying the foreign recipient
  • Executed written contract/service agreement establishing the offshore non-resident beneficiary sourcing the services
  • SWIFT or other internationally recognized bank payment advice showing the net foreign-currency credit for the exported goods or offshore services

New Zealand does not impose a mandatory real-time electronic fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime. Invoicing for GST purposes must contain prescribed particulars in section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; tax invoices may be issued in hard copy or electronic format. New Zealand adopts the voluntary PEPPOL e-invoicing framework and the IRD publishes and maintains an e-Invoicing Service (myIR and secure file transfer channels) for GST filing; however, there is no government-authoritative pre-clearance of individual invoices. Electronic records must be retained in a form that is readily accessible and auditable by the IRD under section 75 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Supply of remote services, digital content and low-value imported goods will follow the IRS 'remote services' rules, with non-resident suppliers required to charge GST at the standard rate on business-to-consumer supplies and register under the GST offshore registration account.

New Zealand law enforces strict export zero-rating substantiation and anti-fraud rules. If the Exporter fails to retain sufficient documentary evidence or supplies are later shown not to have been exported outside New Zealand, the IRD will treat the supply as taxable at 15% GST and assess the original supplier for the shortfall, plus use-of-money interest (currently 8.37% on underpayments) and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994. Without a reasonably arguable position, a 20% shortfall penalty is applied; where the taxpayer has taken a 'dominant purpose' of tax avoidance, a 100% penalty applies; and for evasion or fraudulent conduct, the maximum civil shortfall penalty is 150% of the tax shortfall. The IRD can also recover retrospective clawbacks for up to 4 years (6 years in fraud cases) under section 108 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Where false export documentation is intentionally provided, criminal prosecution may result in imprisonment, fines of up to NZD 50,000 for an individual plus forfeiture of benefits, and conviction-based debarment from GST registration. Directors of corporate trustees or administrators are jointly and severally liable for GST shortfalls caused by the company where they participated in approving the false zero-rating claim. For services exported, the IRD scrutinizes cross-border intra-group payments and requires commercial substance documentation, particularly for discretionary trust ecosystems where New Zealand settlors or beneficiaries may be indirectly financing the trust; if the substance demonstrates that the services are for the benefit of New Zealand-resident individuals, zero-rating is denied and all GST, penalties and interest are assessed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a freely convertible currency under a floating exchange rate regime. The New Zealand Dollar (NZD) is fully convertible for both current account transactions (trade in goods and services, interest, dividends, and fiduciary income) and capital account transactions, subject to general anti-money laundering and sanction screening obligations. There are no exchange controls, no restrictions on the repatriation of profits, dividends, or trust distributions to non-residents, and no requirement for central bank approval for cross-border remittances. However, trustees and fiduciaries must ensure that all remittances are supported by proper underlying documentation to satisfy the financial institutions' compliance obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act). The Inland Revenue Department (IRD) non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) and resident withholding tax (RWT) obligations apply to certain distributions and interest payments, but these do not constitute convertibility hurdles. The practical hurdles are primarily documentary and verification-based, not exchange-control-based.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory requirement for the surrender of export proceeds to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand or any other authority. Exporters and fiduciaries may retain foreign currency earnings in offshore accounts, convert them freely, or remit them to New Zealand without any mandatory conversion ratio or timeline. There is no requirement to repatriate export receipts within a specified period. The only applicable obligations are record-keeping and reporting obligations under the Financial Transactions Reporting Act 1997 (as amended) and the AML/CFT Act, which require prescribed transaction reporting (e.g., for cash transactions exceeding NZD 10,000 or international wire transfers meeting threshold criteria) and suspicious transaction reporting. For a discretionary asset protection trust, the fiduciaries must maintain accurate accounting records of all foreign currency receipts and disbursements to support tax filings with the Inland Revenue Department.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-to-Medium feasibility for offshore digital Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and Payment Facilitators. New Zealand does not have a formal EMI licensing regime equivalent to the EU's Electronic Money Directive. Offshore EMIs can provide ancillary payment services and foreign currency accounts, but they generally are not licensed as banks in New Zealand and are not permitted to accept New Zealand dollar deposits or provide lending services without holding a banking license or a non-bank deposit taker license under the Non-bank Deposit Takers Act 2013. For a high-risk fiduciary trust structure, reliance on an unregulated offshore EMI is likely to trigger enhanced due diligence and may be viewed negatively by New Zealand-registered banks and auditors. The preferred approach is to use tier-1 New Zealand banks for the core fiduciary trust account and cross-border SWIFT wires, with reputable global custody banks or licensed international payment service providers used only for specific foreign currency clearing purposes. The legal constraints include compliance with the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008, and any provider handling money must be registered on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and hold a qualifying dispute resolution membership.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections are routed via SWIFT MT103 wires from the settlor's or contributors' banking institutions to the fiduciary trust account held at a New Zealand tier-1 bank. The wire instruction must include a precise payment purpose code and narrative. For New Zealand matters, the required purpose identification will typically follow the IMF Balance of Payments Manual (BPM6) classification reflected by the New Zealand bank's purpose codes, such as 'Trust Services', 'Personal Capital Transfers', or 'Financial Investment'. The FTRA requires the reporting of international wire transfers under the AML/CFT Act. The receiving bank will require a completed Originating Beneficiary Information form, the source of funds evidence, and the trust deed extract identifying the settlor's entitlement and purpose. The bank must verify that the injection corresponds to a permissible asset protection trust contribution and is not suspicious. Where the injection exceeds the high-value reporting threshold (typically NZD 10,000 for cash transactions, and where unusually large for wire transactions), the bank may require a physical meeting or a solicitor's attestation. All wires must include the trust's legal name, the trustee's name, the New Zealand bank SWIFT/BIC code, and the Trust Account IBAN or equivalent account number.For outbound disbursements, the clearing rails include: (a) SWIFT MT103 single customer credit transfer wires for direct payments to beneficiaries, service providers, trade counterparties, and legal or professional advisers; (b) SWIFT MT202 for bank-to-bank transfers where no direct third-party credit is required; (c) Commercial Letters of Credit (documentary credits issued under UCP 600) for trade settlements where the trust or its underlying trading companies finance goods or services; and (d) international ACH or low-value clearing such as the Trans-Tasman Single Euro Payments Area (SEPA) equivalent or new payments platform (NPP) where applicable, though for high values SWIFT is mandatory. Each outbound disbursement must be strictly aligned with the trust deed's investment and distribution powers, and all payments must be recorded with corresponding source-of-funds documentation and tax withholding certificates where NRWT applies. For trade disbursements under LCs, the bank will require the standard commercial documents: commercial invoices, bills of lading or airway bills, packing lists, certificates of origin, insurance certificates, and inspection certificates, as required by the LC terms. Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a standard 31 March balance date, the IR4 must be filed by 7 July of the calendar year in which the balance date falls. For non-standard balance dates, the filing deadline is generally the 7th day of the 7th month after the balance date. Extended due dates may apply where a tax agent has a provisional arrangement with the Inland Revenue.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Cross-border related-party transactions with an aggregate annual value of NZ$10 million or more trigger the requirement to prepare and retain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Master File and Local File). Multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of NZ$1.1 billion or more are additionally subject to Country-by-Country reporting.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations statute comparable to Cayman or BVI. However, the general anti-avoidance rule in the Income Tax Act 2007 (ss BG 1 and GA 1) and the CFC and foreign trust attribution rules require demonstrable commercial substance, local decision-making and business purpose. A New Zealand limited company acting as trustee of a discretionary asset protection trust must have real physical presence, local board oversight, proper capitalisation, and arm’s length reward for any services provided; otherwise Inland Revenue may recharacterise income, deem dividends, or deny treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Filing

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under s 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994; shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax; criminal fines; imprisonment for up to 5 years; forfeiture of profits attributable to the evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct, participate in, or consent to the evasion are personally liable for civil restitution of the evaded tax and fines; they are subject to travel bans while prosecution is pending and may be imprisoned for up to 5 years.
Shell Company / Sham Trust / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may disregard the corporate or trust form, reattribute trust income to the settlor or resident beneficiaries, reassess tax at 28%, impose shortfall penalties ranging from 20% to 150%, and decline treaty benefits under the anti-abuse clauses of DTAs.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil may be pierced where the company is used for tax avoidance; directors may be personally assessed for unpaid taxes, disqualified from office, and subject to travel restrictions pending resolution of the reassessment.
AML/CFT and Fiduciary Duty Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, civil penalties up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate and up to NZ$200,000 for an individual per contravention; criminal penalties include imprisonment up to 2 years; the qualifying financial services licence may be suspended or cancelled.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly or recklessly permit AML/CFT breaches may incur personal civil penalties, be disqualified from acting as a licensed financial institution director, and face criminal prosecution leading to imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Documentation and Arm's Length Failure

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may make transfer pricing adjustments to taxable income; impose shortfall penalties of 20% for an unacceptable tax position or 40% where the dominant purpose is tax avoidance; apply late payment penalties and use of money interest; and for egregious facts initiate evasion prosecution with a 150% penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly authorise non-arm’s length arrangements may be jointly and severally liable for resultant tax debts; civil penalties and director disqualification can be ordered; travel restrictions may be imposed to secure revenue before departure from New Zealand.
