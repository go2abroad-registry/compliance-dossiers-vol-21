Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Te Tari Taake, IRD / Inland Revenue) of New Zealand; primary digital service channel is the myIR secure online portal accessible via www.ird.govt.nz; Customs matters are separately administered by the New Zealand Customs Service (Te Mana Arai o Aotearoa) through the Trade Single Window (SW) platform.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned New Zealand limited company must first incorporate with the New Zealand Companies Office (under the Companies Act 1993), which automatically issues a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and an IRD number via the Companies Office electronic registration interface. For pre-incorporation or direct registration, the foreign entity may apply online through the IRD Non-Resident Company Registration form (IR 431) or the IRD501 application for an IRD number; the IRD number is issued typically within 5 to 10 business days. A GST number (GST registration) is obtained separately through the same myIR portal by filing the GST registration form (GST 101A) as part of the one-stop registration workflow. A New Zealand resident director or a local agent is required for non-resident incorporations; there is no minimum paid-up capital requirement and no statutory notarization of the memorandum of association at the corporate registry stage.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation: A New Zealand resident limited company is taxed at the entity level on its worldwide income at the corporate income tax rate of 28% (Income Tax Act 2007). It operates under a full imputation system (imputation credit account, ICA) so that distributed dividends carry franking credits to resident shareholders; no look-through or pass-through (check-the-box) classification is available for an ordinary limited company. Non-resident shareholders are subject to non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at 15% on dividends unless reduced under an applicable double tax agreement. The entity is inside the New Zealand territorial and income tax net and is not located in a special tax-exempt jurisdiction or free-zone regime. — A foreign-owned New Zealand limited company must first incorporate with the New Zealand Companies Office (under the Companies Act 1993), which automatically issues a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and an IRD number via the Companies Office electronic registration interface. For pre-incorporation or direct registration, the foreign entity may apply online through the IRD Non-Resident Company Registration form (IR 431) or the IRD501 application for an IRD number; the IRD number is issued typically within 5 to 10 business days. A GST number (GST registration) is obtained separately through the same myIR portal by filing the GST registration form (GST 101A) as part of the one-stop registration workflow. A New Zealand resident director or a local agent is required for non-resident incorporations; there is no minimum paid-up capital requirement and no statutory notarization of the memorandum of association at the corporate registry stage.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A New Zealand resident limited company is taxed at the entity level on its worldwide income at the corporate income tax rate of 28% (Income Tax Act 2007). It operates under a full imputation system (imputation credit account, ICA) so that distributed dividends carry franking credits to resident shareholders; no look-through or pass-through (check-the-box) classification is available for an ordinary limited company. Non-resident shareholders are subject to non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) at 15% on dividends unless reduced under an applicable double tax agreement. The entity is inside the New Zealand territorial and income tax net and is not located in a special tax-exempt jurisdiction or free-zone regime.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not apply the US-style check-the-box election. Instead, the following statutory elections are available: (1) Look-Through Company (LTC) election under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, allowing closely held companies (5 or fewer shareholders, all individuals or trustees) to be transparent for income tax purposes while remaining a separate legal entity; (2) voluntary GST registration election when annual taxable supplies are below the NZD 60,000 threshold (optional registration is irrevocable for at least two years); (3) GST grouping election (sections 55-57 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985), allowing related local companies to be treated as a single GST registrant; (4) election to use the invoice basis for GST output tax timing; (5) hybrid mismatch elections under the BEPS rules (e.g., deemed mismatch and dual-inclusion income elections) affecting branch/permanent establishment deductions for cross-border arrangements; and (6) the New Zealand tax consolidation election (wholly-owned group) under subpart FM of the Income Tax Act 2007. Note that the LTC election triggers a qualifying company election (QC) with shareholder residency restrictions (all resident in NZ); as such, an LTC election is generally unavailable for the described foreign-owned NZ_LTD.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000: A business must register for GST when total taxable supplies in the preceding 12-month period exceeded NZD 60,000, or there are reasonable grounds to expect that the value of taxable supplies in the next 12-month period will exceed NZD 60,000. Voluntary registration is permitted above NZD 0; special compulsory registration applies to non-resident suppliers of remote services (including e-commerce and digital services) under the remote services rules irrespective of turnover once a single NZ customer is served, subject to the NZD 60,000 group-wide threshold for that non-resident class.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under sections 11(1)(a) to (c) of the GST Act 1985 applies to: (i) exported goods removed from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply by or on behalf of the supplier, provided the supplier retains evidence that the goods were exported and that the supplier is the exporter of record or the goods are exported on behalf of the supplier; (ii) services that are supplied to a person who is not resident in New Zealand and who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed (services physically performed outside NZ or contractually supplied to offshore customers); and (iii) services supplied in relation to goods situated outside New Zealand. For a cross-border e-commerce nutraceutical exporter, to claim zero-rating the supplier must ensure (a) the sale is made to a non-resident buyer acquiring for business or personal use abroad, (b) the goods leave New Zealand within 28 days of the invoice date and within 12 months of the supply (extended only with IRD approval), and (c) the supplier retains full export evidence in its GST records for the standard 7-year retention period. Note that the 'remote services' zero-rating (sections 8(4C) to (4E)) also zero-rates online digital services supplied to non-resident recipients, but the physical shipment of dietary supplements must comply with the physical export rules, including the requirement that the supplier or its freight-forwarder produces a valid customs export declaration.

  • Customs Export Declaration (via New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window/ED - accepted electronic declaration), including the exporter reference number, HS 2022 commodity code (e.g., 2106.90 or 3004.50), destination country, and transaction value
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (original or verified electronic copy evidencing physical export from New Zealand port or airport to the offshore consignee, including the name of the overseas buyer and the NZ departure date)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank payment evidence: for New Zealand this is normally the bank's foreign exchange credit advice / SWIFT MT103 credit notification or a bank statement line demonstrating payment in the contracted foreign currency received into the NZ_LTD's bank account; a standalone Inward Remittance Certificate is not issued by NZ banks, but the bank advice serves as the statutory proof of settlement of the foreign sale consideration
  • Commercial invoice and packing list detailing the goods description, quantity, unit value, HS codes, and the zero-rated GST treatment
  • Proof of export within the 28-day window: custom agent's 'Export Confirmation' or the Customs 'Export Entry Released' notification timestamp, plus the carrier's cargo manifest
  • Forwarding instruction and/or freight forwarder certificate of shipment (for consolidated cargo) naming the NZ_LTD as the shipper/consignor

New Zealand does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, API-based continuous controls, or transaction-level digital stamping system for B2B/B2C invoices. E-invoicing is mandated on a B2G basis: all invoices to New Zealand government departments and agencies must be submitted through the Peppol e-invoicing network (e-Invoicing.govt.nz). For B2B e-invoicing the Peppol International (PEPPOL BIS 3.0) format is the recommended standard, and since April 2022 the IRD supports a free e-invoicing solution integrated into myIR. GST returns are filed retroactively (monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly depending on turnover), and there is no transaction-level invoice clearance with the IRD. However, an exporter must maintain a complete digital audit trail, including the GST return data, supplier and customer master files, and export documentation; these records are subject to unannounced IRD audit inspection.

Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, a false or baseless zero-rating declaration triggers: (i) adjustment of output tax to 15% GST on the full export consideration, retrospectively clawed back for up to 7 years from the date of supply; (ii) a shortfall penalty of 20% for not taking reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, or 50% for fraud/evasion, plus use-of-money interest (currently 8.84% per annum for late payment) accruing from the original due date of the GST return; (iii) criminal liability under section 143A (willful evasion of tax) with fines up to NZD 50,000 for individuals or NZD 100,000 for companies and potential imprisonment up to 3 years; (iv) cancellation or suspension of the GST registration; and (v) additional anti-avoidance reassessment under sections GA 1 and GB 1. In cross-border e-commerce, the IRD and NZ Customs jointly audit export flows by comparing Customs export data against GST return outputs under the 'Export Interrogation' data-matching program; a material discrepancy between declared zero-rated exports and actual customs exit records triggers immediate audit and automatic tax demand. Penalty abatement may be available only where the taxpayer demonstrates a 'temporary shortfall' and voluntary disclosure before the IRD initiates audit contact, reducing the shortfall penalty by up to 75%.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions. No exchange control approval is required for trade payments, dividend distributions, or profit repatriation. Capital transfers are generally unrestricted, but may require compliance with the Overseas Investment Act 2005 for sensitive land or significant business assets. Profit remittance by a New Zealand limited company is freely permitted, subject to standard tax clearance, withholding tax, and anti-money-laundering verification obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender requirement on export proceeds. Exporters may retain receipts in foreign currency and are not required to convert into New Zealand dollars within any specified timeline. Export transactions must be reported for balance-of-payments statistical purposes, and supporting commercial documentation must be maintained for customs and tax verification.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-Low. Offshore digital electronic money institutions can provide ancillary payment processing and multi-currency wallets, but they are not registered banks in New Zealand and may not accept deposits or provide banking facilities that require a New Zealand banking licence. Any fintech operating into New Zealand must register on the Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers Act 2008, comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, and be supervised by the Financial Markets Authority or the Department of Internal Affairs as applicable. For high-risk cross-border nutraceutical e-commerce, reliance on an offshore EMI without a physical licensed New Zealand banking relationship is not recommended as a primary clearing rail.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not prescribe a fixed threshold for contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation; however, the IRD's International Questionnaire requires disclosure for cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZ$1 million annually. For country-by-country (CbC) reporting, the consolidated group revenue threshold is EUR 750 million. Recommended documentation: Master File / Local File for controlled transactions exceeding NZ$5 million in aggregate (IR standard practice).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand applies the OECD economic substance principles. A company must have sufficient substance (actual office, employees, decision-making functions) to be a tax resident and to claim treaty benefits. Under the Controlled Foreign Company (CFC) and Interest Limitation Rules, entities without substance may have their income attributed to New Zealand residents. The general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under s BG1 Income Tax Act 2007 pierces arrangements lacking commercial substance. While New Zealand does not have a separate ESR (Economic Substance Regulations) like the UAE, the IRD expects real operational substance for cross-border e-commerce, including inventory sourcing, marketing, order fulfilment, and regulatory compliance (e.g., Medsafe notification).

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Tax Avoidance

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, evasion shortfall penalty of up to 150% of the evaded tax. Criminal prosecution: fines up to NZ$50,000 (individuals) or NZ$250,000 (bodies corporate), and imprisonment for up to 5 years for knowingly providing false returns or aiding evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable under the Companies Act 1993 for allowing the company to trade while insolvent (s 135) or for tax obligations if the company used its assets to evade creditors. Inland Revenue may obtain 'Director Penalty Notice' for unpaid PAYE/GST liabilities; criminal liability attaches to directors who knowingly participate in evasion, including disqualification under s 382 and travel bans/asset freezes under restraint orders and Proceeds of Crime Act 2009.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The IRD may re-characterise transactions under the GAAR, retrospectively deny treaty benefits, impose shortfall penalties of up to 150% for abusive tax position, and cancel registration for GST. The company's tax residency may be disregarded if 'management' is actually exercised offshore. Under the Companies Act 1993, a shell company may be struck off for failing to file returns; dividends can be reclassified as taxable distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company can be personally liable for all tax arrears if they signed a declaration that the company was not insolvent (s 362 Companies Act), and they can be disqualified from managing companies for up to 5 years. For non-resident directors, the IRD may obtain a 'restraining order' preventing departure from New Zealand under s 171 Tax Administration Act 1994, and under reciprocal enforcement arrangements, foreign asset recovery can proceed. Imprisonment up to 5 years for using a shell to defraud revenue.
Anti-Money Laundering (AML) / High-Risk Merchant Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act), a person who carries on a financial activity without registration or compliance faces civil penalties up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate and NZ$300,000 for an individual per breach. Criminal penalties up to 7 years' imprisonment for money laundering under the Crimes Act 1961 s 243. The DIA may issue formal warnings, infringement fees, and public statements. Breaches of the Dietary Supplements Regulations 2021 (Medsafe) can trigger product seizure and fines, with director liability for due diligence failures.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for AML/CFT failures where they knowingly or recklessly permit non-compliance; they can be fined up to NZ$300,000 and imprisoned up to 2 years for false declarations or obstruction of inspections. Under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2009, profits from AML-tainted operations are subject to civil forfeiture and directors may face travel bans preventing exit from NZ during investigations.
Transfer Pricing / Thin Capitalisation Penalties

Statutory sanction: The IRD may make a transfer pricing adjustment under s GC1-GC14 Income Tax Act 2007, disallowing excess interest deductions under the thin capitalisation rules, and impose shortfall penalties up to 150% for an 'abusive tax position' where the taxpayer did not take reasonable care. Interest on underpaid tax accrues at UOMI rates. The company can also lose access to loss carry-forwards and imputation credits.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false transfer pricing documentation or knowingly allow the company to submit misleading International Questionnaires can be held personally liable for the shortfall penalty if they are the 'arranger' of the avoidance arrangement. Under ss 141A-141D Tax Administration Act, directors are liable for civil penalties and may be prosecuted for aiding/abetting evasion, leading to up to 5 years' imprisonment. The IRD can also make personal recovery from directors of a non-resident-owned company for unpaid foreign withholding tax if they were acting as the actual agent for the foreign principal.
Unlicensed Cross-Border Sale of Medical/Legislative Products

Statutory sanction: Breach of the Dietary Supplements Regulations 2021: strict liability fines up to NZ$1,000,000 for a body corporate and up to NZ$200,000 for individuals per offence, plus forfeiture and compliance orders from the Ministry of Health. If prosecuted under the Medicines Act 1981 s 120, selling a product not meeting quality or labelling standards attracts imprisonment up to 3 months and fines. The Commerce Commission may seek civil penalties for misleading advertising under the Fair Trading Act 1986 of up to NZ$600,000 per offence for a company and NZ$200,000 for an individual.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for penalties under the Fair Trading Act 1986 if they were 'knowingly concerned in' the contravention, and criminal liability for product misrepresentation can lead to imprisonment. Directors of a pharmaceutical/nutraceutical company are deemed to authorise unlawful marketing and can be sentenced to imprisonment for up to 2 years under the Medicines Act. Travel bans may be imposed under discovery orders to prevent departure when product safety investigation is ongoing, and under the Criminal Procedure Act 2011 for serious indictable offences.
