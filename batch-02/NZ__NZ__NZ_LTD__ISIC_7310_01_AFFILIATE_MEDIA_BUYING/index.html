A foreign non-resident entity must apply for an IRD number (tax file number) by completing the Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application form (IR742) and submitting it to Inland Revenue. Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to exports of goods (removed from New Zealand within 28 days of supply) and to certain services (e.g., services supplied to non-residents who are outside New Zealand at the time of supply, and the services are not supplied to a person in New Zealand). For digital advertising and media buying services, zero-rating applies if the recipient is a non-resident and the services are not performed in New Zealand, subject to the 'direct export' criteria under Section 11A of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry) lodged via New Zealand Customs Service (Trade Single Window)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill evidencing physical export of goods (if applicable)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank statement showing foreign exchange receipt from the overseas customer
  • Contract or purchase order with the non-resident customer
  • Evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (e.g., overseas tax registration or address)

No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping mandate. GST-registered businesses must issue tax invoices (standard or simplified) containing prescribed details, and retain records for 7 years. Inland Revenue accepts electronic invoices (e-invoicing) via the Peppol network, but it is not mandatory. There is no API-based pre-clearance requirement.

If zero-rating is claimed without sufficient evidence, Inland Revenue may assess GST at 15% on the supply, impose shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax shortfall (depending on whether the error is not deliberate, deliberate, or abusive), and charge use-of-money interest. For fraudulent claims, criminal penalties may apply under the Tax Administration Act 1994, including fines up to NZD 50,000 for individuals and NZD 250,000 for companies, and imprisonment up to 5 years. Retrospective clawback can extend to the original supply date.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised and freely convertible currency regime under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021 and the Foreign Exchange Control Regulations. There are no restrictions on current account transactions, capital account movements, or profit remittances. Non-residents may freely transfer NZD and foreign currency, open accounts, and repatriate capital, dividends, and fees without prior central bank approval. The NZD is a free-floating currency, and the Reserve Bank does not mandate any surrender of export proceeds or conversion obligations. However, the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) imposes reporting and verification duties on financial institutions for cross-border transactions, including prescribed transaction reports for cash and wire transfers over NZD 10,000. Certain capital inflows such as foreign direct investments exceeding NZD 100 million may trigger screening under the Overseas Investment Act 2005, but this does not impose currency exchange restrictions. For the entity described, an affiliate media buying operation conducted via a New Zealand limited company can therefore transact in multiple currencies, hold foreign currency accounts, and remit global advertising payments with minimal exchange control friction, provided standard reporting and AML/CFT obligations are met. The Reserve Bank does not distinguish between onshore and offshore businesses for convertibility purposes, and the medium-risk nature of the sector does not alter the open capital account framework.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export foreign currency receipts in New Zealand. Exporters are entitled to retain 100% of foreign currency earnings in foreign currency accounts and may convert to NZD at their discretion. The Reserve Bank collects balance-of-payments survey data, and the Tax Administration Act requires transferring money from the overseas to be reportable for tax purposes, but no surrender requirement applies. For affiliate media buying services deemed as cross-border exports of digital services, all proceeds can be kept offshore or onshore as the entity prefers.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI providers such as Wise Business and Airwallex are legally permitted to service New Zealand entities under the AML/CFT Act, provided they are registered as financial services providers on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and licensed by the Financial Markets Authority or the Department of Internal Affairs. In practice, Wise has secured a New Zealand money remittance banking partnership and can offer multi-currency accounts, while Airwallex offers global payment accounts with local receiving details. However, tier-1 domestic commercial banks remain the preferred route for the capital escrow and operational treasury due to robust correspondent banking networks and direct SWIFT membership. An EMI cannot replace a licensed New Zealand registered bank for statutory purposes such as capital verification under a capital injection plan or when serving as a party to a bank guarantee. For a medium-risk affiliate media buying entity, an EMI is highly viable as a secondary settlement rail for small-value cross-border affiliate payouts, but the core corporate banking relationship must be with a domestic tier-1 bank to maintain creditability with ad platform partners and tax authorities. Legal constraints: EMIs cannot issue credit facilities and may impose subaccount operation restrictions; a New Zealand entity must still appoint a local agent or compliance officer to meet the FSPR obligations if the EMI is treated as an overseas service provider.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For inbound capital injection from a non-resident parent or shareholder, use SWIFT MT103 with a purpose code consistent with the IMF Balance of Payments classification — specifically code DI (Direct Investment) or, for non-equity finance, code 611 (Loans for direct investment). The domestic NZ tier-1 bank will require a capital verification letter from a registered New Zealand auditor or chartered accountant when funds exceed NZD 50,000; the letter must confirm the capital is credited to the entity's corporate account and not commingled with revenue. The remitter must include a detailed reference such as 'EQUITY INJECTION - SHAREHOLDER LOAN - NZ_LTD - 2025/26' to match the Companies Office records. All funds are freely convertible to NZD at spot by the receiving bank, or can remain in the original currency in a multi-currency account with no conversion obligation. For visa and reserve bank statistical purposes, the entity must report the inbound transfer in its monthly balance-of-payments return if the value exceeds NZD 1 million.For payments to international affiliates and advertising platforms, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 on the bank's correspondent network. Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

NRWT on dividends paid by a New Zealand resident company to a non-resident shareholder is generally 15% under the Income Tax Act 2007, except where the dividend is fully imputed and paid to a resident of a country with a DTA containing a 5% zero-rating provision for wholly-owned subsidiaries (e.g. 5% for Australian non-portfolio dividends; 15% under the OECD DTA). For non-portfolio dividends to DTA countries, the rate is typically 5%; for portfolio dividends under most DTAs, 15%.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The IR4 must be filed by the 7th day of the month following the balance date (standard balance date 31 March: due 7 July annually); however, if the return is filed by an agent, the extended due date is generally 31 March of the following year for a standard balance date, subject to Inland Revenue's agent extension programme. Final income tax must be paid by the terminal tax date, typically 7 February for a standard 31 March balance date (or 7 April for taxpayers with a tax agent and a non-standard balance date). Provisional tax is payable in three instalments during the year under the standard or GST ratio method.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand has no statutory de minimis threshold for local documentation, but a taxpayer with cross-border related-party transactions above NZ$10 million in aggregate value must prepare and keep contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under sections GC 13 and 78 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, consistent with the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines and the Inland Revenue Transfer Pricing Guidelines (IR1044). For a media-buying affiliate with high-volume intercompany transactions (ad inventory procurement, affiliate commissions, and service fees), the NZ$10 million aggregate threshold will almost always be exceeded; Master File and Local File must be prepared. In addition, as of 1 July 2023, New Zealand has implemented Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting requirements for groups with consolidated group revenue of EUR 750 million (NZD equivalent approximately NZ$1.3 billion), requiring the ultimate parent or surrogate parent entity to file with Inland Revenue.

Economic substance scope: Although New Zealand does not have EU-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR), Inland Revenue applies the OECD principal purpose test and permanent establishment attribution rules. For a New Zealand limited company claiming tax residency benefits while its board and management decisions occur offshore, the company's place of effective management is relevant under section YD 2 of the Income Tax Act 2007. A non-resident entity that is centrally managed and controlled from New Zealand is a New Zealand tax resident and is taxable on its worldwide income. Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA), consistent with the OECD's authorised OECD approach, require that the New Zealand limited company that books media-buying income must perform the significant people functions of strategic marketing decisions, contracting, inventory sourcing, and management oversight in New Zealand. If these functions are performed offshore by the parent while the New Zealand entity merely holds invoices, the income is attributable to the offshore head office and the New Zealand entity may be treated as a shell with no economic substance; transfer pricing recharacterisation, withholding taxes, and potential non-resident contractor withholding may apply.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion through contrived related-party media-buying charges (Transfer Pricing Fraud)

Statutory sanction: Sections GC 6 to GC 8 of the Income Tax Act 2007 permit Inland Revenue to disregard the non-arm's length payments and reallocate the profit to the New Zealand entity. A shortfall penalty under section 141D of the Tax Administration Act 1994 of 150% of the resulting tax shortfall applies for evasion or an abusive tax position. Additionally, the promoter penalty under section 141EB may impose NZ$10,000 to NZ$100,000 on any person who promotes a tax avoidance arrangement.

Director personal liability: Section 159 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 imposes a director penalty for PAYE and GST debts; failure to pay corporate income tax after Inland Revenue issues a notice of assessment can make the director personally liable for the unpaid tax if the company cannot pay. Under the Companies Act 1993, a director who permits the company to incur obligations while knowing it is insolvent faces a civil penalty of up to NZ$200,000 and disqualification from managing a company for up to five years (section 383). Criminal liability for tax evasion under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 carries imprisonment of up to five years. Inland Revenue may also pursue a travel ban (lodging a departure prohibition order under section 157 of the Tax Administration Act) preventing a director from leaving New Zealand until tax debts are settled or security is provided.
Sham company / Shell entity lacking economic substance and diversion of business income

Statutory sanction: Under section YD 2(2) of the Income Tax Act 2007, the company's place of effective management may be held to be in New Zealand, making the shell company a New Zealand resident; however, if the parent controls the entity from abroad and the New Zealand entity is a sham, Inland Revenue can void the arrangement under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 (tax avoidance) and reconstruct the taxable position. The company may also be cross-filed under the Financial Reporting Act 2013 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT): failure to maintain a CDD programme for non-resident beneficial owners attracts a civil penalty of up to NZ$200,000 for the company and NZ$100,000 per officer. Offshore companies using New Zealand as a paper presence may be struck off under the Companies Act 1993 and directors banned.

Director personal liability: A director who knowingly allows a company to be a shell for tax evasion is personally liable for the company's unpaid tax under section 159 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 (as a director penalty), and if the director signs false financial statements or tax returns, section 143B applies with imprisonment up to five years. The director can also be liable for a civil penalty of up to NZ$500,000 under section 139 of the Companies Act 1993 if the company trades while insolvent. The court may pierce the corporate veil under Section 633 of the Companies Act or common law where the company is used as a mere sham, rendering the director personally liable for the full tax shortfall plus interest. Departure prohibition orders under section 157 may be issued against the director for non-payment, and the director's name is placed on the publicly searchable Securities Register and the Director Banning Register.
AML/CFT non-compliance and unlicensed financial services in affiliate media remittances

Statutory sanction: If the company provides financial services through ongoing wire transfers and foreign-exchange settlement, it may be treated as a 'financial institution' under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, requiring registration with the Department of Internal Affairs and compliance with the Act. Failure to comply is a strict liability offence with a maximum penalty of NZ$2 million for each liability finding; each transaction can constitute a separate offence. Inland Revenue may impose non-resident withholding tax additional penalties for failure to file RWT/NRWT returns, and the Serious Fraud Office can prosecute under the Crimes Act 1961 for obtaining a pecuniary advantage by deception (imprisonment up to seven years).

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers of AML/CFT-reporting entities are personally liable where they were knowingly concerned in the contravention: the Court may impose a pecuniary penalty of up to NZ$200,000 per contravention for individuals. Directors who fail to prevent the company from being used to launder illicit media-buying proceeds may be charged with money laundering under section 243 of the Crimes Act 1961, carrying imprisonment up to seven years. A director may also be disqualified from being a director under section 383 of the Companies Act 1993, and the High Court may make a pecuniary penalty order for up to three times the benefit derived from the offending. Additionally, Inland Revenue can blacklist the director through a departure prohibition order and sue the director for the company's tax debt under director penalty provisions, effectively piercing the corporate veil to collect the unpaid tax from the director's personal assets.
