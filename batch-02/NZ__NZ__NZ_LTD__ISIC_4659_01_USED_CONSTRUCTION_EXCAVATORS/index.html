Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Taake) of New Zealand, operating through the myIR secure online portal and the New Zealand Customs Service for border controls.

E-government filing portal

A New Zealand-registered limited company obtains its IRD number automatically via the New Zealand Companies Office registration event; the company must then activate myIR and confirm its registration details. A non-resident foreign company deriving New Zealand-source income or making taxable supplies must apply for an IRD number and a GST registration through the Inland Revenue Department's myIR portal. The statutory application process requires the foreign entity to provide certified proof of incorporation, evidence of the registered office, the full legal name and physical residential address, identification of directors and ultimate shareholders, and the appointment of a New Zealand tax agent or authorised liaison if the company is not resident in New Zealand. If the non-resident is registering only for GST, the same myIR application process is used, and the IRD number serves as the combined tax and GST registration number. No entity-level TIN separate from the IRD number exists; the IRD number is the single national taxpayer identifier.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A GST-registered exporter may claim the 0% (zero-rated) GST rate on supplies of used construction excavators and rollers under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 when the goods are exported from New Zealand by the supplier and leave New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply. The supplier must obtain documentary proof of export, the goods must not be consumed in New Zealand, and the sale must be made to a non-resident purchaser or delivered to an overseas destination. Supplies of offshore services connected to the machinery trade may be zero-rated under section 11A where the services are supplied to a person not resident in New Zealand.

  • New Zealand Customs Export Entry (CEE) lodged through the Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from New Zealand
  • Commercial invoice, packing list, and purchase contract
  • Inward foreign exchange repatriation evidence (bank credit advice, SWIFT MT103, or equivalent bank confirmation)

No real-time fiscal clearance mandate exists in New Zealand. GST returns are filed electronically through myIR, and registered businesses must maintain and issue compliant tax invoices under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. E-invoicing is voluntary and based on the PEPPOL interoperability framework; there is no anti-fraud digital stamping requirement.

If the exporter cannot substantiate that the goods were exported or that the sale proceeds were remitted to New Zealand within the required period, the supply is re-characterised as a standard-rated taxable supply at 15% GST, and Inland Revenue may issue a retroactive assessment, impose late payment interest, and apply civil penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 and the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Deliberate false zero-rating claims, document fabrication, or unremitted foreign exchange with intent to evade GST may give rise to criminal prosecution, fines, and imprisonment. Exporters must retain the supporting export and foreign-exchange documentation for at least seven years for audit and verification.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains an open-market liberalised foreign exchange regime. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible, and no foreign exchange control regulations are in force to restrict current international transactions or outward capital transfers, provided settlements are conducted through registered banks and New Zealand tax filing/reporting obligations are met. New Zealand has accepted Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement, entailing the absence of exchange controls or multiple currency practices. The principal gate is the Overseas Investment Act 2005; non-resident investment into a used-machinery trading company that does not touch sensitive land or exceed the current significant-business-assets threshold requires no Reserve Bank approval. Profit remittance is unrestricted from an exchange-control perspective: a New Zealand Ltd may pay dividends to a foreign shareholder after directors satisfy the Companies Act solvency test and Inland Revenue non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) is deducted where appropriate. There is no obligation to surrender export proceeds or to obtain permission for bank account funding, and no requirement that invoices be converted into NZD. The applicable checks are bank AML/CFT treatment, sanctions screening, and transfer-pricing substance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rules apply in New Zealand. Mandatory conversion ratio is 0% and there is no legally specified timeline for converting export invoices into NZD. Exporters may hold foreign currency receipts in an onshore foreign-currency account or freely sell them to a registered bank at the prevailing market rate. For trade-based AML/CFT compliance, a bank may require the export invoice, proof of shipment (e.g., bills of lading or cargo manifests), and customs export entry before crediting proceeds, but this is documentary verification, not an exchange-control surrender. IRD reporting requirements (e.g., GST on exported second-hand goods, if applicable) continue to apply.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for ancillary cash management and inbound collections, Low as a sole treasury or capital-escrow rail. Foreign non-bank electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as offshore digital wallets and FX platforms are not registered banks in New Zealand and cannot provide deposit protection, formal Letters of Credit, bank guarantees, or trade loans under the Banking Prudential Supervision Act 1989. An offshore EMI is legally barred from servicing an NZ entity unless it is registered on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) for the relevant services and has an AML/CFT programme under the AML/CFT Act 2009. Even where such registration exists, an EMI cannot replace a Tier-1 bank for capital verification by an NZ auditor, settlement under a Commercial LC (UCP600), or documentary collections through a bank negotiator. The optimal blueprint uses a Tier-1 domestic bank for capital escrow and trade finance; an EMI may be used only for low-value supplier deposits or foreign-currency operating balances, with full reconciliation and sanctions screening maintained.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward equity capital from the foreign parent should be wired as a SWIFT MT103 customer transfer to the NZ entity's capital escrow or equity subscription account at a Tier-1 registered bank. The remittance must state the purpose as 'DIRECT INVESTMENT - EQUITY CAPITAL' and include the invoice/reference number; New Zealand does not prescribe a national FX purpose-code list, but the bank will map the transfer under OECD Balance of Payments category 1.3 (Direct Investment Equity) for record-keeping. No Reserve Bank approval is required before receipt; however, if the NZ company will acquire sensitive land or significant business assets above the current Overseas Investment Act threshold, an OIO consent application must be filed before completion. Upon receipt, the bank will issue an inward foreign exchange settlement ticket if conversion to NZD is made; the shareholder resolution, subscription agreement, bank confirmation, inward remittance advice, and updated share register from the Companies Act allotment should be retained to prove capital verification. The funds must not be disbursed until the account is active and the bank confirms the source-of-funds review is complete.For import-side settlements from New Zealand to overseas used-machinery vendors, the preferred rails are: (i) SWIFT MT103 for open-account payments or advance deposits against pro-forma invoices; (ii) Commercial Letters of Credit issued via MT700 under UCP600 for larger auctions or high-risk jurisdictions, with sight drafts and documents consisting of Bill of Lading, commercial invoice, packing list, and original OEM certificate; and (iii) Documentary Collections (DP/DA) using a bank transmission such as MT400 or MT410 when the buyer wants verification of documents without LC risk. For the export side from NZ to end buyers, receive funds by MT103; or ask the buyer's bank to open an LC in favour of the NZ entity, with the NZ exporting bank acting as confirming or negotiating bank. All outbound payments must embed invoice numbers and machine serial numbers in the remittance field, and the bank's Trade Finance desk should screen corresponding banks for OFAC and NZ sanctions compliance. NZD high-value settlements clear through RBNZ's Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) with SWIFT message continuity, while USD/EUR/GBP settlements use the bank's foreign correspondent clearing network.Deliverable FX forwards in NZD/USD, NZD/AUD, NZD/EUR, and NZD/JPY for firm import/export exposures, executed under an ISDA 1998 FX Plus agreement or the bank's standard treasury terms, limited to underlying traded goods and with no leverage beyond the invoice value.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) for emerging-market trade currencies that are not freely deliverable in New Zealand (e.g., IDR, VND, NGN), only where a genuine used-machinery purchase or sale is evidenced by a contract and the NDF notional equals the trade receivable or payable under the invoice.FX swaps and cross-currency swaps to hedge working-capital mismatches between NZD operating funds and foreign-currency settlement accounts, approved by the board in a treasury policy that prohibits speculative uncovered positions.European-style FX options (including zero-premium collars) for contingent tenders or auction bids where the quantity and price are not yet committed, provided the underlying trade documentation is maintained for the bank's periodic review.

Strike-off trigger

Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: Under the Companies Act 1993 and Financial Reporting Act 2013, a mandatory independent audit by a licensed/certified CPA auditor applies where the company, including controlled subsidiaries, satisfies either (i) consolidated total assets exceeding NZD 60,000,000, or (ii) consolidated annual turnover exceeding NZD 30,000,000, in at least one of the two most recent consecutive accounting periods. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10 million

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) regime of the type imposed in zero-tax jurisdictions, but the Commissioner applies OECD-aligned transfer pricing rules and the general anti-avoidance provision in section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007. Treaty benefits and PE profit attribution require the existence of substantial operational activity in New Zealand. Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) analysis is used to determine whether significant people functions exist in New Zealand for PE attribution, and thin-capitalisation rules restrict interest deductions for non-resident controlled entities. A company that cannot demonstrate commercial substance, real offices, actual trading risk, and substantive employees may be recharacterised as a sham or its financing deductions denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under section 144 of the Tax Administration Act 1994: the company is liable to a fine of up to NZD 50,000 and the offending director to imprisonment for a term not exceeding 10 years; the Commissioner also imposes a 150% evasion shortfall penalty and recovers use-of-money interest on the unpaid tax.

Director personal liability: The director who signs or causes the false return is personally jointly and severally liable for the unpaid tax under section 165 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Inland Revenue may issue a departure prohibition order for unpaid tax debt exceeding NZD 10,000, freezing the director's ability to leave New Zealand. The court may also confiscate the director's assets under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 and prohibit the director from managing a company under the Companies Act 1993.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner adjusts the taxable income under sections GC 4 to GC 8 of the Income Tax Act 2007; the transfer pricing shortfall penalty is 20% for unsubstantiated documentation, escalating to 40% for gross carelessness and up to 150% for evasion. In addition, a NZD 20,000 penalty may be charged for failure to provide the mandatory transfer pricing documentation where cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZD 10 million.

Director personal liability: Directors who instructed or approved the artificial pricing and who fail to take reasonable steps to ensure accurate documentation are personally liable for the company's tax shortfall and the documentation penalty. New Zealand courts will pierce the corporate veil where the company is used as a façade for the directors' overseas asset shifting; directors face civil asset restitution, a departure prohibition order, disqualification from managing companies, and imprisonment if the manipulation is part of a wider fraud.
Trade-Based Money Laundering and False Customs Declarations

Statutory sanction: Violation of the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 carries corporate penalties that can reach NZD 2 million for a reporting entity; customs offences under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 attract forfeiture of goods and vessels, fines, and, for serious fraud, imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers who knowingly participate in the money laundering scheme face criminal liability under the Crimes Act 1961 and the AML/CFT Act 2009. Personal civil liability includes restitution of laundered funds, asset freezing orders, seizure and forfeiture of personal property, mandatory blacklisting in the FIU watch-list, travel bans under the Tax Administration Act for unpaid tax, and imprisonment for up to 7 years where money laundering forms part of a major transaction.
