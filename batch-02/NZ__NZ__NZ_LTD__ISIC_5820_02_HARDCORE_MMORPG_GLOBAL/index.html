Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office, a division of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE), operating through the Companies Register online portal (companiesoffice.govt.nz).

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (New Zealand), in particular sections 10-14 (registration), 15-22 (company names), 151-158 (director requirements), 189-199 (share capital and share registers), 200-204 (financial records), and 386A (New Zealand Business Number integration).

Formation / constitutional filings

Application for Registration of a Company filed under section 12 of the Companies Act 1993, accompanied by director consents and subscriber details; registration is perfected by the Registrar issuing a Certificate of Incorporation under section 14. Filing fee: NZ$105.20 (approximately US$64).

Incorporation processing time

⚡ 24 business days (SOP sum)

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed by the Companies Act 1993. The company must issue and allot at least one share; market practice is 1,000 ordinary shares at NZ$1.00 each, fully or partly paid. No escrow deposit, capital contribution, or share-premium account is required before incorporation, and a wholly non-resident shareholder may own 100% of the shares without prior exchange control approval.

Funding & unlock path: Not required for a telework or cloud-based digital operation. If a physical office is leased in New Zealand, the occupier must: (i) obtain a building warrant of fitness (BWOF) compliance schedule under the Building Act 2004 for any specified systems such as fire alarms, sprinklers, or mechanical ventilation; (ii) comply with the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, including worker engagement and participation duties and hazard management obligations; and (iii) notify WorkSafe New Zealand of any notifiable work. There is no separate 'civil defense clearance' under New Zealand law; territorial authority building inspections and fire-safety certifications satisfy all statutory requirements.

Industrial Access / EIA & Civil Defense Approval Exempted / Not Required

Not applicable - ISIC 5820 digital software publishing is an unlicensed activity in New Zealand, and no industrial license is issued by any authority. The general business-registration umbrella is the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE) and the New Zealand Companies Office, but neither issues an operating permit for MMORPG publishing. The only ancillary regulator touching content is the Office of Film and Literature Classification, which does not grant a pre-market license but may impose classification and labeling obligations.

Category: Nil - No Operating License Required. There is no separate computer-game-publisher operating license under New Zealand law. The Films, Videos, and Publications Classification Act 1993 may require age-rated labeling of the game, but this is a content-labeling obligation and not a licensing precondition to operating the business.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue), New Zealand Government; sole national tax administration and tax registrar for income tax, GST, PAYE and withholding taxes. Online service portal: myIR (ird.govt.nz/myIR); official website: ird.govt.nz. Entity incorporation and NZBN registration are administered by the New Zealand Companies Office, Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE), which coordinates integrated tax registrations at formation.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 - Incorporate the NZ_LTD: registration is effected online via the Companies Office (companies-register.companiesoffice.govt.nz); the integrated registration service automatically issues the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN), the Companies Register number and the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) number in a single filing. Step 2 - Confirm income tax registrations: the IRD number is the statutory tax file number (TIN); the company must complete its IRD file details, including director, shareholder and registered office information, through myIR; an NZ resident company is taxed on worldwide income while a non-resident company is assessed only on New Zealand-sourced income. Step 3 - Foreign-incorporated companies: an offshore company carrying on business in NZ must apply for an IRD number using form IR596 (IRD Number Application for a Non-Resident Company) supported by certified certificate of incorporation, constitution, director list, registered office address and banker confirmation; the same IRD number serves as the GST registration number. Step 4 - GST/VAT registration: statutory registration is compulsory when taxable supplies made in NZ exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period (GST Act section 51); registration is made via myIR, and non-resident suppliers of remote services may use the simplified remote-services GST registration and file quarterly returns on a pay-only (cash) basis; voluntary registration is permitted. Step 5 - Ancillary registrations: PAYE (employer withholding), fringe benefit tax, non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends, interest and royalties, and CRS/FATCA financial account declarations are completed in the same myIR account.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box regime, but the following statutory elections materially determine classification and tax outcomes for an NZ_LTD: (1) Look-through company election (form IR525; Income Tax Act 2007 subpart HB): a close-held company with up to five look-through owners (individuals, trustees or other LTCs) may elect fiscal transparency so that income, deductions and credits flow through to shareholders; non-resident individual shareholders are permitted but taxed only on NZ-source income. (2) Imputation credit account election: a company elects (in its first income tax return) to maintain an imputation credit account so that NZ resident shareholders receive imputation credits for corporate tax paid on dividends. (3) Consolidated group and loss-grouping elections: companies within a 100% common ownership group may elect to transfer losses and file consolidated returns. (4) GST grouping election: related companies may elect to be treated as a single GST group with a nominated representative member. (5) GST accounting basis election: eligibility to account for GST on an invoice basis or payments (cash) basis; non-resident remote-services suppliers may elect the simplified pay-only basis. (6) Hybrid and branch mismatch elections: NZ applies the OECD hybrid and branch mismatch rules (subpart FH); no elective mismatch is available, but taxpayers must disclose MNE structures via IRD form IR817 and may elect ordering of the rules for dual-inclusion income and imported mismatch deductions. (7) CFC/FIF elections: the NZ parent may elect the FIF fair dividend rate method and the CFC active-business exclusion treatment for offshore gaming subsidiaries, where applicable.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period of taxable supplies made in New Zealand. For non-resident suppliers of remote services (including downloadable MMORPG software, subscriptions and in-app purchases), the threshold tests the projected value of supplies to NZ-resident consumers over the following 12 months. Where the supply is made through an electronic marketplace such as Apple App Store or Google Play, the marketplace operator is deemed to be the supplier for GST purposes, so the NZ_LTD developer is generally not required to register for GST in respect of those NZ B2C sales; the marketplace operator collects and remits the 15% GST.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST is available for genuine exports. Goods: the supplier must export the goods from NZ within 28 days of supply and hold contemporaneous evidence of export (section 61(1)(c) of the GST Act and the Evidence of Export Rules), namely a customs export declaration, bill of lading or air waybill. Services: supplies to a non-resident who is outside NZ when the services are performed can be zero-rated unless the services are connected with NZ land or goods in NZ. Digital gaming revenue: remote services supplied to non-resident end-customers are outside the scope of NZ GST altogether (sections 8(3) and 8C); sales to NZ consumers through Apple/Google marketplaces are deemed supplies by the marketplace operator. To substantiate the zero-rating or out-of-scope treatment, the NZ_LTD must maintain two-fold evidence of customer location and payment (device IP address, billing address, app-store payout reports, bank remittance advices) and reconcile all export lines in its GST returns and annual income tax return. If the NZ_LTD is GST-registered, it may claim input tax credits for NZ business costs and obtain cash refunds of net credits from Inland Revenue on a regular cycle.

  • Customs Export Declaration lodged electronically via the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading (sea freight) or Air Waybill (air freight) evidencing physical export and destination
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List with full supplier, consignee and destination details
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank foreign-exchange credit advice evidencing receipt of export proceeds
  • Executed sales contract or purchase order (for digital services: electronic supply contract, IP geo-location logs, and app-store payout statements)
  • GST return reconciliation worksheet identifying zero-rated export lines and the corresponding evidence of export records

New Zealand mandates no real-time fiscal clearance, no digital stamping, no continuous transaction controls and no pre-clearance API for tax invoices. A registered person must issue a GST tax invoice within 28 days for any taxable supply exceeding NZD 50 (GST-only invoices for exempt purchasers), and must retain records sufficient to verify the GST treatment. Electronic invoicing is permitted and the Government has adopted the Peppol e-invoicing framework on a voluntary basis (New Zealand Peppol Authority, MBIE); Inland Revenue itself is an early adopter. The only mandatory electronic channel is the filing of GST and income tax returns through myIR, and the electronic lodgement of customs export declarations through the Trade Single Window operated by the New Zealand Customs Service. Inland Revenue's operational practice for remote services (GST and the digital economy) requires platform suppliers to maintain digital records of the customer's location evidence, payment rail and transaction logs for audit inspection.

Inland Revenue actively audits export zero-rating claims for digital exporters. Where zero-rating conditions are not met (including goods not exported within 28 days or insufficient evidence), the supply is recharacterised as taxable at 15% GST; the previously refunded output tax is clawed back with use-of-money interest from the date of refund and late payment penalties (1% per month on the unpaid amount, increasing to 2% after the first month). Tax shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 scale with culpability: 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 50% for an abusive tax position and 100% for evasion, applied per affected GST period. Fraudulent zero-rating (including false customs declarations, fabricated bills of lading or counterfeit remittance certificates) is a criminal offence under section 143B, carrying fines up to NZD 250,000 for a company and director-level personal liability, together with potential asset confiscation under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009; the reassessment window extends to eight years in evasion cases and is unlimited where intent to defraud is established. Practical consequences also include deregistration of GST status, denial of refund claims, public disclosure of the assessment, and mandatory future rulings before zero-rating digital export revenue.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a free-floating currency with full current account and capital account convertibility. No general exchange control restrictions exist under the Foreign Investment Act 2005 or exchange control regulations; non-resident profit remittances are permitted subject to standard tax withholding requirements under the Income Tax Act 2007. Dividend remittance is unrestricted, but a resident withholding tax (RWT) or non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) may apply at 15% or a reduced treaty rate. Capital repatriation is free for non-resident investors.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender obligation exists in New Zealand. Export receipts may be held in foreign currency accounts with any registered New Zealand bank, retained offshore, or converted to NZD without mandatory reporting or conversion ratios. Certain anti-money laundering record-keeping obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 apply to cross-border wire transfers of NZD 10,000 or more, but no conversion or surrender is mandated.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) and the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for market conduct; foreign exchange transfer functions rest with the Reserve Bank as the monetary authority and prudential regulator for banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. New Zealand does not provide a dedicated electronic money institution (EMI) licensing category. Digital banks and non-bank financial institutions must either hold a banking licence from the Reserve Bank under the Banking Prudential Requirements or operate as a registered financial service provider and AML/CFT reporting entity under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008. Offshore EMIs may be able to partner with New Zealand-based banks for local clearing, but they cannot independently provide local domiciled accounts. Legal constraints include the need for NZX-regulated money remittance registration, a New Zealand business address, and compliance with the AML/CFT Act. For this MMORPG treasury, using an offshore EMI as a primary liquidity rail is suboptimal; direct banking with a tier-1 Australian-owned New Zealand bank is more robust.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant protocol: shareholder or parent company initiates a SWIFT MT103 wire transfer from a corporate bank account to the NZ Ltd operational/non-resident capital account at the designated New Zealand bank. The wire instruction must include: beneficiary legal name, NZ bank BIC/SWIFT, NZ domestic account number (ANZ, ASB, BNZ or Westpac), payment purpose code 'SUPPLY' (intercompany capital contribution) or 'CAPITAL INJECTION', the NZD equivalent amount, and a tax identifier for the beneficiary. The remitter should obtain the New Zealand bank's local clearing code (e.g., ANZ 01-, ASB 12-, BNZ 02-, Westpac 03-) to ensure straight-through processing. For amounts above NZD 10,000, a completed transaction monitoring report and AML verification for the source of funds will be retained. For inward direct investment, a notification under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 is required only if the investor acquires 25% or more ownership of a significant business asset; a wholly owned NZD treasury entity used solely for capital maintenance is generally not considered significant unless the business holds sensitive land or a substantial asset value.Primary clearing rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 for intercompany supplier payments, vendor royalties, licensing fees, and cross-border operational expenses. (2) Commercial Letter of Credit (LC) payable at sight or deferred basis for large software service licences and hosting arrangements where physical or software goods are procured from trade counterparties. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium. New Zealand does not provide a dedicated electronic money institution (EMI) licensing category. Digital banks and non-bank financial institutions must either hold a banking licence from the Reserve Bank under the Banking Prudential Requirements or operate as a registered financial service provider and AML/CFT reporting entity under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008. Offshore EMIs may be able to partner with New Zealand-based banks for local clearing, but they cannot independently provide local domiciled accounts. Legal constraints include the need for NZX-regulated money remittance registration, a New Zealand business address, and compliance with the AML/CFT Act. For this MMORPG treasury, using an offshore EMI as a primary liquidity rail is suboptimal; direct banking with a tier-1 Australian-owned New Zealand bank is more robust.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Compulsory transfer pricing documentation for cross-border related-party transactions exceeding: NZ$1 million (services), NZ$2 million (loans/interest-bearing arrangements), and NZ$10 million (other transactions including IP royalty and goods). In addition, New Zealand groups with annual revenue exceeding NZ$50 million must maintain a TP position statement, and multinational groups with consolidated revenue above EUR 750 million must file a Country-by-Country report through IRD.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) statute, but the 'economic substance' principle operates through the CFC regime, the branch equivalent tax rules, and general anti-avoidance provisions (Income Tax Act 2007, ss BG 1 and GA 1). A non-resident-owned NZ Ltd must have real local management, employees, and board decision-making for the MMORPG operation; otherwise, the IRD may disregard the entity or reattribute profits, and DTA benefits can be denied under the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) in Article 25 of NZ's DTAs as implemented under the MLI.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Under-Reporting

Statutory sanction: Criminal conviction under Tax Administration Act 1994 ss 143B, 143C, 143D; fines up to NZ$250,000 for a company and up to NZ$50,000 for an individual, plus a shortfall penalty of 50% of the evaded tax, statutory interest on the unpaid amount, and imprisonment for up to five years for individuals.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted personally as primary offenders, subject to criminal fines and imprisonment. Civil liability for fraudulent trading (Companies Act 1993 s 320) allows courts to order directors to make personal restitution to creditors, and the Inland Revenue Department can seek director penalty notices for unpaid PAYE/GST and, in debt cases, for income tax.
Shell Company / Treaty Shopping / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Under the general anti-avoidance rule (s BG 1), the IRD can cancel tax advantages and recharacterise the structure; a 100% shortfall penalty applies for a dominant purpose of tax avoidance; specific anti-treaty-shopping provisions deny treaty benefits; deemed withholding tax adjustments of 30% NRWT on recharacterised dividends can be imposed.

Director personal liability: Directors who are knowingly involved in a sham arrangement can be held personally liable under the sham/agency principles and the Companies Act; Inland Revenue may obtain freezing orders on personal assets, and in serious cases the courts may disqualify directors and restrict international travel pending criminal investigation.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing / Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: An IRD transfer pricing adjustment to substitute an arm's length price, resulting in additional income tax at 28% plus use-of-money interest; a 20% shortfall penalty for failing to take reasonable care, 40% for taking an unacceptable tax position, or 100% for evasion; mandatory documentation penalties of NZ$10,000 plus NZ$2,000 per day for continued failure to provide documents.

Director personal liability: Directors can be liable for shortfall penalties if they signed or approved inaccurate tax returns with wilful blindness; the Companies Act s 135 (reckless trading) allows a court to impose personal liability for creditor losses, and the IRD is a preferential creditor.
GST Evasion on Remote Digital Services

Statutory sanction: Compulsory GST registration is required from the first NZ$60,000 of supplies to NZ consumers; evasion triggers a notional GST assessment at 15%, a 100% shortfall penalty for evasion, and late payment penalties; a fine of up to NZ$250,000 and forfeiture of assets under the Tax Administration Act.

Director personal liability: Directors of the NZ company can be made personally liable for GST debts under the director penalty regime (ss 161H-161J Tax Administration Act 1994) if the company fails to pay GST in dissolution or liquidation; travel bans can be imposed under the District Court Act for non-compliance with assessment orders.
AML/CFT Non-Compliance (Digital Payment Flows)

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, civil penalties for each breach can be up to NZ$800,000 for a company and NZ$160,000 for individuals; criminal penalties for reckless money laundering carry imprisonment up to 7 years; the AML/CFT supervisor can issue compliance notices and suspend the entity's operations.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally exposed for knowing participation in money laundering (Crimes Act 1961 s 243); the High Court can impose director disqualification orders and asset freezing; non-resident directors are not immune and may face extradition or travel bans for AML offences committed in New Zealand.
