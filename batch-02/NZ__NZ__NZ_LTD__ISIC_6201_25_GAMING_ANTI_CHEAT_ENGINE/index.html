Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No minimum paid-up capital rule applies to a New Zealand limited liability company. At least 1 share must be issued upon incorporation, which may be subscribed and paid as agreed by the directors; there is no statutory escrow or blocked bank account requirement. The share register must record the number of shares, issue price, and payment status.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand has no check-the-box regime. An eligible close-held company may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status under the Income Tax Act 2007 to flow profits and losses through to shareholders, and wholly-owned groups may elect consolidated group tax treatment under the group tax consolidation rules. New Zealand's hybrid mismatch rules in Part FM of the Income Tax Act 2007 apply integrity adjustments and do not provide elective hybrid-mismatch elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period; mandatory GST registration applies once this threshold is met or exceeded. Voluntary registration is available below the threshold. Non-resident offshore suppliers of remote and digital services to New Zealand consumers are also required to register when their New Zealand consumer supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated for New Zealand GST when supplied for export and exported within the time periods prescribed by the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, including supplier-exported goods generally exported within 28 days of the time of supply, goods exported by a non-resident customer within the applicable statutory export window, and goods delivered into a customs-controlled area for export. Services, including digital gaming anti-cheat middleware, software licences, cloud services, and integrity-engine services, are zero-rated when supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed and when the services are not supplied directly in connection with New Zealand land or New Zealand moveable property. For qualifying offshore digital services, the supplier must retain evidence demonstrating the customer's non-resident status, overseas business location, and offshore service delivery. Zero-rating is denied where goods are diverted for domestic use or where services are performed in or enjoyed in New Zealand.

  • Export declaration lodged through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window (export entry approval and validation record)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure from New Zealand (for digital services, the contract, service order, and IP delivery log confirming offshore delivery)
  • Foreign-currency bank remittance advice or SWIFT MT103 evidencing the inward payment from the non-resident customer
  • Signed software licence agreement, purchase order, anti-cheat middleware subscription records, and evidence of the customer's non-resident registration or overseas business address

New Zealand does not mandate real-time electronic invoice clearance, API stamping, anti-fraud digital stamps, or tax-authority pre-approval of individual invoices. E-invoicing is available through the voluntary Peppol network and for business-to-government transactions. GST-registered suppliers must issue statutory GST tax invoices containing the supplier's name, address, IRD number, invoice date, description, quantity, GST-exclusive price, GST amount, and total payable; tax invoices for supplies of NZD 1,000 or more must also show the recipient's name and address. GST returns and supporting digital records must be filed electronically with Inland Revenue, but there is no mandatory per-invoice clearance mechanism.

Inland Revenue may audit export zero-rating by verifying the customs export declaration, transport documents, export timestamps, foreign-exchange remittance evidence, customer residence status, and the substantive offshore use of the services or goods. If the statutory export conditions are not satisfied, the supply is reclassified as taxable at 15% GST, output tax is recovered, and use-of-money interest is imposed. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 may apply at increasing rates for failure to exercise reasonable care, gross carelessness, abusive tax avoidance, and evasion or knowingly providing false GST information, up to a maximum shortfall penalty of 150%. Input tax credits previously claimed in respect of the diverted or unverified supplies may also be clawed back, and criminal prosecution may follow in cases of intentional misrepresentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised foreign exchange regime. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) sets monetary policy but does not impose exchange controls, prior approvals, surrender obligations, or administrative restrictions on current or capital account transactions. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely floating and fully convertible. Residents and non-residents may lawfully hold foreign currency bank accounts in New Zealand, receive export proceeds in any currency, convert them at will, and remit capital, dividends, royalty, interest, or loan principal abroad without RBNZ consent. The Overseas Investment Act 2005 may require consent for overseas persons purchasing sensitive land or significant business assets, but an ordinary inwards equity subscription into a New Zealand operating limited company is not a regulated transaction under that Act. Profit repatriation from an NZ Ltd is not blocked by FX rules; the only practical frictions are tax-related, namely Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) on dividends, interest, and royalties, the Approved Issuer Levy (AIL) for related-party cross-border borrowing, and standard tax filing/reporting obligations. There is no capital account convertibility license and no exchange-trading earmarking requirement. Thus the treasury structure can be built on a clean NZD/USD multi-currency platform without needing central bank waivers or special FX licensing.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion timeline, and no minimum conversion ratio for export proceeds in New Zealand. Exporters may retain foreign currency receipts indefinitely in a foreign currency account, invoice and settle in any freely usable currency, and decide at their own commercial discretion whether and when to convert receipts into NZD. Banks may require customers to state the underlying commercial purpose for Balance of Payments statistical reporting, but the RBNZ does not operate a surrender scheme and no entity is required to sell export earnings to the central bank.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Conditionally viable as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail, but not as a primary treasury bank. Fintech EMIs and payment service providers such as Airwallex, Wise, or Stripe may lawfully service New Zealand corporates if they are registered Financial Service Providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008 and comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. They can support low-value cross-border collections, card payments, and fast settlement, but they are not registered banks, cannot take deposits in the prudential sense, cannot issue commercial letters of credit, cannot provide documentary collections, and usually cannot evidence statutory capital escrow to a third-party compliance committee. The NZ Ltd should therefore use a Tier-1 domestic bank as the settlement anchor and treat Stripe as a merchant collection gateway only. No legal prohibition applies, but the bank-centric structure is materially safer for trade finance, FX hedging, and capital verification.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital should be wired from the overseas shareholder parent account to the NZ Ltd's dedicated NZD capital / operating account at a Tier-1 New Zealand bank using SWIFT MT103. The wire must identify the remitter, the NZ Ltd legal name, the NZBN / company number, and the payment purpose coded as inward direct investment, equity subscription, or shareholder loan. The bank will require the company's bank resolution and proof of signatory authority. Upon receipt, the company must file a share allotment notification with the New Zealand Companies Office and retain the share subscription agreement, board minutes, and source-of-funds evidence. No statutory capital verification certificate is required because New Zealand imposes no minimum capital requirement, but for tax and balance-sheet cleanliness the directors should record the contribution explicitly as either share capital or a subordinated shareholder loan. For intercompany debt, execute a formal loan deed, consider an Approved Issuer Levy election to reduce Non-Resident Withholding Tax, and ensure the remittance ticket contains the correct purpose code to avoid misclassification for RBNZ balance-of-payments statistics.Operational outflows such as cloud-host fees, software licences, AI-model training costs, legal retainers, and engineering salaries should be settled using SWIFT MT103 from the NZD or USD operating account, with invoice currency matching to avoid unnecessary FX conversion. For cross-border intellectual property royalties or software distribution commissions, the payment instruction must include the royalty/IP purpose code and the NZ Ltd must apply the required NRWT or AIL treatment. For procurement of hardware components or high-value vendor contracts where documentary security is needed, commercial letters of credit issued through the Tier-1 NZ bank under UCP600 (MT700) are permissible and protect the company against vendor non-delivery. For moderate-risk trade transactions where speed is preferred but full LC security is not justified, documentary collections (courier documents under D/P or D/A terms) can be used. All payments should be screened against sanctions lists, and the treasury function should maintain a rolling 13-week cash-flow model to ensure settlement-date funding in the correct currency.Outright deliverable NZD/USD forward contracts with an RBNZ-registered Tier-1 bank, typically transacted on ISDA/CUMA master agreements for maturities up to 12 months and rollovers permitted with underlying commercial exposure.Cross-currency swaps for long-term shareholder loans or substantial acquisition bridge funding, subject to board treasury policy, ISDA documentation, and bank credit approval; these instruments must be limited to hedging identified balance-sheet or cash-flow exposures.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are permissible only for invoiced trade flows involving currencies with onshore convertibility restrictions, such as CNY, INR, or IDR; the NZD itself is fully deliverable, so an NZD NDF is unnecessary and should not be used.FX vanilla options and zero-cost collars are permissible to cap adverse exchange-rate moves on committed licence fee streams, premiums and mark-to-market reserves must be disclosed under the company's IFRS standards, and speculative option-writing is prohibited by treasury policy.Boundary: all hedging instruments must evidence a documented underlying commercial exposure, hedge tenors must align with contract maturities, and the board must approve the treasury risk policy, dealer limit list, and maximum notional exposure limits.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Conditionally viable as a supplementary collection/disbursement rail, but not as a primary treasury bank. Fintech EMIs and payment service providers such as Airwallex, Wise, or Stripe may lawfully service New Zealand corporates if they are registered Financial Service Providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008 and comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. They can support low-value cross-border collections, card payments, and fast settlement, but they are not registered banks, cannot take deposits in the prudential sense, cannot issue commercial letters of credit, cannot provide documentary collections, and usually cannot evidence statutory capital escrow to a third-party compliance committee. The NZ Ltd should therefore use a Tier-1 domestic bank as the settlement anchor and treat Stripe as a merchant collection gateway only. No legal prohibition applies, but the bank-centric structure is materially safer for trade finance, FX hedging, and capital verification.
