Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office is permissible for a Low_Risk_Standard Micro-SaaS company only if it provides a physical street address in New Zealand and real-time mail handling; a PO Box, bare commercial mailbox, or shared accommodation address without a physical reception point will not satisfy section 186 of the Companies Act 1993. The Inland Revenue Department generally accepts a virtual office with a fixed New Zealand postal address and mail forwarding capability, but it may request the actual business premises of the founder. For banking, most New Zealand banks reject a pure commercial mailbox and require either a director's residential address or the physical office of an authorized incorporation agent. If the company uses Paddle or Lemon Squeezy as its sole payment rails and does not open a local bank account, a registered virtual office is operationally acceptable; if a New Zealand bank account is required, engage an licensed corporate service provider to maintain the registered office and provide a separate IRD postal address.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) and myIR online portal (https://www.ird.govt.nz)

E-government filing portal

For a foreign non-resident entity, the procedure to obtain a New Zealand IRD number (Tax Identification Number) is as follows: (1) Complete the application form IR596 (Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application), available on the IRD website. (2) Provide supporting documentation including the certificate of incorporation, proof of identity for directors and shareholders, and a statement of the entity's business activities in New Zealand. (3) Submit the application via post or through a registered tax agent. (4) Upon approval, the IRD issues an IRD number. If the entity is required to register for GST (Goods and Services Tax) due to taxable activity in New Zealand, it must separately apply for GST registration via myIR, providing the IRD number and details of expected turnover. The GST registration is mandatory if the entity's taxable supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to exports of goods and services under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. For goods, zero-rating applies if the goods are exported from New Zealand. For services, zero-rating applies if the recipient is a non-resident and the services are supplied to a person who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply, and the services are not directly connected with land or goods in New Zealand. For digital services (including SaaS), the place of supply rules determine zero-rating; if the recipient is a non-resident business (B2B) or a non-resident consumer (B2C) outside New Zealand, the supply is zero-rated. The supplier must hold sufficient evidence to prove the recipient's non-resident status and that the services are performed outside New Zealand.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry)
  • Bill of Lading
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC)

No real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory e-invoicing regime exists in New Zealand. GST invoices must contain specific details as per the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, but there is no API requirement or anti-fraud digital stamping. Invoices can be issued electronically or on paper, and must be retained for seven years.

Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, penalties for incorrect zero-rating include shortfall penalties of up to 150% of the tax shortfall, plus interest on unpaid tax. For fraudulent or willful misrepresentation, criminal penalties may apply, including fines and imprisonment. The IRD may retrospectively claw back zero-rated supplies if the required documentation is not maintained or if the export conditions are not met within the statutory timeframe.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised foreign exchange regime with no exchange controls, no central bank approval requirements, and no surrender obligations for export proceeds. The New Zealand dollar is freely convertible on both the current and capital accounts. Resident and non-resident entities may open foreign currency accounts, receive overseas capital injections, and repatriate profits without FX authorisation. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not vet individual FX transactions except for prudential supervision and overall financial stability. Payments are subject only to anti-money laundering and counter-financing of terrorism (AML/CFT) screening under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Profit remittance can be made through standard bank transfers, with the sole practical hurdle being standard KYC verification and source-of-funds proof. There are no surrender requirements or mandatory conversion ratios; export earnings may be retained in any convertible currency. The absence of surrender rules and the free flow of capital makes New Zealand a highly attractive treasury jurisdiction for a micro-SaaS entity seeking cross-border liquidity and low-friction global payment routing.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratios. Exporters may retain 100% of foreign currency receipts in domestic or offshore accounts, or convert to NZD at any time at market rates. Only standard reporting obligations under AML/CFT apply for wire transfers.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

New Zealand does not issue e-money institution (EMI) licences akin to the EU EMI directive. Offshore digital EMIs cannot service NZ entities as standalone banks because they are not registered banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and cannot accept deposits. Some fintech payment firms operate under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and the AML/CFT Act, but must partner with a local bank for fiat settlement. For a low-risk micro-SaaS, a global fintech such as Wise or Stripe can be used as a payment rail, but the primary corporate treasury account should be held at a Tier-1 domestic bank to ensure reliable clearing, FX execution, and capital verification.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols require the originator to issue an SWIFT MT103 or equivalent with a detailed payment narrative stating 'Equity Injection for NZ Ltd' and the applicable Foreign Exchange purpose code (typically OECD code 4050 for direct investment equity capital). The funds must be cleared through the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's exchange settlement account system and are subject to standard AML/CFT screening. Capital verification is executed by a New Zealand Chartered Accountant who cross-references the bank receipt against the Companies Office share issue return, ensuring no foreign currency conversion is forced and that the NZD or foreign currency remains on the entity's balance sheet as paid-in capital.Trade settlements and operational vendor payments use SWIFT MT103 for direct wire transfers, commercial letters of credit for larger overseas procurement, and documentary collections for low-value recurring purchases. For subscription SaaS disbursements, the entity can also use Paddle or Lemon Squeezy as merchant-records-of-record, which route gross settlement via their own licensed payment partners, while domestic NZD invoices are settled via the local Payments NZ clearing rails (BECS or the upcoming ISO 20022 NPP grade rails). Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: Under the Companies Act 1993 and Financial Reporting Act 2013, a New Zealand limited liability company is exempt from a mandatory audit only if all 'small company' thresholds are satisfied. A company is 'large' and thereby subject to compulsory statutory audit when it breaches at least two of the following three objective thresholds for each of the last two consecutive accounting periods: (1) total assets exceed NZD 5,000,000 (approximately USD 3,050,000), (2) total turnover exceeds NZD 10,000,000 (approximately USD 6,100,000), (3) more than 50 full-time equivalent employees. In addition, an audit is statutorily required even for a small company if members holding at least 5% of the voting shares validly request an audit, or if the company is an overseas-owned subsidiary or a financial reporting entity prescribed by the Financial Markets Authority. The target micro-SaaS entity, with consumer subscription revenues typical below these thresholds, is expected to remain within the small-company exemption.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 of aggregate cross-border related-party transactions in an income year triggers the expectation of contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation under Inland Revenue's Transfer Pricing Guidelines. MNE groups with consolidated revenue above approximately NZD 1.3 billion (the EUR 750 million OECD threshold) are also expected to maintain Master File and Country-by-Country reporting.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no separate Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to offshore financial centres, but substance is enforced through the general anti-avoidance rule (Income Tax Act 2007 section BG 1), the business continuity test for loss carryforward, thin capitalisation limits, and Inland Revenue's economic substance review for cross-border arrangements. A consumer micro-SaaS must evidence real operational decision-making, local personnel or contractors, actual management of servers and customer data, and board meetings held in New Zealand. Absence of substance will trigger recharacterisation, disallowance of deductions, and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under Income Tax Act 2007 sections GC 6-GC 15; disallowance of deductions; shortfall penalty of 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, or 150% for evasion; plus daily use-of-money interest on the adjusted tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly execute or approve artificial transfer pricing arrangements can be personally assessed for the company's tax shortfall under anti-avoidance principles, exposed to civil penalties, disqualified under the Companies Act 1993, and subject to travel restrictions via departure prohibition orders where tax debt is outstanding.
Tax Evasion and False or Misleading Returns

Statutory sanction: Criminal liability under Tax Administration Act 1994 sections 143A and 143B: fines up to NZD 50,000 for a first offence and NZD 100,000 for a subsequent offence, and imprisonment for up to 5 years; civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax; the normal 4-year statutory time bar is extended indefinitely for evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and shadow directors who aid, abet, or consent to evasion face joint and several liability for penalties, personal prosecution, restraint orders, and departure prohibition orders issued by Inland Revenue; civil restitution and confiscation may also be ordered.
Shell Company Abuse and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Application of the general anti-avoidance rule (section BG 1) to void the arrangement; reconstruction of taxable income; denial of loss carryforwards under the business continuity test; penalty of up to 100% for an abusive tax position; extended reassessment period of 7 years where avoidance exists.

Director personal liability: Directors breach their statutory duties under Companies Act 1993 sections 131-138, including the duty to avoid reckless trading under section 135; civil liability to the company and creditors, disqualification, personal compensation orders, and possible criminal prosecution for fraudulent use of the corporate form.
AML/CFT Failure and Money Laundering

Statutory sanction: Civil penalties under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 of up to NZD 1,000,000 for an individual and NZD 2,000,000 for a body corporate per contravention; money laundering offences under Crimes Act 1961 section 243 carry imprisonment of up to 7 years for individuals, with confiscation of proceeds under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in laundering proceeds or who fail to establish adequate AML/CFT compliance systems face personal criminal liability, imprisonment, fines, and disqualification; travel bans may also be imposed pending criminal investigation or asset recovery proceedings.
