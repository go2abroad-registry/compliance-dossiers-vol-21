Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office with a genuine physical street address in New Zealand and a staffed service-of-process facility is generally acceptable for company registration and for a low-risk MarTech/SaaS entity. A commercial post-office mailbox alone is not acceptable as a registered office because the Companies Act 1993, section 189, requires a physical address at which legal documents may be served and records kept. Inland Revenue and New Zealand tier-one banks will normally reject a bare letterbox for tax correspondence or bank account opening, especially if the company seeks GST registration or a merchant account with Stripe or Paddle. The safest structure for this industry is to use a licensed registered agent with a real New Zealand street address, mail handling, and director forwarding service, and to maintain evidence of commercial substance such as a physical support address, director location, and customer contracts before requesting high-risk banking approval.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand, operating through the myIR secure online portal (https://www.ird.govt.nz/). The New Zealand Companies Office (under the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment) handles company incorporation and issues the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN), which is a prerequisite for most tax registrations.

E-government filing portal

For a New Zealand resident limited company (NZ_LTD), the following exhaustive procedure applies: (1) Incorporate the company with the New Zealand Companies Office (Companies Office / Āria) to obtain a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and Certificate of Incorporation. (2) Using the NZBN and company details, apply for a tax identification number known as the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) number. This can be completed online via myIR or by submitting form IR596 (Registration of a Company) to the IRD. Non-resident directors or shareholders must provide their foreign tax identification numbers, passports or national identity proofs, and overseas addresses as part of the application. (3) Upon successful processing, the IRD issues a unique 8 or 9-digit IRD number, which serves as the company's Tax File Number (TFN) for income tax and GST purposes. (4) While completing the registration, the company must also register for Goods and Services Tax (GST) if its taxable turnover exceeded NZD 60,000 in the preceding 12 months or is expected to exceed NZD 60,000 in the next 12 months. If not immediately required, voluntary GST registration is available. (5) If the company will have employees or pay salaries, it must also register as an employer for PAYE (Pay As You Earn) and, if applicable, for FBT (Fringe Benefit Tax) and ACC (Accident Compensation Corporation) obligations. (6) The entire registration process is consolidated within myIR, and the IRD number is the single identifier used for all tax filings, including GST returns, income tax returns, and employer schedules.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: A New Zealand resident limited company is subject to corporate income tax on its worldwide income at the standard corporate rate of 28%. The default classification under New Zealand domestic law is entity-level taxation with no automatic pass-through treatment. Companies are entitled to imputation credits (dividend imputation) to avoid double taxation of distributed profits.

Available election forms

New Zealand does not offer a US-style check-the-box election or hybrid mismatch election under domestic law. However, an eligible closely-held New Zealand company (no more than 5 shareholders, none of which are companies, and meeting ownership tests) may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status under the Income Tax Act 2007. If elected, the company's income, expenses, and tax credits are transparently attributed to shareholders, subject to eligibility criteria and a binding election filed with the IRD. LTC status is not available to non-resident companies or companies with corporate shareholders. Additionally, certain hybrid mismatch rules apply under the OECD BEPS framework, but these are not 'elections' but rather anti-avoidance provisions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000: A person or entity must register for GST if the total taxable supplies made in New Zealand (or intended to be made) exceeded NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period, or if it is expected that the total value of taxable supplies will exceed NZD 60,000 in the next 12 months. Registration is also mandatory for non-resident persons who provide remote services (e.g., digital SaaS) to New Zealand consumers and exceed this threshold, but for a domestic NZ_LTD, the standard threshold rules apply. Voluntary registration is available even below the threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, the supply of services is zero-rated if the services are supplied to a non-resident person who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, and the services are not physically performed (directly in connection with) land or tangible personal property situated in New Zealand. For a SaaS-based SEO content analytics tool (ISIC 6201), zero-rating applies when the customer is a non-resident business or individual, the software/platform is used remotely, and the customer has no presence or nexus in New Zealand. The supplier must obtain and retain evidence of the customer's non-resident status and offshore consumption. Additionally, exports of goods are zero-rated when physically exported from New Zealand under customs supervision. Zero-rated GST does not entitle the supplier to a refund on input tax unless the input tax relates to taxable activities.

  • Commercial contract or service agreement with the non-resident customer, clearly stating the nature of the SaaS service, payment currency, and overseas jurisdiction.
  • Tax invoice issued under GST Act 1985 showing 0% GST, customer name and address outside New Zealand, and a zero-rating reference (e.g., Section 11(1)(m)).
  • Bank statement or SWIFT/remittance credit advice demonstrating payment from an overseas account (New Zealand banks do not issue FIRC; bank-generated settlement evidence is accepted).
  • IP address logs, server geolocation data, and customer self-certification (for B2B) or proof of customer's offshore address to substantiate non-resident consumption.

New Zealand does not mandate a real-time fiscal clearance system, nor does it require anti-fraud digital stamping or government pre-approval of invoices. Electronic invoicing is permitted and encouraged, and the IRD supports the use of the Peppol network as a voluntary standard. There is no statutory API-based clearance mechanism for GST returns. However, all tax invoices must be retained in either paper or electronic form and contain prescribed information under the GST Act 1985, including: supplier name and GST number, date of issue, description of supply, consideration, and GST amount (or zero-rating statement for exports). For continuous supplies (e.g., SaaS subscriptions), a single tax invoice may cover recurring charges, but reconciliation records must be maintained.

Inland Revenue may subject zero-rated export claims to rigorous audit scrutiny, including verification of the customer's non-resident status, contractual terms, payment provenance, and the actual offshore consumption of the service. If zero-rating is claimed without adequate documentation, or is found to be fraudulent or misrepresented, the following penalties apply under the Tax Administration Act 1994: (a) shortfall penalties up to 20% or 40% for not taking reasonable care or gross carelessness, and 100% or 150% for fraudulent intent; (b) use-of-money interest from the original due date of GST; (c) retrospective GST assessments clawing back the unpaid GST plus interest and penalties, potentially extending to prior returns within the statutory reassessment period (typically 4 years, extended to 10 years in cases of fraud or suppression). In addition, criminal prosecution may be pursued for tax evasion offences, leading to fines up to NZD 50,000 for individuals and imprisonment up to 3 years. Directors and managers of the NZ_LTD may be held personally liable for penalties in cases of knowing participation in GST fraud.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand does not maintain a stringent foreign exchange control regime. The New Zealand dollar is freely floating and fully convertible for both current account and capital account transactions. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand retains statutory powers to issue prudential and currency-related regulations, but it does not impose surrender requirements, prior approvals, or licensing hurdles on ordinary foreign exchange transactions. Inward capital contributions, equity injections, intra-group loans, royalty payments, and profit remittances may be converted into NZ dollars or foreign currency with minimal administrative friction. There is no statutory requirement to convert export proceeds into NZD, and no limitations on repatriating dividends, provided the usual corporate law solvency tests and tax clearance obligations are satisfied. Capital account openness is high, though certain overseas investments in sensitive land or significant business assets require consent under the Overseas Investment Act. For an ISIC 6201 software analytics company with no sensitive-asset exposure, convertibility is effectively unrestricted. Banks apply standard AML/CFT transaction monitoring and may request supporting documents for large cross-border flows, but there is no central bank foreign exchange approval gate.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio exist for export foreign currency receipts. New Zealand resident exporters may retain receivables in foreign currency, hold NZD, or freely transact through any registered New Zealand bank, subject only to standard AML/CFT record-keeping and suspicious transaction reporting obligations.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Cross-border digital EMI platforms are operationally viable in New Zealand for SaaS merchants but only under clear legal constraints. An EMI must either hold a New Zealand money remittance registration or operate as an appointed agent of a registered provider under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, with supervision by the Department of Internal Affairs. Such platforms are not licensed as registered banks and cannot provide insured deposit facilities; they may offer payment collection, FX conversion, and payout rails. A fintech EMI cannot replace the statutory corporate bank account required for audit trails, capital verification, and trade finance documentation. It is best positioned as a secondary ancillary rail for subscription collections and contractor payouts, while the primary treasury architecture remains anchored at a Tier 1 domestic bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections should be routed through the SWIFT network using MT103 messages into the New Zealand company's NZD or foreign-currency account at a Tier 1 bank. The remitter must use a precise payment purpose description such as 'Equity subscription toward ordinary shares under the Companies Act 1993' and include appropriate ISO 20022 purpose code, generally 'PDBA' or the bank-designated code for foreign direct investment. No New Zealand Exchange Control approval is required, but the bank may request a board resolution, subscription agreement, and share allotment filing with the Companies Office. A New Zealand Chartered Accountant can issue a capital verification certificate, confirming the receipt and allocation of funds, which is particularly useful for future dividend distributions and capital repatriation documentation.Outbound disbursements for trade settlements and SaaS vendor payments should use SWIFT MT103 for larger international wire transfers, with beneficiary bank and intermediary bank codes clearly specified. Commercial Letters of Credit issued by a Tier 1 New Zealand bank are appropriate for high-value procurement of hardware, cloud infrastructure, and licensed software development services requiring documentary risk mitigation. Documentary collections are viable for medium-value transactions where the New Zealand company and offshore supplier maintain established trade history. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Cross-border digital EMI platforms are operationally viable in New Zealand for SaaS merchants but only under clear legal constraints. An EMI must either hold a New Zealand money remittance registration or operate as an appointed agent of a registered provider under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, with supervision by the Department of Internal Affairs. Such platforms are not licensed as registered banks and cannot provide insured deposit facilities; they may offer payment collection, FX conversion, and payout rails. A fintech EMI cannot replace the statutory corporate bank account required for audit trails, capital verification, and trade finance documentation. It is best positioned as a secondary ancillary rail for subscription collections and contractor payouts, while the primary treasury architecture remains anchored at a Tier 1 domestic bank.

28%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The statutory Non-resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) rate on dividends is 30%. This rate is reduced to 15% for a fully imputed dividend paid by a New Zealand resident company. Under certain DTAs, the rate may be further reduced – for instance, Australia grants a 5% rate on dividends where the beneficial owner holds at least 80% of the voting power. The US DTA generally allows 15% (5% for 10%-or-more shareholdings).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: A New Zealand company with cross-border transactions with associated non-resident enterprises must prepare contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (Local File) when the aggregate gross value of all such transactions exceeds NZ$10 million in an income year. A Master File is required for multinational groups where the ultimate parent's annual consolidated group revenue exceeds NZ$1.2 billion. Even below these thresholds, taxpayers are expected to retain reasonable supporting documentation to demonstrate an arm's length outcome.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no standalone 'Economic Substance Regulations' (ESR) as applied in certain offshore financial centres. However, the Inland Revenue Act 2007, specifically s GA 1 (general anti-avoidance rule), the business continuity test for losses, and the underlying purpose of the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines and DTAs require that a NZ company demonstrate genuine commercial substance. Maintaining an office, having local staff, holding board meetings in NZ, executing key contracts locally, and exercising real decision-making functions are essential. A shell company or 'letterbox' arrangement without substance will be disregarded for tax purposes, its transactions re-characterised, and penalties imposed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraud

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under s 143A of the Tax Administration Act 1994, carrying a maximum fine of NZ$50,000 per offence on the company. The company may be liable for a shortfall penalty of 150% of the avoided tax, plus late payment penalties and interest. Under s 144, aiding and abetting tax evasion attracts the same penalties. Criminal conviction may result in confiscation of proceeds under the Proceeds of Crime Act 1994.

Director personal liability: Directors who are knowingly concerned in the evasion can be held personally liable for the company's tax shortfall under s 149 of the Tax Administration Act. They may face a separate criminal fine of up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment of up to 5 years under the Crimes Act 1961. Travel bans and asset freezing orders can be imposed under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner of Inland Revenue may invoke the general anti-avoidance rule (s BG 1, s GA 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007) to disregard the company as a taxpayer or reallocate income to the true source. The company will lose the benefit of any tax deductions, face reassessment of income at 28% with interest, and be liable for shortfall penalties of 40% (gross negligence) or 100% (abusive tax position) of the resulting tax shortfall. The company's loss carry-forward may be denied.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorised or participated in the formation and operation of such a shell arrangement may be personally liable for the unpaid corporate tax under s 149 of the Tax Administration Act. Additionally, they may be charged with tax evasion under s 143A causing a travel ban, and may be barred from managing a company for up to 5 years under the Companies Act 1993.
Transfer Pricing Adjustment

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may make a transfer pricing adjustment under Part GC (s GC 6 and GC 7) of the Income Tax Act 2007, increasing the company's taxable income by the offending margin, plus interest. Shortfall penalties of up to 100% of the additional tax can be applied, and up to 150% in the case of intentional disregard of the rules. The company's ability to claim a penalty reduction is lost if contemporaneous documentation is absent.

Director personal liability: Directors responsible for setting transfer prices without commercial justification and without attempting to comply with the arm's length principle may be held personally liable for the associated shortfall penalties under s 141CB of the Tax Administration Act. In egregious cases, directors may be prosecuted for tax evasion, leading to imprisonment and travel restrictions.
AML/CFT Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the AML/CFT Act, a company that is a reporting entity can be fined up to NZ$100,000 for non-compliance, and directors may be fined up to NZ$50,000. In serious cases, civil penalties may reach NZ$200,000 per contravention. The AML/CFT supervisor (the Department of Internal Affairs or the Financial Markets Authority) may also issue compliance orders, suspend or cancel registration, and the company may be added to the public warning list.

Director personal liability: Directors who were knowingly involved in the AML/CFT breach can be personally fined up to NZ$50,000 under s 110 of the AML/CFT Act. For deliberate money laundering, directors face prosecution under the Crimes Act 1961 with up to 7 years' imprisonment, and under s 9E of the AML/CFT Act may receive up to 2 years' imprisonment for obstruction. Travel bans may be imposed as a condition of bail or sentence.
Unacceptable Tax Position

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue will reassess the tax shortfall and impose shortfall penalties: 20% for an 'unacceptable tax position' (if the tax shortfall exceeds the threshold of NZ$10,000 or 1% of gross income, whichever is greater), 40% for gross carelessness, and 100% for an abusive tax position. A further 20% penalty may apply if the taxpayer has a history of the same issue. Late payment interest accrues from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed or directly participated in the preparation or lodgement of the return without reasonable care can be personally liable for the shortfall penalties under s 141CB. If the position is fraudulent, personal criminal liability attaches under s 143A with imprisonment possible.
