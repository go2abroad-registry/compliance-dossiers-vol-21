Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

New Zealand Companies Office, a business unit of the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE), operating the New Zealand Companies Register.

Governing statute

Companies Act 1993 (1993 No 105), sections 12 to 16 (incorporation by registration), sections 21 to 24 (company name), section 26 (constitution), sections 37 to 48 (allotment and issue of shares), section 77 (registered office), section 152 (appointment of directors), section 189 (financial records), and section 209 (annual return); Trusts Act 2019 (duties and powers of the corporate trustee); Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, Part 9 Subpart 3 (employee share scheme offer exemptions); Financial Service Providers (Registration and Disputes Resolution) Act 2008 (if the trustee provides regulated trust services for reward); Income Tax Act 2007, sections CE 2 and HC 15 (employee share scheme trust income and rates).

Formation / constitutional filings

Form 1 - Application for Registration of a Company, followed by the issuance of Form 2 - Certificate of Registration; supporting instruments include the Company Constitution (if adopted), Consent to Act as Director, Shareholder Consent, Notice of Registered Office and Address for Service, and, where applicable, FSP-01 (Financial Service Providers Register application), IR 596 (IRD number application) and GST 101 (GST registration).

Incorporation processing time

⚡ 12 business days (SOP sum)

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

New Zealand imposes no statutory minimum paid-up capital, no authorised capital threshold, and no escrow account requirement for a company limited by shares (Companies Act 1993, sections 37 and 48). The founding shareholder may subscribe for shares at any agreed par value, commonly NZD 1.00 per share, payable in cash or, for an ESOP trust, satisfied by way of a shareholder contribution or loan. The corporate trustee is capitalised with one fully paid ordinary share; all trust assets contributed by the settlor employer are segregated in a separate trust bank account and do not form part of the company's share capital.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification: an annual Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) and Certificate of Compliance issued under sections 108 and 109 of the Building Act 2004 for the registered office floor plate, with fire evacuation procedures verified under the Fire and Emergency New Zealand Act 2017. Structural clearance: structural load verification that the record storage area sustains not less than 3.5 kPa imposed loading under AS/NZS 1170.1:2002, and a Certificate of Acceptance from the Territorial Authority where any fit-out alterations were made. Occupational clearance: workplace health and safety clearance from WorkSafe New Zealand under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015, covering workstation ergonomics, safe electrical installations, emergency lighting, and an emergency response plan for the ESOP administration office.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department of New Zealand, Wellington, New Zealand; myIR secure online services portal accessible at ird.govt.nz.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the New Zealand limited company with the New Zealand Companies Office and obtain the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN). Step 2: Apply to Inland Revenue for a company IRD number using form IR596 (new company application) or form IR742 (non-resident company application) within 30 days of incorporation; the IRD number serves as the single statutory tax identification number for income tax, GST, PAYE, and FBT. Step 3: Register for GST under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 through myIR if annual taxable supply turnover exceeds or is expected to exceed NZD 60,000. Step 4: Appoint at least one director resident in New Zealand, maintain a registered office, and provide ultimate beneficial ownership information for any foreign corporate shareholders. Step 5: Complete registration elections for employer obligations (PAYE, ESCT, FBT) if the trust will operate an employee share scheme in respect of New Zealand employees. Non-resident companies without a fixed establishment in New Zealand may still require an IRD number for withholding tax and GST compliance purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand does not maintain a check-the-box classification regime. Available statutory elections include: (a) a Look-Through Company election under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007 allowing eligible closely held companies to flow income and losses through to shareholders for income tax purposes; (b) wholly owned group loss offset and consolidated group elections under subpart IG of the Income Tax Act 2007; (c) hybrid mismatch elections and ordering rules under sections FH 1 to FH 14 of the Income Tax Act 2007, which apply to hybrid financial instruments and hybrid entity arrangements to neutralise base erosion and profit shifting outcomes; and (d) GST group registration election under section 55 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 permitting members of a 100 percent owned corporate group to be treated as a single taxable person. An ESOP trust acting as a financial arrangement holder may also elect to accrue interest income on a yield-to-maturity basis under the financial arrangements rules (subpart EW).

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 gross taxable supplies in any rolling 12-month period (compulsory registration); voluntary registration is permitted below the threshold where the supplier carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand, subject to Inland Revenue approval. Financial services supplied by an ESOP trust may be exempt supplies for GST purposes, which affects the threshold calculation and input tax recovery position.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating of exported goods requires physical exportation from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply, with the supplier retaining documentary evidence of export under sections 11(2), 11(3), and 11(5) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Services may be zero-rated under section 11A(1)(a) where the supply is made to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, provided the services are not supplied in connection with land in New Zealand or moveable personal property in New Zealand. For an ESOP administration trust, administrative, trustee, and asset-servicing fees supplied to a non-resident grantor, non-resident employee benefit trust, or offshore plan sponsor may qualify for zero-rating where the non-resident status and offshore presence of the recipient is evidenced by proper documentary proof in the supplier's records, including a declaration of non-residence, offshore business address, and confirmation that the services are performed while the recipient is outside New Zealand. The zero-rating of exported goods or services is denied where the supplier fails to hold the prescribed evidence at the time of supply or where the goods are diverted for local use in New Zealand.

  • Customs Export Declaration lodged through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window (TSW)
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent transport document (sea waybill, air waybill, or cargo manifest) evidencing physical exportation from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign exchange settlement record evidencing receipt of payment in foreign currency together with the underlying commercial invoice

No real-time fiscal clearance, mandatory digital stamping, or API-based pre-clearance regime operates in New Zealand. GST returns must be filed electronically through the myIR portal using GST101 or GST103 periodic returns. Electronic invoicing is supported voluntarily through the New Zealand Peppol e-invoicing network via a certified access point; the New Zealand government promotes business-to-government e-invoicing through Peppol, but Inland Revenue does not mandate continuous transaction controls, real-time reporting, or fiscal acceptance of invoices. GST taxable invoices must nonetheless comply with content requirements under section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, and records must be retained for seven years.

Inland Revenue may reassess zero-rated exports and claw back input tax credits, GST refunds, and zero-rating benefits where the statutory export conditions are not met, goods are not exported within 28 days, documentation is fabricated or incomplete, or the transaction is found to have a dominant purpose of tax avoidance. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply on the resulting tax shortfall: 20 percent for an unacceptable tax position taken without reasonable care (section 141B), 50 percent for gross carelessness (section 141C), 100 percent for an abusive tax position (section 141D(1)), and 150 percent for evasion or a dominant purpose of tax avoidance (section 141D(3)). Use-of-money interest accrues on unpaid tax and on clawed-back refunds, and late payment penalties apply on any amount remaining unpaid after the due date. Criminal prosecution is available under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for knowingly providing false or misleading GST returns or for tax evasion, with penalties including imprisonment of up to five years and fines of up to NZD 50,000, in addition to full recovery of the tax shortfall. The ESOP trust administrator must retain the complete export and zero-rating documentary bundle for at least seven years to sustain the zero-rating position on audit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised current and capital account consistent with its IMF Article VIII obligations; the New Zealand dollar is a free-floating and freely convertible currency. There are no exchange-control approvals, no surrender requirements, no capital-account licensing, and no profit-remittance gates for dividends, trust distributions, share redemptions, interest payments, or ESOP payouts. The only circumscribed area is the Overseas Investment Act 2005, which screens foreign investment in certain 'sensitive' assets (farmland, coastal land, fishing quota, and significant business assets above statutory thresholds) and is unlikely to apply to an ordinary ESOP trust that receives cash contributions and issues securities. Dividend and interest remittances to non-residents are subject solely to New Zealand tax rules: Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) or, in the case of interest paid to qualifying offshore lenders, the Approved Issuer Levy (AIL) under the Income Tax Act 2007. Beyond tax, the residual obligations are statistical: reporting entities (banks) must capture cross-border payment narratives for the Reserve Bank's balance-of-payments (BPM6) compilation, but no regulatory permission is required from the payer or beneficiary for any capital movement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no export-proceeds surrender rules, no mandatory conversion ratios, and no statutory repatriation timeline in New Zealand. An exporter, trustee, or service company may lawfully retain one hundred percent of foreign-currency receipts in a foreign-currency account held at any registered New Zealand bank. The only ongoing obligations are: (i) the bank's reporting of the foreign-currency credit to the Reserve Bank for balance-of-payments statistical purposes; (ii) the entity's ordinary New Zealand tax declarations for foreign-source income; and (iii) the AML/CFT requirement to report 'unusual transactions' where a receipt pattern is inconsistent with the customer's documented business profile. No central-bank permission or administrative filing precedes the retention or conversion of export or service-sale proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), operating as the apex monetary, prudential, and foreign-exchange policy authority. Its powers over banking and settlement systems derive from the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 (including the 2021 amendments), the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act), the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 (FMCA), and the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2019. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) separately regulates financial-market conduct and employee share schemes, while the Department of Internal Affairs supervises non-bank reporting entities. No separate Forex Administration or exchange-control authorisation body exists in New Zealand.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM (conditional). New Zealand law does not provide a standalone 'Electronic Money Institution' licence equivalent to the EU's PSD2 regime. Foreign and domestic fintech payment providers, including Airwallex (registrable as a non-bank payment/service provider on the Financial Service Providers Register), Wise NZ (Wise Payments Limited), and various Banking-as-a-Service platforms, may lawfully hold funds, originate and receive SWIFT credits, issue virtual foreign-currency account identifiers, and arrange correspondent settlement through a sponsoring tier-1 bank. However, such providers are NOT members of the domestic settlement system (the Reserve Bank's Exchange Settlement Account System / ESAS and the wholesale clearing run by EMNPay). Consequently, their settlement finality is indirect and dependent on a sponsoring licensed bank. Critical legal constraint for an ESOP trust: employee distributions that function as payroll (Direct Payroll Wire) must settle in New Zealand dollars on New Zealand clearing rails with same-day or next-day value; an offshore digital EMI holding settlement funds in a foreign jurisdiction would undermine the trustee's fiduciary duties under the Trusts Act 2019 and would likely be rejected by auditors and by the bank's customer-onboarding compliance review. An offshore EMI is therefore viable only as a secondary, small-value disbursement channel (e.g., sub-NZD 50,000 per transfer for foreign-resident beneficiary payments), never as the primary operating account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing for sponsor or head-office capital into the ESOP trust structure: (1) The sponsor executes a SWIFT MT103 (single customer credit transfer) using a traceable UETR and a payment narrative in the following recommended format: 'CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION - ESOP TRUST; BENEFICIARY TRUSTEE: [NZ_LTD legal name]; NZ IRD NUMBER: [IRD No.]; CLASSIFICATION: CAPITAL / EQUITY - DIRECT INVESTMENT; TRANCHE REF: [internal reference]'. Banks will typically require the narrative to include a balance-of-payments classification description (e.g., 'equity capital contribution to related enterprise' or 'trust contribution for employee share acquisition') because the receiving bank is obliged to report the flow to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand for BPM6 statistical compilation. (2) The bank screens the credit against sanctions and politically-exposed-person lists, then credits the pre-verified trust operational account; no exchange-control approval is required. (3) Where the inbound leg is in a foreign currency, the bank either converts at the prevailing market spread (typically 8-15 pips for institutional clients) or credits a multi-currency sub-account. (4) For amounts exceeding NZD 2 million, the bank's internal fiduciary-customer escalation committee re-reviews the trust's source-of-funds documentation before release of funds; this is a bank-level risk control and not a central-bank permission. (5) The Overseas Investment Act 2005 consent requirement is relevant only where the ESOP proceeds are applied to acquire 'sensitive' New Zealand assets (farmland, quota, significant business assets); a standard equity/debt contribution into a service-trust bank account triggers no OIA filing.Primary clearing rails for ESOP outbound settlements: (i) Payroll-style employee distributions in New Zealand dollars - use Direct Credit files via the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS, operated by EMNPay) settled in real-time across the Reserve Bank's Exchange Settlement Account System; the trustee uploads an authorised payroll file on D-1 and receives same-day value on the payment date, with PAYE obligations applied to New Zealand-resident employees. (ii) Urgent same-day employee payments or corporate actions - use New Zealand real-time payment services offered by tier-1 banks (RTGS / high-value clearing), enabling immediate finality in central-bank money. (iii) Cross-border settlements to foreign parent companies, brokers, or non-resident beneficiaries - use SWIFT MT103 (or MT103+199) with SWIFT GPI tracking; the message must carry the full beneficiary account details, UETR, and a purpose narrative such as 'ESOP DISTRIBUTION / SHARE REDEMPTION / TRUST DISBURSEMENT'. (iv) Commercial letters of credit (UCP600) are permissible, though generally reserved for contingent share-purchase milestones and escrow layers; ANZ, BNZ, and Westpac are the most active NZ LC issuers, and an LC may be denominated in NZD, USD, EUR, or AUD with typical commission charges of 0.1% - 0.4% per quarter. (v) Escrow and settlement account finality - for high-value share redemptions, the trustee should instruct payments via RTGS rather than BECS, ensuring irrevocable settlement. All outbound disbursements above NZD 50,000 should be tethered to a board-authorised ESOP distribution schedule and dual-signatory approval protocol; banks may decline any outbound payment that conflicts with the account's documented purpose and KYC file.Outright deliverable NZD/USD foreign-exchange forwards: quoted on an OIS-discounted basis, tenors up to 12 months available without regulatory restriction; tenors beyond 12 months permitted subject to bank credit appetite and ISDA/CSA margin arrangements.Cross-currency swaps (CCS): permitted without Reserve Bank approval where the ESOP trust receives foreign-currency funding (e.g., USD from the sponsor) and requires NZD liquidity for employee distributions, share subscriptions, or dividend payments.Vanilla FX options and zero-premium collars (risk reversals): permissible for hedging committed ESOP capital calls, share-repurchase obligations, and the foreign-currency exposure of non-resident employee payouts.Offshore USD/NZD non-deliverable forwards (NDFs): available in the London and Singapore interbank markets for price discovery and unfunded hedge positions; although the onshore NZD is fully deliverable, NDFs remain standard where the counterparty cannot access the onshore forward market or requires a non-funding hedge.FX basis swaps on the NZD/USD cross-currency basis: bank-offered under an ISDA 2002 Master Agreement, CSA with New York law-governed credit support, and the FX Global Code; suitable for aligning the currency of long-dated ESOP trust assets with the sponsor's functional currency.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: MEDIUM (conditional). New Zealand law does not provide a standalone 'Electronic Money Institution' licence equivalent to the EU's PSD2 regime. Foreign and domestic fintech payment providers, including Airwallex (registrable as a non-bank payment/service provider on the Financial Service Providers Register), Wise NZ (Wise Payments Limited), and various Banking-as-a-Service platforms, may lawfully hold funds, originate and receive SWIFT credits, issue virtual foreign-currency account identifiers, and arrange correspondent settlement through a sponsoring tier-1 bank. However, such providers are NOT members of the domestic settlement system (the Reserve Bank's Exchange Settlement Account System / ESAS and the wholesale clearing run by EMNPay). Consequently, their settlement finality is indirect and dependent on a sponsoring licensed bank. Critical legal constraint for an ESOP trust: employee distributions that function as payroll (Direct Payroll Wire) must settle in New Zealand dollars on New Zealand clearing rails with same-day or next-day value; an offshore digital EMI holding settlement funds in a foreign jurisdiction would undermine the trustee's fiduciary duties under the Trusts Act 2019 and would likely be rejected by auditors and by the bank's customer-onboarding compliance review. An offshore EMI is therefore viable only as a secondary, small-value disbursement channel (e.g., sub-NZD 50,000 per transfer for foreign-resident beneficiary payments), never as the primary operating account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under the Income Tax Act 2007 (Subpart GC) and the OECD-aligned Transfer Pricing Guidelines, New Zealand applies the arm's length principle. There is no statutory local currency filing threshold expressed in the Income Tax Act for mandatory transfer pricing documentation; however, Inland Revenue requires transfer pricing documentation to be prepared where cross-border related-party transactions exceed NZ$10 million in aggregate for a group, or where the transaction is significant and the taxpayer is a large enterprise (turnover over NZ$50 million or related-party debt over NZ$10 million). NZ Inland Revenue's transfer pricing compliance framework requires a contemporaneous master file and local file for taxpayers with cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZ$10 million; a Country-by-Country (CbC) report is required for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of NZ$1.15 billion (or EU equivalent threshold) in the preceding year. The local file must be filed within 30 days of a request from Inland Revenue; master file within 60 days of request. New Zealand corporate tax law does not have a filing deadline for TP documentation but requires the documentation to exist at the time of filing the return if the transaction meets the significant threshold.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations (ESR). Instead, the applicable doctrine is the 'anti-avoidance' rules (Part G of the Income Tax Act 2007): general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) in s BG 1 and specific anti-avoidance rules, including the business purpose test and the reconstruction rule. An ESOP trust or holding company with insufficient economic substance, used principally to defeat the application of tax provisions, can be voided by the Commissioner of Inland Revenue under the reconstruction rules (s GC 1), causing the resettled income to be reattributed to the underlying employees or shareholders. Inland Revenue also applies the concept of 'tax avoidance arrangement' under s BG 1 if a trustee's activities lack commercial purpose, and the corporate veil can be pierced for tax liabilities if the company or trust is a mere sham. For transfer pricing, New Zealand requires that the substance of controlled transactions be aligned with actual economic activity; the OECD transfer pricing guidelines are incorporated into New Zealand law via s GC 6 of the Income Tax Act 2007. An SPY/ESOP trust must comply with Court-approved trust deed formalities and the Trusts Act 2019; a trust without sufficient substance—such as no independent trustee, no accounts, no bank account, and slavish adherence to the terms of an offshore protector—risks being treated as a sham or as an agency arrangement.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Deliberate Non-Filing

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, evading tax is a criminal offence: a person liable for a shortfall penalty of 150% of the unpaid tax for evasion or an abusive tax position, in addition to 100% for an unacceptable tax position; criminal conviction carries a maximum fine of NZ$50,000 for an individual and NZ$250,000 for a company; imprisonment for up to 5 years for individuals (s 143A, 143B of Tax Administration Act 1994). Inland Revenue may also issue a default assessment and impose all late payment penalties and UIM.

Director personal liability: Directors and trustees may be personally liable for corporate tax where tax has been deducted from employees (PAYE) or withheld from non-residents and knowingly not accounted for to Inland Revenue; directors can be personally liable under s 108 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for PAYE, and under the Companies Act 1993 for reckless trading. The Commissioner may make directors personally liable for withholding tax and penalties if they participated in the breach; a director who is resident in New Zealand can face a restrictions order under the Tax Administration Act 1994 (a 'taxpayer home management' prohibition) and the court may impose a travel ban on a director with significant outstanding tax who attempts to leave New Zealand without an arrangement (Inland Revenue can obtain a 'departure prohibition order' under s 157A-160; in serious cases the Crown can seek extradition).
Abusive Tax Position / GAAR

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty of 100% of the resulting tax for an abusive tax position (s 141D of the Tax Administration Act 1994) and the Commissioner may reconstruct the arrangement pursuant to s GC 1(2), denying all tax benefits and assessing the arrangement as if it had been reconstructed in an arm's length form; interest and late payment penalties accrue.

Director personal liability: A director can be jointly and severally liable for the company's tax obligations if the company is a mere conduit and the director signed a declaration that the return was correct; where the company is thinly capitalised and acting as a sham, New Zealand Courts have applied quasi-contractual principles to require the director to disgorge the tax benefit; there is no personal liability shield for dishonesty under s 150 of the Companies Act 1993.
AML / Terrorism Financing / Money Laundering (Shell Company Abuse)

Statutory sanction: Under the AML/CFT Act 2009, the penalty for a reporting entity's breach is up to NZ$2 million for a body corporate and NZ$200,000 for an individual for each non-compliance; in serious cases the High Court can award pecuniary penalties up to NZ$2.5 million. Non-reporting and failing to maintain a beneficial ownership register under the Companies Act 1993 (no separate UBO register but the Companies Office requires resident directors and share registry) may lead to a strike-off and disqualification. Knowingly providing a false statement to the Companies Office is an offence with a maximum fine of NZ$10,000 or imprisonment up to 2 years (s 377). For serious fraud, the Crimes Act 1961 imposes imprisonment up to 7 years for false accounting and up to 10 years for obtaining by deception.

Director personal liability: Directors and trustees are personally liable for the AML/CFT penalties if they are senior managers and failed to comply; a Court can impose a management banning order and a pecuniary penalty against a director; of note, the absence of a New Zealand-resident director is a strict violation under s 14 of the Companies Act 1993 (a company must have at least one director resident in New Zealand or an Australian resident who is director of an Australian company); defective director appointment can cause the company to lose the ability to enforce contracts and leave director liability for the acts of the company.
Withholding Tax / PAYE Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The failure to deduct is an absolute liability offence for the payer/trustee; the tax becomes immediately payable by the company/trustee (s RA 15), plus late payment penalties. Shortfall penalties for a lack of reasonable care are 20% of the tax; for taking an unacceptable tax position, 20%; if the position is not 'more likely than not' the penalty is 20%; if deliberately considered and abandoned, 20%. Criminal penalty for knowing failure to make tax deduction under s 143A: NZ$50,000 (individual) / NZ$250,000 (company) plus imprisonment up to 5 years. The Commissioner can also impose a 'penalty for delaying payment' at 4% plus monthly 1%.

Director personal liability: Under s 108 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, directors of a company and the trustee of a trust can be personally liable for PAYE, RWT, NRWT, and other source deductions which the company/trust has deducted but not remitted if they knew or ought to have known that the tax was not remitted; the personal liability can equal the unremitted amount and any penalties and interest; the Commissioner may pursue recovery directly against personal bank accounts without first recovering from the company; directors of an insolvent company cannot be discharged from this liability through liquidation.
Transfer Pricing Offences / Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner can adjust the consideration to reflect the arm's length amount under s GC 6-8 of the Income Tax Act 2007; in addition, a transfer pricing shortfall penalty may be imposed at 20% of the adjustment if the taxpayer had a 'miscalculation but not a reasonable care' or at 40% if the documentation was not maintained contemporaneously and the transfer pricing arrangement lacked reasonable basis. There is no specific statutory economic substance penalty, but the same GAAR penalty applies; multinationals with a NZ group revenue over NZ$1.15 billion must file a CbC report; failing to file CbC when required can result in a penalty of NZ$100,000 for each year under the Penalties for CbC non-filing regime.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly make false statements in transfer pricing documentation may be prosecuted for fraud under s 143C of the Tax Administration Act 1994 (maximum NZ$5,000); directors' personal liability can be triggered if they were party to the agreement and signed the return (s 46), and the corporate veil can be pierced under s 24G(3)(c) or through the sham doctrine; foreign directors who reside outside New Zealand can still be extradited for tax evasion or fraud.
Insolvent Trading and Duty-of-Directors Violations

Statutory sanction: Under s 135 of the Companies Act 1993, a director is liable to compensate the company for losses resulting from reckless trading, and under s 136, if the company incurs obligations that the director does not honestly believe are able to be performed. The Court may order the director to contribute to the assets of the company, up to any amount; liquidation proceedings can be initiated by Inland Revenue immediately on unpaid tax, and the company may be removed from the register. For trusts, the trustee in insolvency can be personally surcharged if it distributed assets while the trust was insolvent (Trusts Act 2019 duties).

Director personal liability: Directors have personal unlimited liability for the company's loss caused by insolvent trading; this obligation is statutory and cannot be waived in the constitution; the Society of Accountants and the Registrar of Companies may issue a banning order disqualifying the director for up to 10 years; a director with unpaid tax can also be prohibited from holding office in any company, be subject to a travel ban, and face sequestration of personal assets to satisfy the liability.
