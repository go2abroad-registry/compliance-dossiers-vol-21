Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A non-resident NVOCC must maintain a physical registered office in New Zealand capable of receiving service of process and official correspondence. A virtual office or commercial mailbox that only forwards mail is generally rejected by the Inland Revenue Department and licensed New Zealand banks as a principal place of business or records-premises address. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand; electronic service delivery via myIR Secure Online Services located at https://www.ird.govt.nz/myIR.

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident limited company must apply to Inland Revenue for a New Zealand IRD number before registering for GST. The non-resident company application requires completion of Inland Revenue form IR596 (Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application), or the equivalent non-resident company registration function available inside myIR, accompanied by certified copies of the company's certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents (memorandum and articles of association or equivalent), evidence of registered office, taxation residence certificates, full details of directors and ultimate beneficial shareholders, and, where required by the Companies Act 1993, appointment of a New Zealand resident agent for service. A company incorporated in New Zealand under the Companies Act 1993 will ordinarily have its IRD number issued automatically by Inland Revenue using data transmitted from the New Zealand Companies Office. To register for GST, the entity must file a GST registration application (the Inland Revenue GST registration form or the myIR GST registration online task), stating the principal business activity as ocean freight forwarding/NVOCC brokerage, expected annual taxable supplies, bank account details, and indicating that a substantial proportion of supplies will be zero-rated exports. If the non-resident entity's only New Zealand supplies are zero-rated, the Commissioner may nevertheless register it voluntarily to permit GST refund claims, and may require GST security where the Commissioner considers it necessary for the protection of the revenue.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (a New Zealand-resident limited company is taxed at the entity level on worldwide income at the 28% corporate rate; a non-resident company is taxed only on New Zealand-sourced income in accordance with the territorial scope rules of the Income Tax Act 2007).

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a general check-the-box regime. The principal transparency election is the Look-Through Company (LTC) election for qualifying close-held companies under the Look-Through Company rules in the Income Tax Act 2007, which permits flow-through tax treatment for up to five individual shareholders; no LTC election is available to an NVOCC entity with corporate shareholders unless it satisfies the qualifying ownership tests. Hybrid and branch mismatch integrity rules in the Income Tax Act 2007 neutralise mismatches and are mandatory; no elective hybrid mismatch elections are offered to taxpayers.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 - GST registration is compulsory if the entity's total taxable supplies made in New Zealand in any 12-month period exceed or are expected to exceed NZD 60,000, and non-resident suppliers supplying remote services to New Zealand consumers are subject to the same NZD 60,000 threshold. For a pure NVOCC exporting freight forwarder, zero-rated supplies count as taxable supplies when testing the threshold, but registration may become beneficial to claim input tax refunds even where taxable activity is wholly zero-rated.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for an NVOCC/ocean freight forwarding entity is available under sections 11 and 11A of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Qualifying supplies include (i) goods physically exported from New Zealand by the supplier, its agent, or on its behalf, where the goods are removed from New Zealand within the statutory period (normally 28 days from the time of supply, or in accordance with Customs-controlled area procedures under the Customs and Excise Act 2018); (ii) services supplied to a non-resident customer who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, comprising NVOCC consolidation, ocean freight brokerage, international sea carriage, container handling, freight forwarding, and ancillary logistics services, provided the services are not supplied directly in connection with land in New Zealand; (iii) the international transport, or arranging of international transport, of goods between New Zealand and a place outside New Zealand; and (iv) services supplied in relation to moveable personal property situated outside New Zealand at the time the service is performed. The NVOCC must maintain contemporaneous evidence that the customer is a non-resident (non-New Zealand address, foreign tax invoice, certificate of incorporation, or passport), issue a GST invoice separately identifying zero-rated international freight services, secure a New Zealand Customs export declaration, and hold shipping and banking evidence showing the goods were exported and the foreign currency consideration is being collected from outside New Zealand.

  • Customs export declaration (New Zealand Customs export entry/export advice prepared through the Trade Single Window or Customs Connect portal of the New Zealand Customs Service, as required under the Customs and Excise Act 2018).
  • Original negotiable On-Board Marine Bill of Lading or Sea Waybill issued by the ocean carrier, showing the New Zealand port of loading, overseas port of discharge, vessel name and voyage, container numbers, seal numbers, description of goods, and freight terms evidencing cross-border shipment from New Zealand.
  • Detailed commercial invoice and packing list describing goods supplied and stating the true export consideration, together with the NVOCC freight forwarding invoice or broker confirmation showing the zero-rated services rendered.
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC), or in New Zealand banking practice the equivalent inward remittance/foreign exchange repatriation receipt issued by the New Zealand receiving bank, evidencing that the export invoice has been settled by a telegraphic transfer or SWIFT transfer from the foreign buyer.
  • Full record of the NVOCC's booking file, including master/non-vessel-operating common carrier house bill of lading, shipping instructions from the overseas principal, vessel stowage/cargo manifest references, and Customs clearance number linking the export consignment to the GST zero-rating claim.

New Zealand imposes no real-time fiscal clearance mandate, no mandatory API-based e-invoicing clearance, no continuous transaction controls, and no anti-fraud digital stamping obligation for GST. GST invoices must be issued within the time limits and content requirements of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; paper and electronic tax invoices are equally recognised, and Peppol e-invoicing is supported on a voluntary basis through the New Zealand Peppol Authority operated by the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment. GST returns must be filed online through myIR; the default filing frequency is two-monthly, with monthly filing available, and annual filing permitted only for certain small registered persons.

Inland Revenue audits zero-rated export claims using New Zealand Customs Service export clearance data, shipping manifests, bill-of-lading records, myIR GST return data, bank account analysis, and foreign exchange receipts. If goods are not actually exported within the statutory 28-day export period or the export and customs documentation does not reliably establish that the services were supplied to a non-resident or in respect of goods exported from New Zealand, the zero-rating is reversed and output GST at 15% is assessed on the full consideration, with retrospective clawback of any refunds already paid. If export proceeds are not repatriated to New Zealand within 180 days from the date of the export invoice or the date of shipment, the Commissioner may treat the export as not substantiated and apply output GST together with statutory interest and penalties. Legal penalties include shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 (20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion), use-of-money interest under the Tax Administration Act 1994, late payment penalties, and potential prosecution for GST fraud or false customs declarations under the Customs and Excise Act 2018. Financial institutions are also required to identify trade-based money-laundering red flags under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, and a pattern of unsubstantiated zero-rated NVOCC invoices can lead to enhanced due diligence, refusal of refunds, deregistration, and criminal liability for directors.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a free-floating, fully convertible currency regime under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021. There are no restrictions on capital account convertibility, no surrender requirements for export proceeds, and no mandatory conversion of foreign currency receipts into New Zealand dollars. Both residents and non-residents may freely open foreign currency accounts, borrow offshore, and remit dividends or branch profits without exchange control approval. The only statutory obligations arise from anti-money laundering/countering financing of terrorism (AML/CFT) reporting under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 and cross-border cash reporting under the Border Cash Reporting Regulations. Profit remittances are subject to normal corporate tax withholding rules and non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) or approved issuer levy (AIL) of 2% on interest, but no exchange control permit is required. Transactions are cleared via the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS) operated by RBNZ; the New Zealand dollar (NZD) is among the most freely traded OECD currencies. The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and RBNZ jointly supervise derivatives dealers; NDFs are permitted but rarely used domestically due to full onshore deliverable liquidity. In summary, capital controls are effectively absent; the key practical hurdles are documentary verification for bank onboarding and OECD automatic exchange of information (CRS/AEOI) compliance rather than FX convertibility restrictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender rules exist in New Zealand. Exporters may retain 100% of foreign currency export proceeds in any onshore or offshore account, indefinitely, and are free to remit, convert, or use them for trade settlement without timeline constraints or conversion ratios. The only obligation is to record transactions accurately for tax and AML/CFT purposes.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) - Te Pūtea Matua

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI (Electronic Money Institution) penetration is viable for New Zealand entities but severely constrained for primary corporate banking. New Zealand does not issue EMI licenses per se; instead non-bank financial institutions must obtain a Financial Services Provider (FSP) registration and, if offering money transmission, a money services business (MSB) registration under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act. Pure offshore virtual banking platforms such as Wise Platform or Airwallex operate in New Zealand via FSP-registered subsidiaries, but they cannot provide character-based lending, escrow accounts, or documentary letter-of-credit capabilities required for NVOCC operations. The Reserve Bank prudential regulatory perimeter limits deposit-taking to registered banks; an EMI cannot hold client funds on an on-balance-sheet basis. Therefore, for an NVOCC entity handling ocean freight settlements, a full Tier-1 bank account is mandatory for core Treasury operations. EMIs can serve as secondary payment corridors for fast B2B remittances, particularly USD/EUR/CNH payments, but are not permissible for holding proceeds pending capital verification or for issuance of bank guarantees/standby LCs.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For an NZ Ltd entity, inbound equity or shareholder loans from offshore parent companies should be routed via SWIFT MT103/MT202 to a NZ Tier-1 bank account using the standard purpose code - which under NZ banking practice aligns with the OECD Category of Balance of Payments (BoP) codes. For equity injection, use BoP code 'M2 - Equity Capital and Investment Fund Shares' with narrative 'Share capital contribution'. For shareholder loans, use BoP code 'M1 - Direct Investment Loans' and provide a signed loan agreement to avoid debt-equity thin capitalisation challenges under NZ tax rules. The capital verification process requires a New Zealand chartered accountant (NZICA) to issue a certificate confirming the funds have been received in the company's bank account and recorded against allotment of shares in the Companies Office. Funds must not be commingled with trading income; a separate 'Capital Escrow Account' or dedicated non-operational settlement account is mandatory during the verification window. Inward remittances must comply with the AML/CFT Act Section 12(c), requiring the remitter and beneficiary to be fully identified, and for transactions over NZD 10,000, the bank must obtain source-of-funds declaration. Wire originations should exclude any high-risk jurisdiction codes and must clearly state the commercial invoice / share certificate reference to avoid quarantine by RBNZ's cross-border payment screening system.For NVOCC settlements, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 for directbilling to shipping lines, port authorities, and overseas agents. For service a disbursements exceeding USD threshold or for Chinese corridors, Documentary Collection (DP/DA) is suitable when seller is a bank with established relationships; for large-value ocean freight and contract logistics payments, an irrevocable commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) at 60–120 days sight is recommended, with the NZ bank acting as issuing bank or confirming bank depending on counterparty jurisdiction. The bank must apply for a dedicated 'Trade Finance Facility' to accommodate SBLCs or standby LC for carrier contracts. Payment files can be batched via SWIFT FIN or ISO 20022 (MT103/MT202) using the bank's corporate e-banking portal. All outbound disbursements must include a narrative field with the underlying cargo type, container number, carrier name, and voyage reference to expedite AML screening. There is no FX conversion tax; the NZ financial system supports NZD/USD/EUR/CNH settlement via the CLS system. For regular container freight rebates to China, ACH Direct via New Zealand's electronic clearing system (Bulk Electronic Clearing System, BECS) is not international; instead use the USD via correspondent banks, or CNH direct via RMB Offshore Clearing (HSBC/BOCHK).Deliverable FX forward contracts (up to 3-year tenors, benchmarked to NZD OIS and cross-currency swaps) with NZ Tier-1 bank dealing rooms for hedging USD/NZD exposure from marine freight receivables and carrier payables.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are legally permissible but rarely needed due to full NZD convertibility; they may be utilised only when a counterparty is in a restricted currency jurisdiction (e.g., MYR or KRW) and must be booked through the onshore NZ bank via a master ISDA agreement.Cross-currency swaps (CCS) are permitted for tenor-matched FX and interest rate hedging on foreign-currency ship charter or equipment finance liabilities; RBNZ prudential reporting applies for derivatives above NZD 1 million notional.FX options (European style) are permissible for belt-and-braces protection on large single-trip charter payments; premiums and settlement are fully onshore and subject to ISDA 2002 schedule with an approved Credit Support Annex.No mandatory hedge accounting framework; if the entity uses FX forwards, it must elect IAS 39/IFRS 9 hedge accounting rules under NZ International Accounting Standards Basis, documenting hedge effectiveness testing.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Offshore digital EMI (Electronic Money Institution) penetration is viable for New Zealand entities but severely constrained for primary corporate banking. New Zealand does not issue EMI licenses per se; instead non-bank financial institutions must obtain a Financial Services Provider (FSP) registration and, if offering money transmission, a money services business (MSB) registration under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act. Pure offshore virtual banking platforms such as Wise Platform or Airwallex operate in New Zealand via FSP-registered subsidiaries, but they cannot provide character-based lending, escrow accounts, or documentary letter-of-credit capabilities required for NVOCC operations. The Reserve Bank prudential regulatory perimeter limits deposit-taking to registered banks; an EMI cannot hold client funds on an on-balance-sheet basis. Therefore, for an NVOCC entity handling ocean freight settlements, a full Tier-1 bank account is mandatory for core Treasury operations. EMIs can serve as secondary payment corridors for fast B2B remittances, particularly USD/EUR/CNH payments, but are not permissible for holding proceeds pending capital verification or for issuance of bank guarantees/standby LCs.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10 million aggregate cross-border related-party transactions trigger the mandatory contemporaneous Transfer Pricing documentation (Master File and Local File) in New Zealand. For non-inventory related-party transactions such as freight forwarding service fees and commission sharing, the disclosure threshold on the IR406 International Disclosure regime is NZD 1 million. A Country-by-Country Report is required where the multinational group's consolidated revenue exceeds EUR 750 million (or the NZD equivalent).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a named Economic Substance Regulations statute, but the OECD BEPS Action 5 framework and the New Zealand general anti-avoidance rule (section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007) require that an entity claiming treaty benefits or reduced withholding carries on substantive activity in New Zealand. For an NVOCC, Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) include negotiating ocean container freight rates, procuring and contracting vessel capacity, issuing bills of lading, managing receivables and payables, invoicing customers, arranging customs-related documentation and making strategic risk decisions. A letter-box company with no personnel, office or management decision-making in New Zealand will be disregarded and all profits will be re-attributed to the true business location.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Return

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act 2007, sections BG 1 and GB 1; Tax Administration Act 1994, sections 143A and 144B. Civil shortfall penalty of up to 150% of the evaded tax; criminal conviction carries a fine of up to NZD 50,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Companies Act 1993, section 239 imposes personal liability for fraudulent trading.

Director personal liability: Directors who knew of or participated in the evasion are jointly and severally liable for the unpaid tax, penalties and interest. Courts may pierce the corporate veil under Companies Act 1993, section 239, order asset restitution under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, impose a travel ban under section 36B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, and disqualify a director from managing any New Zealand company for up to 5 years or sentence them to imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act 2007, sections GC 1 to GC 8 empower Inland Revenue to reconstruct arm's-length profits; Tax Administration Act 1994, sections 141 and 141A impose a shortfall penalty of 50% of the resulting underpaid tax, increased to 150% where the misstatement is deliberate. Non-filing of IR406 attracts a separate disclosure penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve a fraudulent transfer pricing policy or sign a false IR4 may be made personally liable for the full tax shortfall. Where the arrangement is found to be a sham, the Companies Act 1993, section 239 allows the court to pierce the corporate veil and hold directors directly liable for company debts; a director may also be disqualified and, in egregious cases, prosecuted for perjury or tax evasion.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse / AML

Statutory sanction: Tax Administration Act 1994, sections BG 1 (general anti-avoidance) and GA 1; Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, sections 42 and 59 impose customer due diligence and reporting obligations. Non-compliance fines may reach NZD 2.5 million for a company and NZD 100,000 or 2 years' imprisonment for an individual director. Unlawful arrangements may be set aside and all treaty benefits denied.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil will be pierced where the entity is a mere facade. Directors who knowingly participate in the shell arrangement are personally liable for unpaid tax and penalties, may face civil asset recovery under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, travel blacklisting under the Tax Administration Act 1994, and disqualification. Criminal liability for money-laundering facilitation under the AML/CFT Act 2009 carries imprisonment of up to 2 years, and tax evasion charges may add imprisonment for up to 5 years.
