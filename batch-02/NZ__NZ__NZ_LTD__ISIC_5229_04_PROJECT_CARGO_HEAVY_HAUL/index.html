Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office with a physical street address and staffed mail handling can satisfy the Companies Act 1993 registered office requirement if it is not a post office box and can accept service of process. However, for a project cargo and out-of-gauge heavy haul logistics company under ISIC 5229, reliance on a pure virtual mailbox is operationally insufficient and is likely to be rejected by the Inland Revenue Department for GST and income tax registration and by New Zealand banks for account opening under AML/CFT because they require a substantive physical operating location. During formation, a serviced executive office acting as the registered office and administrative hub is acceptable, but the company must separately maintain or lease an operational depot, vehicle parking, cargo staging area, and document-controlled loading site before banking approval or customs engagement.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. New Zealand imposes no statutory minimum paid-up share capital and no blocked escrow account for a limited company. The board must allot shares for adequate consideration; shares may not be issued at a discount and the agreed issue price must be fully or partly satisfied by lawful consideration. If contribution is other than cash, the directors must record the asset or benefit received and maintain the share register. No capital proof or escrow deposit is required at registration.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue Te Tari Taake) — the national tax authority of New Zealand; online electronic portal: myIR (myIR.ird.govt.nz).

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must obtain a New Zealand Inland Revenue Department (IRD) number if it: (i) carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand; (ii) derives New Zealand-sourced income; (iii) is required to register for Goods and Services Tax (GST) under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; or (iv) has New Zealand withholding obligations. The procedure for a non-resident entity is: (1) confirm the registration obligation by reference to the Income Tax Act 2007, the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, and the Tax Administration Act 1994; (2) compile the statutory corporate documentation, including certified certificate of incorporation from the foreign jurisdiction, constitutional documents or equivalent, directors and ultimate beneficial ownership details, registered business address, tax residence certificate, and description of intended New Zealand activities; (3) submit the prescribed IRD number application via myIR or the Inland Revenue non-resident application channel; (4) undergo Inland Revenue identity verification and standard anti-money-laundering due diligence; (5) upon approval, receive the IRD number which serves as the sole national tax identifier; and (6) where relevant, simultaneously elect to register for GST and any employer (PAYE) obligations. New Zealand does not impose a minimum share capital requirement for a Limited Company, and non-resident ownership is fully permissible.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

New Zealand does not operate a US-style check-the-box election. Under the Income Tax Act 2007, an eligible close-held company with five or fewer look-through counted owners and no ordinary corporate shareholder may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status, causing tax items to flow through to owners as if the company were a partnership. The LTC election is made on a prescribed Inland Revenue form and takes effect from a specified date. No elective hybrid mismatch regime exists; the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018 applies automatically to neutralise hybrid and branch mismatches, and no additional entity-classification elections are otherwise available.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of total taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) in any consecutive 12-month period; for non-resident suppliers supplying remote services into New Zealand, a NZD 60,000 annual threshold applies for GST registration in respect of supplies to New Zealand consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, a supply of goods qualifies for zero-rating when the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply and retains sufficient documentary evidence of export. Services may be zero-rated under section 11A of the GST Act where the recipient is a non-resident located outside New Zealand at the time of supply and the services are not supplied directly in connection with New Zealand land or goods physically situated in New Zealand. For the out-of-gauge heavy industrial project cargo logistics business, export freight, handling, storage, and transport services supplied to an offshore principal for goods departing New Zealand will generally qualify for zero-rating where the supplier holds export entry evidence and the receivables are paid in convertible foreign currency; domestic-only legs or services connected with goods remaining in New Zealand remain standard-rated at 15%.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry) submitted via the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window or equivalent electronic export entry
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Air Waybill (air) evidencing physical export from New Zealand and delivery to an overseas destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or equivalent bank foreign-exchange credit advice/remittance statement evidencing repatriation of export proceeds in a freely convertible currency

New Zealand does not currently impose mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, compulsory government API e-invoicing validation, or anti-fraud digital stamping. GST-registered taxpayers are required to file GST returns electronically through myIR when registered for GST. New Zealand has adopted the Peppol interoperability framework for voluntary e-invoicing, but no tax-authority pre-clearance or real-time validation is required. Taxpayers must retain complete electronic invoice and record-keeping trails and make them available to Inland Revenue on audit demand.

Inland Revenue may audit all zero-rated export documentation, including customs lodgement records, bills of lading, contracts of carriage, insurance certificates, bank remittance statements, and invoices. Where a zero-rating claim is unsupported, or where goods are not exported within the statutory 28-day export window, the supply is recharacterised as a standard-rated taxable supply and GST is assessed at 15%, with use-of-money interest running from the original due date. Penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 include a 20% shortfall penalty for failure to exercise reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or fraud; material or repeated false export claims may also trigger criminal liability and retrospective reassessment for multiple open tax periods. In cases involving deliberate fraud, directors and controlling shareholders may be held personally liable under the joint and several liability provisions.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates under a free-floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility on both current and capital account transactions. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) does not impose mandatory exchange controls, surrender requirements, or preferential exchange rates for exporters. The current account, comprising trade in goods and services, income and current transfers, is freely convertible, allowing project cargo logistics companies to receive or pay foreign currency without prior central bank approval. The capital account is likewise open: foreign direct investment, equity injections, intercompany loans and profit repatriation are generally permitted, subject to standard due diligence under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 only for significant land and fishing quota acquisitions. Profits may be remitted overseas through any licensed New Zealand bank, and dividend remittances require only normal tax residency and imputation credit documentation. Non-resident shareholders are free to fund the entity with share capital or shareholder loans, although interest remittances may attract non-resident withholding tax. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully deliverable in global markets, and there is no restriction on the use of foreign exchange hedges, forward contracts or swaps, provided they hedge genuine commercial exposure. There is no mandatory obligation to convert any portion of foreign currency receipts into NZD, nor any timeline for repatriation. Companies should nevertheless comply with tax disclosure and transfer pricing rules for related-party cross-border transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement exists in New Zealand. Exporters may retain all foreign currency proceeds in an offshore or onshore account indefinitely, and there is no mandatory conversion timeline or minimum percentage requirement. Foreign currency accounts can be held domestically with licensed commercial banks.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

As a fully convertible and highly regulated jurisdiction, New Zealand permits non-bank financial institutions and fintech EMI providers to operate under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, but they are not licensed as depository institutions and cannot offer classic corporate checking accounts with New Zealand bank account numbers (NZD domestic BANs). Offshore digital EMI platforms domiciled in the EEA or UK face an additional constraint: they cannot provide onshore NZD clearing functionality because New Zealand's local payment system, the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS), is limited to registered banks and RBNZ-approved deposit-takers. Cross-border EMIs may be used only as a supplementary virtual IBAN provider for sub-account collection in NZD, but the primary corporate treasury infrastructure must reside with a locally incorporated registered bank. Overall cross-border EMI viability for this entity is therefore rated Low for core banking and Medium only for ancillary payment collection, provided the EMI is licensed and supervised in its home jurisdiction and does not hold client funds in New Zealand.

The receiving tier-1 bank will require pre-advice of the inward sum for an over-tolerance balance check; the company must present a board resolution approving the capital contribution and, after funds are settled, the bank will issue a Foreign Exchange / Capital Verification Certificate which can be endorsed by a chartered accountant in New Zealand for Companies Office capital return purposes.Operational disbursements to freight forwarders, vessel charterers, heavy-haul subcontractors and inland transport providers will be initiated through SWIFT MT103 for standard vendor payments, while large milestone-based commitments, particularly payments to non-OECD counterparties, may be structured as irrevocable confirmed commercial letters of credit under UCP 600 or documentary collections under URC 522. Payments in non-NZD currencies may be converted through the bank's spot desk, and if the beneficiary requires settlement through a designated nostro account, the bank will advise the MT103 with a local clearing reference. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not prescribe a local-currency turnover threshold for the mandatory preparation of a formal Local/Master File in statute, but Inland Revenue's compliance focus and the OECD TP Guidelines apply. The IRD requires contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation where the taxpayer has cross-border related-party transactions above NZD 10 million in gross revenues; in practice, the IRD expects full documentation for transactions exceeding NZD 10 million per year, and simplified documentation for transactions over NZD 2 million. The Master File is expected for multinational groups with total group revenue of EUR 750 million or more as aligned with Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR); the Local File is expected for New Zealand operations with related-party revenue or expenses above NZD 10 million.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand applies general tax anti-avoidance rules under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, the substantive business purpose test, and the permanent establishment rules in section GB 2. There is no standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime in the New Zealand domestic code, but the Inland Revenue applies the OECD BEPS substance requirements, including the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) in New Zealand's DTAs and the strengthened transfer pricing rules that require the controlled transaction to be economically substantiated with realistic alternatives, accurate delineation, and risk alignment. Shell companies can be disregarded where the actual functions, assets, and risks are not performed, held, or borne in New Zealand, and the IRD can recharacterise arrangements lacking economic substance. For New Zealand treaty claims, the OECD Multilateral Instrument (MLI) principal purpose test will deny treaty benefits if one of the principal purposes is to obtain treaty benefits, absent genuine commercial substance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Offshore Shell/Conduit Company Misuse)

Statutory sanction: Imprisonment up to 5 years and/or a fine up to NZD 50,000 for evasion under the Tax Administration Act 1994, section 143A; civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax; Inland Revenue's ability to reassess within the normal 4-year period (extended to 10 years for evasion); interest and late payment penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors personally function as agents of the company under the Companies Act 1993; directors who are knowingly concerned in evasion can be held individually liable for the company's tax shortfall penalties and fines under the Tax Administration Act 1994 sections 143A and 149A; courts may issue director disqualification orders; travel bans are available under the Insolvency Act 2006 and in proceeds-of-crime restraint proceedings where the evasion is linked to money laundering; imprisonment of the director remains possible where the evasion is prosecuted as an offence.
Trans-Tasman/International Profit Shifting and Transfer Pricing

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under sections GC 13 to GC 17 of the Income Tax Act 2007, recharacterising the controlled transaction to arm's length, resulting in additional taxable income; shortfall penalty of 40% for an unacceptable tax position, increasing to 150% for evasion; administrative penalty for late transfer pricing documentation of NZD 500 per month up to NZD 20,000 (Tax Administration Act 1994, section 111C); anti-avoidance recharacterisation under section GB 2 can deny the deduction entirely.

Director personal liability: Directors approving or participating in a pricing scheme lacking economic substance may be jointly and severally liable for the resulting shortfall penalties where the company cannot pay and the director was knowingly a party to the avoidance arrangement (Tax Administration Act 1994, section 149A(2)); the Companies Act 1993 director duties underpinning reckless trading (section 135) can expose directors to civil liability for losses, without travel ban exposure unless related to criminal insolvency.
Shell Company / Lack of Commercial Substance (Permanent Establishment Risk)

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may treat the company as having a permanent establishment in New Zealand or disregard its corporate personality under the general anti-avoidance rule; foreign income derived through the company may be re-sourced to New Zealand and subjected to 28% corporate tax; the company may be struck off the New Zealand Companies Register and unregistered for GST; an administrative penalty of NZD 1,000 for failing to file annual returns and fines for GST non-registration; interest and shortfall penalties apply.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company face personal liability under the Tax Administration Act 1994 for unpaid GST and PAYE withheld from employees (section 34A), including recovery from the director's personal assets; Companies Office enforcement can lead to a director's disqualification for up to 5 years per non-compliance; if the shell structure is used to launder proceeds, the director is exposed to money laundering charges with a maximum of 7 years' imprisonment and forfeiture of benefits under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, which may include a travel ban as a condition of bail or restraint order.
AML/CFT Non-Compliance and UBO Deception

Statutory sanction: Offence of knowingly providing false or misleading UBO information to the Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993, sections 320 and 377: fine up to NZD 10,000 plus NZD 500 per day; AML/CFT Act 2009 penalties for non-compliance: civil liability up to NZD 2 million for a company; criminal penalties up to NZD 300,000 for individuals and 2 years' imprisonment for willful obstruction (AML/CFT Act 2009, sections 92-94); the court may issue a restraint order freezing company and personal assets.

Director personal liability: Directors of an AML/CFT reporting entity are individually liable for civil penalties up to NZD 200,000 (AML/CFT Act 2009, section 92) if they intentionally or recklessly fail to comply; criminal liability under section 94 gives imprisonment up to 2 years; directors may also be banned from managing a company for up to 5 years under the Companies Act 1993; travel can be restricted through a detention order or bail condition in a proceeds-of-crime prosecution; personal civil liability for restitution of funds laundered through the company can be imposed.
