Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. New Zealand law imposes no minimum paid-up capital and no statutory escrow or blocked deposit requirement. Under the Companies Act 1993, shares may be issued for consideration determined by the board, but any share subscription consideration must be paid or satisfied in accordance with section 38 of the Act. The company may be incorporated with one or more shares of any nominal value, and non-resident ownership is permitted.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1986 to goods physically exported by the supplier within 28 days, and to services, including employer-of-record and labour staffing services, supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time of performance, provided the services are not performed for a New Zealand resident and do not relate directly to land in New Zealand or to other excluded domestic assets.

New Zealand does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance or electronic invoicing; e-invoicing is voluntary on the PEPPOL network; GST returns are filed and paid electronically via myIR; there is no continuous transaction control or anti-fraud digital stamping regime.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates an open and fully convertible currency regime; the NZD is freely floating with no foreign-exchange controls administered by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. Both current and capital account transactions are unrestricted. A New Zealand entity may raise inbound equity, maintain foreign-currency accounts, and remit dividends, royalties, or service fees without routine exchange-control approval. Banks conduct resident bank transaction reporting for statistical purposes only and perform standard AML/CFT checks. There are no surrender requirements on export proceeds or inward remittances. For an EOR staffing agency, contractor payroll outflows and client invoicing in foreign currencies may be settled directly in the selected currency or converted to NZD at commercial dealing rates. The only material friction is documentation: a bank must be satisfied as to the source of funds and the underlying commercial substance of each cross-border flow to discharge obligations under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Profit remittance to foreign shareholders is permitted, subject to solvency and tax compliance such as resident withholding tax on dividends. Because the jurisdiction does not impose investment licences or exchange-control barriers, the treasury design should focus on bank onboarding, counterparty due diligence, and clean clearing rails rather than regulatory approval.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable. New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender obligation, no mandatory conversion period, and no required ratio for export foreign currency receipts. Exporters and service providers may retain, borrow, or convert such proceeds at their discretion.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Conditionally viable as a supplementary rail, not a primary banking substitute. New Zealand has no dedicated EMI licensing regime. An offshore digital EMI may not operate as a bank or accept deposits unless licensed under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand framework and registered on the Financial Service Providers Register. However, registered money remitters and payment facilities can deliver cross-border contractor payouts. Wise is commonly used for low-value payroll disbursements, but for a global EOR with material throughput the treasury should maintain primary balances in NZ tier-1 banks and use an EMI only for small-value, non-critical payments after confirming the EMI's FSP registration, AML/CFT supervision, and trust account segregation.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity into a New Zealand limited company requires no pre-approval, but bank compliance expects the payment narrative to state 'Shareholder Capital Contribution' or 'Equity Injection'. The transfer should be routed as a SWIFT MT103 from the shareholder's own account to the company settlement account at a licensed New Zealand bank and must not be sourced from commingled third-party funds. Where funds arrive in foreign currency, the bank either converts to NZD at the dealing rate or credits a designated multi-currency account. For capital verification, the company must retain the SWIFT reference, a board resolution authorising the share allotment, the shareholder's source-of-funds documentation, an updated share register, an allotment return filed with the New Zealand Companies Office, and an auditor or chartered accountant confirmation of the cleared funds.Local New Zealand contractor payroll is cleared via domestic payroll direct credit through the incumbent bank using NZD same-day clearing. Cross-border contractor payments are best executed via SWIFT MT103 to the contractor's local bank account, using the bank's payment purpose code for payroll or professional services. Commercial letters of credit and documentary collections are not appropriate for payroll disbursements and should be reserved for physical goods or trade finance assets. Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a standard 31 March balance date, the IR4 must be filed by 7 July following the balance date (Tax Administration Act 1994, s 37). Terminal tax on the assessed income is due by 7 February. A tax agent holding a valid extension of time may defer filing to 31 March of the following year, which correspondingly moves the terminal tax due date to 7 April.

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a 31 March balance date, the three provisional tax instalments are due on 28 August, 15 January, and 7 May of the relevant income year, with a terminal balancing payment on 7 February. The standard uplift for a continuing business is the prior year's residual income tax plus 5%.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Box 1A - Total Income All income derived in the income year, including gross revenue from global EOR payroll services, contractor placement fees, employer-of-record margin, and any foreign-source income of the New Zealand resident company. A resident New Zealand company is taxable on worldwide income; the return must include all overseas-derived amounts subject to New Zealand tax, with foreign tax credit claims recorded separately. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand has no single bright-line mandatory Local File threshold, but the Inland Revenue's transfer pricing guidelines require contemporaneous documentation where related-party cross-border transactions aggregate above NZD 1 million for non-financial transactions and NZD 100,000 for related-party loans and financial arrangements. MNE groups with consolidated annual group revenue of NZD 1.14 billion (EUR 750 million) or more must file Country-by-Country Reports with the IRD and retain a Master File. EOR platforms should nonetheless prepare full Local File documentation for all intercompany payroll service fees, software royalties, and management charges, as the IRD applies a risk-based approach.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain separate Economic Substance Regulations of the Cayman/BVI type. However, the Inland Revenue applies the general anti-avoidance rule (ITA 2007 s BG 1), source and permanent establishment principles, and the transfer pricing reconstruction rules (ITA 2007 subpart GC) to disregard shell companies lacking commercial substance. For a global EOR entity, substance is demonstrated by a local board, employment contracts with local staff, operational payroll systems, invoicing functions, risk assumption, and physical premises. A company with no employees, no office, and no payroll function used solely to route contract income to a foreign principal will be treated as a conduit, its profits reconstructed, and penalties applied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Criminal Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax (Tax Administration Act 1994 s 141E). Criminal conviction under Tax Administration Act 1994 s 143B carries a maximum fine of NZD 150,000 and/or imprisonment of up to 15 years. Forfeiture orders apply under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

Director personal liability: Directors are liable as principals or parties to the evasion; the corporate veil is pierced under Companies Act 1994 s 15. Civil restitution orders, restraint and freezing orders, travel bans, and extradition arrest warrants are available against directors.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Base Erosion

Statutory sanction: Taxable income reconstruction under Income Tax Act 2007 s GC 13. Shortfall penalties: 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. Use-of-money interest runs from the original tax due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who execute or approve non-arm's-length intercompany agreements are personally exposed; TAA 1994 s 86 permits the IRD to recover unpaid company tax from directors; reckless trading claims under Companies Act 1994 ss 135–136 may lead to civil restitution and disqualification.
Failure to Deduct PAYE / KiwiSaver / ESCT

Statutory sanction: Unremitted PAYE plus late payment penalties (1% immediately, 4% after 7 days, then 1% monthly) and use-of-money interest. Criminal prosecution under TAA 1994 s 143 can result in fines of up to NZD 50,000; evasion penalties of 150% apply where there is deliberate non-deduction.

Director personal liability: TAA 1994 s 86 imposes personal liability on directors of companies that fail to pay PAYE, KiwiSaver, and GST within 30 days of IRD demand. Directors may face bankruptcy proceedings, asset seizure, and departure prohibition orders.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax Avoidance on Dividend Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The full NRWT amount is recoverable from the New Zealand company, plus late payment penalties and interest. Shortfall penalties up to 150% apply where the non-withholding was deliberate. The IRD may recharacterise the payment under s BG 1 and impose an additional 100% penalty for an abusive tax position.

Director personal liability: Under TAA 1994 s 86, directors are jointly and severally liable for unpaid NRWT when the company defaults. Personal assessment, registration of civil judgments against directors, travel restrictions, and contempt-of-court sanctions are enforceable in NZ courts.
AML/CFT Violations and Shell Company Utilisation

Statutory sanction: Civil pecuniary penalties under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 of up to NZD 2 million per contravention for companies; criminal fines of up to NZD 100,000 and imprisonment of up to 2 years for officers. The Companies Act 1994 permits shareholder filing; IRD can apply for deregistration for persistent non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Senior managers and directors are exposed to personal prosecution, property forfeiture under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, director disqualification under Companies Act 1994 s 383, and cross-border arrest through mutual assistance treaties.
