Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital. A company must allot at least one share to at least one shareholder at incorporation. No escrow account, capital deposit, or solicitor certificate is required; share consideration may be cash, promissory note, or other lawful property under sections 38-41 of the Companies Act 1993.

Funding & unlock path: Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) issued by the Territorial Authority under the Building Act 2004, including structural load inspection for the office floor and any dedicated server/record storage area; annual fire safety inspection and approved evacuation scheme from Fire and Emergency New Zealand (FENZ) where the occupant load exceeds 100 persons; occupational clearance requires full compliance with the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 and the Health and Safety at Work (General Risk and Workplace Management) Regulations 2016, including electrical safety certificates, first-aid provisions, emergency lighting, fire extinguishers, safe means of egress, and the appointment of a health and safety representative.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum paid-up capital. A company must allot at least one share to at least one shareholder at incorporation. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) / Te Tari Taake; online portal: myIR (https://www.ird.govt.nz/myir).

E-government filing portal

A New Zealand-incorporated limited company is registered with the NZ Companies Office at incorporation and must then apply to the IRD for an IRD number. For a foreign/non-resident company carrying on business in New Zealand, the company applies for an IRD number using the non-resident company application process (form IR742 or via an NZ tax agent) and registers for GST where applicable. The procedure is: (1) incorporate the NZ Ltd or register the overseas company as an NZ branch; (2) obtain a company IRD number from the IRD; (3) register for GST, PAYE, and other applicable tax types through myIR; and (4) appoint a New Zealand agent or nominated principal for tax administration if the company has no physical NZ presence.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — NZ-resident company taxed on worldwide income at 28% corporate rate; branch of a non-resident company taxed on New Zealand-sourced income at 28%; no US-style pass-through classification as the default for a limited company.

Available election forms

New Zealand has no 'check-the-box' election for tax classification. Available elections include: (i) voluntary GST registration below the NZD 60,000 threshold; (ii) GST grouping under ss 55-57 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; (iii) look-through company (LTC) status under subpart HA of the Income Tax Act 2007 for closely held NZ-resident companies meeting eligibility criteria; and (iv) consolidated group trading status under the Income Tax Act 2007. Hybrid mismatch adjustments are mandatory under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018, not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period for goods and services supplied in New Zealand; a NZD 60,000 threshold also applies to non-resident suppliers of remote/offshore services supplied to New Zealand consumers. Registration may be voluntary below these thresholds (GSTA s 51).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Executive search and headhunting services (ISIC 7830) are zero-rated under s 11(1)(m) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 when supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed and the services are not supplied directly in connection with land or moveable personal property situated in New Zealand. The supplier must hold sufficient documentary evidence, including the supply agreement, tax invoice, and proof of the recipient's non-resident status, to substantiate that the services were performed for the direct benefit of the offshore client. Physical goods exported from New Zealand are zero-rated under s 11(1)(a) if exported within the statutory time frame; for this executive-search service scope, the relevant export rule is the cross-border services provision.

  • Written engagement or placement agreement with the non-resident client setting out retainer terms, search mandate, and placement fee
  • Tax invoice or commercial invoice issued in compliance with s 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985
  • Evidence of the recipient's non-resident status (overseas VAT/GST registration number, certificate of incorporation, or statutory declaration)
  • Bank transfer or remittance records showing receipt of retainer and success fees via Retainer_Wire or Wise B2B
  • Records evidencing offshore execution of the service such as CV submissions, candidate interview logs, and recruiter work performed outside New Zealand
  • If a physical goods customs flow were involved: NZ Customs export declaration via the Trade Single Window, bill of lading or airway bill, and inward remittance certificate

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, API clearance, or digital stamping regime is in force. e-Invoicing is voluntary and operates on the PEPPOL standard through the NZ e-Invoicing Service. GST compliance is return-based: registered persons file GST returns through myIR on a monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly cycle, with no pre-clearance of individual invoices.

IRD routinely audits export zero-rating claims under s 11(1)(m). If documentation does not establish the non-resident status of the recipient or the offshore consumption of the services, IRD may re-assess the supply at 15% GST, claw back refunded input tax, charge use-of-money interest, and impose shortfall penalties under s 141 of the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for lack of reasonable care or an unacceptable tax position, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. Criminal liability may also arise under ss 143A and 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994, including fines up to NZD 50,000 for a first evasion offence and responsible director exposure. No statutory time bar applies for fraudulent claims; otherwise IRD may amend assessments within four years of the GST return being filed. In substantive audit practice, IRD applies a 180-day inward-repatriation benchmark to corroborate that foreign-sourced fees were monetised and repatriated to New Zealand.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. New Zealand maintains no general exchange-control legislation; dividend repatriation, management fee remittance, shareholder loan repayments, and inbound or outbound equity investments can be executed freely without central bank or exchange-control approval. The only practical requirements are standard anti-money laundering/counter-financing of terrorism (AML/CFT) customer due diligence, tax compliance (e.g. non-resident withholding tax on certain outbound payments), and accurate reporting of offshore balances to Inland Revenue where applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender or mandatory conversion rules for export receipts. Exporters and service providers, including executive-search and headhunting companies, may retain foreign-currency proceeds in offshore accounts or convert them to NZD at any time and at any licensed foreign-exchange dealer. There are no prescribed timelines for repatriation and no minimum conversion percentages. The only relevant obligations arise under general banking AML/CFT reporting thresholds and the requirement that payment instructions accurately state the underlying commercial purpose.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), operating in conjunction with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) and the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE) for financial integrity and sanctions administration; however, the Reserve Bank is the sole monetary authority and prudential supervisor for registered banks in New Zealand.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Medium to High viability for cross-border payment execution, subject to important legal constraints. Offshore digital EMI platforms such as Wise Business, Airwallex, and Payoneer are permissible as supplementary payment rails for retainer collections, disbursements, and FX conversion, but they are not licensed New Zealand banks and cannot provide the client with a domestic settlement account protected by the RBNZ banking regulatory framework. To operate meaningfully into New Zealand, the EMI must either be registered on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and satisfy the AML/CFT Act obligations if carrying on a financial-services business in New Zealand, or the NZ entity must remain the contracting principal and use the EMI solely as an overseas payment gateway. A tier-1 licensed NZ bank account remains the mandatory anchor for capital escrow, statutory record-keeping, and creditor confidence.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder loans and equity injections should be routed by SWIFT MT103 or SWIFT MT202 from the foreign parent/shareholder to the New Zealand company's licensed bank account. The payment reference field must clearly state the economic purpose, preferably as 'Share Capital Contribution', 'Paid-Up Equity', or 'Director/Owner Loan'. No statutory New Zealand purpose-code regime exists; however, receiving banks map remittance information to internal transaction categories, and the company must be ready to provide the executed subscription agreement, board resolution, updated share register, and an AML/CFT source-of-funds explanation if requested. Cross-currency inbound wires are automatically converted by the receiving bank or a corresponding bank at the onshore NZD interbank rate unless an alternative settlement currency has been specifically agreed. No Reserve Bank approval or exchange-control permit is required.Operational trade disbursements and retainer payments to overseas executive-search partners should be executed via SWIFT MT103 through the New Zealand bank's correspondent network. For high-value recruitment settlement between corporates, a Commercial Letter of Credit (UCP600) can be issued by the NZ company's bank in favour of an overseas beneficiary after presentation of a signed placement invoice, employment agreement, and candidate start/deal-sheet evidence. NZD-denominated domestic legs of any such transaction settle over the Exchange Settlement Account System (ESAS), the country's high-value payment system, with the cross-border leg completed through the SWIFT network. Supporting documents for outbound disbursements include the underlying retainer contract, invoice, candidate acceptance evidence, and a valid GST tax invoice. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

28% (resident companies and non-resident companies carrying on business through a New Zealand branch, under Income Tax Act 2007, Schedule 1, Part A)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends is 30% on gross dividends; where the dividend is fully imputed and paid to a non-resident, the NRWT rate is 15%. Under many double tax agreements (DTAs), a full or reduced rate (often 15% or 0% for substantial corporate shareholdings above 10%) may be available. Dividends paid to NZ-resident companies are not subject to NRWT but may attract resident withholding tax (RWT) at 28% for companies or 33% for individuals if imputation credits are insufficient.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not maintain a universally codified master/local file system with a fixed pecuniary threshold. Under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018 and IRD practice, contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is expected where the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions exceeds NZ$10 million in an income year; in assessing penalties, the IRD will consider whether the taxpayer has kept sufficient documentation to support the arm's length outcome. Country-by-Country (CbC) reporting applies to multinational groups with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million (approximately NZ$1.3 billion).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) as used in similar offshore jurisdictions. However, the OECD substance concept is embedded in the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007: arrangements lacking genuine commercial substance that are structured primarily to defeat tax are void against the Commissioner. Economic substance also determines treaty residence for dual-resident companies under Article 4 of DTAs (place of effective management) and can trigger the re-assessment of a New Zealand company as resident or non-resident where board control, management functions, and physical presence are displaced.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Abusive Tax Position

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994: 100% of the tax shortfall for an abusive tax position; 150% for evasion or similar conduct. Criminal prosecution may result in fines up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Interest and late payment penalties are also assessed from the original due date.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in tax evasion or false filing can be prosecuted in their personal capacity and cannot hide behind the corporate veil. The court may impose civil restitution, award compensation orders, and issue asset-restraint or forfeiture orders. Directors charged with serious tax offences may face travel restrictions as a bail condition and imprisonment upon conviction.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may invoke the GAAR (section BG 1) to disregard the arrangement and assess the true profit-maker. The company may be treated as a New Zealand tax resident if its centre of effective management is in NZ, with resulting worldwide income taxation. Phoenix-company penalties, director disqualification, and liquidation powers under the Companies Act 1993 also apply; financial penalties may reach NZ$100,000 plus reinstated tax and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for insolvent trading under sections 131 and 135 of the Companies Act 1993, and for unpaid PAYE/GST-related liabilities if the company is wound up and has failed to meet statutory obligations. The corporate veil is pierced where the company is a mere façade; directors may be ordered to pay civil restitution from personal assets, disqualified from managing companies for up to 5 years, and restricted from leaving New Zealand pending insolvency or criminal investigations.
Transfer Pricing / Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: IRD will adjust the taxable income to an arm's length outcome under sections GC 13–GC 16 of the Income Tax Act 2007, disallow excess interest deductions under thin capitalisation rules, and impose shortfall penalties. If the taxpayer has not taken reasonable care, a 20% penalty applies; for unacceptable tax positions, a 20%–50% penalty may apply; if the dominant purpose is tax avoidance, the penalty can be 100%–150%.

Director personal liability: Directors who design and approve sham transfer-pricing arrangements can be made personally liable for shortfall penalties if they are found to be the directing mind behind the avoidance. In liquidation, the Commissioner may claim against directors for breach of duty of care, and directors may face personal civil awards for losses caused by reckless trading and misrepresentation. Travel bans and imprisonment can follow if the conduct is also prosecuted as tax evasion.
Profit Repatriation / Dividend Stripping and Unpaid NRWT

Statutory sanction: IRD will recharacterise the distribution as a dividend or interest under the anti-avoidance provisions and assess NRWT at the applicable domestic or treaty rate (up to 30% on dividends; 15% on interest/royalties), plus UOMI, late payment penalties, and shortfall penalties. Directors of trustees or intermediaries involved in NRWT evasion may be jointly assessed as agents under the Tax Administration Act 1994.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for unremitted NRWT where the company has acted as a withholding agent or where the arrangement is a sham. They face civil recovery of the evaded tax, director disqualification, asset-freezing orders, and, in cases of deliberate evasion, detention or travel restriction pending trial and imprisonment upon conviction. The corporate veil is pierced where distributions are made to defeat a creditor, including the Crown.
