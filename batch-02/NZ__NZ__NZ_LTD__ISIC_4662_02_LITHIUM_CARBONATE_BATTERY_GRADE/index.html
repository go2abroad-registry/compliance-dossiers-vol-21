Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

New Zealand has no statutory minimum paid-up capital for a limited liability company and no escrow account requirement. Shares may be issued for such consideration as the directors determine, including nil or deferred consideration, and may be issued partly paid unless the constitution provides otherwise. The company must nonetheless maintain a registered office and an address for service in New Zealand.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety clearance under the Fire and Emergency New Zealand Act 2017 and Building Act 2004, including means of escape, fire doors, exit signage, and emergency lighting, is required for all occupied New Zealand premises. No structural load inspection is required for an office-only tenancy, but a workplace safety assessment is recommended for bank account opening and lease counterparties.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; resident companies are taxed on worldwide income at the 28% corporate rate; non-resident companies are taxed only on New Zealand-sourced income. No automatic pass-through; closely-held resident companies may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) transparency, distributing tax attributes to shareholders. For the cross-border lithium carbonate arbitrage model, the entity will be treated as a separate taxable person for both income tax and GST purposes.

Available election forms

Look-Through Company (LTC) election under Subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, available only to qualifying closely-held New Zealand-resident companies whose shareholders are NZ tax residents; no US-style check-the-box election exists. Hybrid mismatch self-adjustment elections and branch mismatch rules under Subpart FH (BEPS) require the entity to align deductions with corresponding inclusions in the counterparty jurisdiction. A non-resident company may also elect the simplified pay-only GST registration option for cross-border remote supplies, and may apply for a binding ruling or GST exemption for supplies exclusively zero-rated, in accordance with the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in the immediately preceding 12 months or reasonably expected in the next 12 months; non-resident suppliers of remote services and low-value goods (including electronic marketplaces) are subject to the same NZD 60,000 threshold under the simplified pay-only GST regime. Volumetric or periodic tests do not apply; registration is per-supplier, with separate thresholds for each GST-registered group or branch.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Supply of goods physically exported from New Zealand by the supplier qualifies for the 0% GST rate under section 11(1)(a) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, provided the goods are exported within 28 days of supply or within any further period allowed by the Commissioner. For battery-grade lithium carbonate powder sold cross-border, the supplier must both physically export the goods and retain contemporaneous documentary proof of export. Supplies of services to non-residents may qualify under section 11A if the services are performed outside New Zealand or are directly connected with goods exported from New Zealand. The zero-rating claim fails if the exporter or an associated party uses the goods in New Zealand before export, or if the goods are subsequently returned.

  • Customs Export Declaration (NZ Customs Service electronic export entry, C3/EE)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT/telegraphic transfer confirmation evidencing cross-border payment
  • Commercial invoice, packing list, and purchase contract demonstrating the overseas buyer and lithium carbonate specification

No real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping mandate exists. New Zealand operates a voluntary e-invoicing framework based on Peppol; central government agencies are required to receive Peppol e-invoices, but commercial issuers may use any format. GST returns are filed on a monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly basis through myIR, and payments are due by the 28th day of the following month. Inland Revenue has broad electronic data-gathering powers, and e-invoicing output may be subject to audit analytics, but there is no pre-clearance or cryptographic tax-stamp requirement.

If export documentation is not substantiated, Inland Revenue will recharacterize the supply as standard-rated (15% GST), assess the resulting output tax with interest from the date of supply, and impose shortfall penalties under Part 9 of the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. Customs false-declaration offences under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 impose civil penalties up to NZD 10,000 per untrue statement, forfeiture of goods, and criminal liability for knowingly false declarations. Retrospective clawbacks may extend for the statutory reassessment period (generally 7 years for GST, 4 years for income tax, and unlimited in cases of fraud, evasion, or non-disclosure). Inland Revenue and NZ Customs operate joint border-intelligence analytics targeting overvalued or fictitious exports, and a zero-rated export claim will be denied where the remittance deadline of 180 days or the 28-day physical export rule is not met.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full convertibility monetary regime. New Zealand removed exchange controls in 1985 with the floating of the NZD and operates under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989, the Companies Act 1993, the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009; no exchange control law or foreign exchange management act is in force. Both current account items (import/export settlement, freight, insurance, commissions, royalties) and capital account items (equity injections, shareholder loans, intercompany financing, dividend distributions, interest payments, loan repayments, and liquidation proceeds) may be freely converted into any currency at market-determined rates and remitted without prior RBNZ approval. There are no surrender requirements, quantitative limits, repatriation timelines, mandatory exchange rate fixings, or profit-remittance permits. The operational checks that do apply are: (i) AML/CFT Act 2009 prescribed transaction reporting for cash and certain cross-border transactions exceeding NZD 10,000 or any suspicious transaction regardless of value; (ii) sanctions and targeted financial measures screening under the United Nations Act 1946, the Russia Sanctions Act 2022, and the New Zealand Consolidated Sanctions List, administered through the NZ Police Financial Intelligence Unit and MFAT; (iii) the Overseas Investment Act 2005, which applies national-interest screening only to non-resident acquisitions of sensitive land or significant business assets above statutory value thresholds (generally NZD 100–200 million) — the procurement and resale of battery-grade lithium carbonate in ordinary inventory is outside the screening regime; and (iv) the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, which regulates the offer and distribution of derivative products but does not restrict hedging. For profit remittance to non-resident shareholders, dividends are subject to Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) unless reduced under an applicable Double Tax Agreement, or where the dividend is fully imputed and supported by New Zealand foreign investor tax credits; no FX approval is required. Interest on qualifying registered offshore debt may be remitted free of withholding tax where the New Zealand borrower elects the Approved Issuer Levy (2% of gross interest) under the Income Tax Act 2007.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export proceeds surrender requirement exists. New Zealand imposes no mandatory conversion ratio, no repatriation deadline, and no holding restriction on foreign currency receipts. An exporting or trading company may lawfully retain export receipts in any foreign currency in onshore or offshore accounts, may use them directly for onward trade settlement, may borrow against them, or may convert them when commercially convenient. The only statutory obligations are reporting and tax-accounting related: (i) financial statements under the Companies Act 1993 must be prepared with a New Zealand-dollar functional reporting overlay; (ii) taxable income is computed in NZD under the Income Tax Act 2007 using either RBNZ published exchange rates or another consistent method accepted by Inland Revenue; (iii) transfer pricing rules (OECD-aligned under the Income Tax Act 2007 and the Tax Administration Act 1994) require related-party cross-border transactions to be at arm's length, which indirectly determines the FX conversion pricing applied to intercompany receivables and payables; and (iv) the RBNZ collects Balance of Payments data, while the AML/CFT Act 2009 triggers prescribed transaction reporting on threshold cash movements. None of these obligations mandates conversion, surrender, or repatriation of export proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for conduct and AML/CFT supervision of banks, and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade for the administration of the New Zealand Consolidated Sanctions List.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Low-to-Medium (supplementary, non-core). Legal constraints: an offshore digital EMI (e.g., Wise Business, Airwallex, Revolut Business, or a Hong Kong/Singapore virtual bank) that provides a New Zealand-dollar account to the NZ Ltd must register on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008, join an approved dispute resolution scheme, and comply as a reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009 with full identity verification, transaction monitoring, sanction screening, and suspicious transaction reporting to the NZ Police Financial Intelligence Unit. In practice, EMIs offer virtual account numbers, multi-currency wallets, and low-value SWIFT/SEPA/CHIPS transfers, but they cannot provide the treasury-grade rails required for battery-grade lithium carbonate procurement: they cannot issue or confirm UCP600 documentary credits, cannot provide committed revolving credit or commodity trade finance, cannot open a segregated Exchange Settlement Account with the RBNZ, and cannot guarantee same-day finality for large-value USD/CNY trade settlement. Chinese and Hong Kong refining counterparties usually demand issuer-bank SWIFT MT103 messages or a letter of credit issued by a Tier-1 New Zealand bank; consequently, the EMI channel should be used only for operating expenses, employee payments, tax payments, and incidental FX, while all trade settlement flows run through primary Tier-1 banking relationships.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility Rating: Low-to-Medium (supplementary, non-core). Legal constraints: an offshore digital EMI (e.g., Wise Business, Airwallex, Revolut Business, or a Hong Kong/Singapore virtual bank) that provides a New Zealand-dollar account to the NZ Ltd must register on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008, join an approved dispute resolution scheme, and comply as a reporting entity under the AML/CFT Act 2009 with full identity verification, transaction monitoring, sanction screening, and suspicious transaction reporting to the NZ Police Financial Intelligence Unit. In practice, EMIs offer virtual account numbers, multi-currency wallets, and low-value SWIFT/SEPA/CHIPS transfers, but they cannot provide the treasury-grade rails required for battery-grade lithium carbonate procurement: they cannot issue or confirm UCP600 documentary credits, cannot provide committed revolving credit or commodity trade finance, cannot open a segregated Exchange Settlement Account with the RBNZ, and cannot guarantee same-day finality for large-value USD/CNY trade settlement. Chinese and Hong Kong refining counterparties usually demand issuer-bank SWIFT MT103 messages or a letter of credit issued by a Tier-1 New Zealand bank; consequently, the EMI channel should be used only for operating expenses, employee payments, tax payments, and incidental FX, while all trade settlement flows run through primary Tier-1 banking relationships.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand transfer pricing rules apply to cross-border related-party transactions irrespective of a de minimis turnover threshold for the core obligation to transact at arm's length. However, Inland Revenue's operational threshold for requiring contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation (master file and local file) is NZ$3 million in aggregate annual related-party cross-border transacting for the local New Zealand entity. Where the aggregate value of related-party transactions is NZ$3 million or more, documentation must be prepared before the filing date of the income tax return. Inland Revenue may also require documentation below this threshold on a risk-assessed basis. The NZ$3 million threshold is set by Inland Revenue's Transfer Pricing Guidelines (TPG) and is applied consistently with OECD TP Guidelines.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a specific Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to the UAE. However, the general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under section BG1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 disregards arrangements whose sole or main purpose is tax avoidance. In addition, the business profits article of New Zealand's double tax agreements incorporates a permanent establishment threshold, and the 'land-rich' and other anti-avoidance provisions may apply. For a New Zealand limited company claiming treaty benefits or carrying on procurement and trading of lithium carbonate, Inland Revenue will apply the OECD substance-over-form doctrine, requiring real decision-making, risk management, and operational substance in New Zealand. If the company is a mere conduit or shell acting for a non-resident principal, the Commissioner may recharacterise the arrangement, attribute income to the non-resident under the transfer-pricing or GAAR rules, and deny treaty benefits. The company must maintain a board, bank account, records, and operational substance commensurate with its functions, assets, and risks.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (fraudulent tax filing)

Statutory sanction: Criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994 (sections 143A and 143B): the maximum fine for a company is up to NZ$50,000 for knowledge-based evasion errors, plus a further fine of up to NZ$50,000 if intent to evade tax is proven. Civil shortfall penalties under Part 7 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply: 150% of the shortfall for evasion or a gross carelessness penalty of 40% without voluntary disclosure; an abusive tax position penalty of 100% applies to arrangements with a dominant purpose of tax avoidance. Interest on the shortfall is calculated from the due date.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable under the Financial Reporting Act 2013 and the Companies Act 1993 for false statements, and Inland Revenue may apply a director penalty under section 34B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 for unpaid PAYE or GST (not income tax, unless the director is a promoter of a fraudulent arrangement), permitting recovery of unpaid tax from directors personally if the company has failed to meet tax obligations and directors knew or should have known. For deliberate evasion, directors may face imprisonment of up to 5 years under section 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 and personal civil restitution for tax losses. Travel bans and asset freezes may be imposed under the Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters Act 1992 where extradition or restraint orders are issued.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may recharacterise the company as a sham or conduit, invalidate transfer pricing arrangements, and increase taxable income under sections GC1 to GC7 of the Income Tax Act 2007. Shortfall penalties for unacceptable tax position (20%) or abusive tax position (100%) may be imposed. The company may be struck off under the Companies Act 1993 if it has no active business or is used for illegal purposes. The Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT) imposes obligations on reporting entities; a shell structure without beneficial ownership transparency can be reported as suspicious, and the company's bank accounts may be frozen.

Director personal liability: Directors and shadow directors are jointly and severally liable for the company's obligations if the company trades recklessly or fraudulently under section 135 and section 136 of the Companies Act 1993. A director may be personally disqualified from managing companies for up to 10 years under section 383 of the Companies Act and prohibited from travelling pursuant to a restraint order under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. Imprisonment of up to 7 years applies under section 380 of the Companies Act for making false statements or permitting fraudulent trading.
AML/CFT Breach and Structuring Sanctions Evasion

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, reporting entities (including banks and certain financial service providers) must report suspicious transactions to the Financial Intelligence Unit. The New Zealand Police Financial Intelligence Unit can issue suspicious transaction reports; penalties for AML/CFT failures are administered by the Department of Internal Affairs and can reach NZ$5 million for a body corporate for each serious breach. Criminal sanctions under the Crimes Act 1961 for money laundering carry a maximum term of 7 years' imprisonment. Sanctions evasion under the Russia Sanctions Act 2022 or the United Nations Act 1946 is a criminal offence with imprisonment up to 7 years and fines at the court's discretion.

Director personal liability: Directors can be prosecuted for knowingly facilitating money laundering or sanctions evasion. Personal liability includes up to 7 years' imprisonment, criminal fines, asset confiscation under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, and travel bans under the Extradition Act 1999 or terrorism suppression. Civil liability for breach of AML/CFT obligations is joint and several where a director has directed or participated in the breach; the director may be ordered to pay restitution and be disqualified from holding a management role.
Transfer Pricing and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may adjust the taxable income under sections GC6 to GC14 of the Income Tax Act 2007 to reflect an arm's length result. A shortfall penalty of 40% for gross carelessness or 100% for an abusive tax position may be imposed, plus use-of-money interest on the underpaid tax. Inland Revenue may also impose a document-production penalty of up to NZ$50,000 under the Tax Administration Act for failure to maintain transfer-pricing documentation. A tax avoidance arrangement can be voided under section BG1, and the Commissioner may reconstruct the arrangement to include a fair profit margin in New Zealand. Criminal liability is possible where false documentation is created.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false transfer-pricing documentation or knowingly participate in profit-shifting arrangements may be jointly liable for civil shortfall penalties under section 31B of the Tax Administration Act 1994 (which extends the Commissioner's power to recover penalties from directors and shareholders who are party to the avoidance arrangement). Directors may face criminal prosecution for evading tax and imprisonment up to 5 years, personal civil debt for the shortfall plus penalties, and prohibition orders under the Companies Act.
