Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand; online portal: myIR Secure Online Services; corporate/entity registration via the New Zealand Companies Office and New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) Registry

E-government filing portal

For an NZ-incorporated limited company, first incorporate with the New Zealand Companies Office and obtain an NZBN. Then apply to Inland Revenue for an IRD number using the prescribed application for a non-resident-owned company, which requires the NZBN, registered office address in New Zealand, director and shareholder details, and the company's tax residency status. After the IRD number is issued, GST registration is completed through myIR using the GST registration application; the GST registration number is the same as the IRD number. For a foreign non-resident entity not carrying on an active business in New Zealand, an IRD number may still be required for GST registration when supplying remote services or imported goods to New Zealand; the application is made through myIR or using the relevant Inland Revenue form, and the entity must provide foreign registration evidence, jurisdictional tax identification, contact details, and estimated New Zealand turnover. No separate TIN/VAT ID is issued under a different numbering sequence because the IRD number operates as the unified tax identifier in New Zealand.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election is available under New Zealand law. Eligible close-held resident companies may elect look-through company (LTC) treatment under the Income Tax Act 2007 to have income, expenses, gains, and losses attributed to shareholders; consolidated group treatment is available for groups of wholly owned New Zealand resident companies; tax loss grouping elections are permitted between commonly owned companies. Hybrid mismatch rules under the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018 apply and override elective outcomes that would otherwise exploit hybrid entity mismatches.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any continuous 12-month period, including for non-resident offshore suppliers of remote services supplied to New Zealand GST-registered or non-registered recipients; registration is voluntary below this threshold but mandatory once the threshold is crossed.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. For goods, supplies are zero-rated when they are exported by the supplier or delivered to a non-resident who exports them from New Zealand within the statutory timeframe. For services, including AI/LLM inference API subscriptions and token-based digital platform services, zero-rating applies when the services are supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply and when the services are not supplied for direct use or consumption in New Zealand; remote services supplied to an offshore GST-registered business may also be zero-rated if the supplier holds sufficient documentary evidence of the recipient's non-resident status and the absence of New Zealand use. Services supplied to New Zealand-resident customers remain subject to 15% GST.

  • For tangible exports: New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window (TSW) export declaration or outward cargo report for departing goods
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the New Zealand supplier, overseas buyer, and exact goods description
  • For sea: Bill of Lading; for air: Air Waybill evidencing physical export from New Zealand
  • GST tax invoice issued by the New Zealand supplier stating the zero-rating basis and the overseas customer's identity
  • Evidence of cross-border payment received in the New Zealand entity's bank account, such as bank SWIFT/credit advice or foreign exchange repatriation record, demonstrating settlement from the foreign customer

New Zealand does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or digital anti-fraud stamping regime. GST-registered suppliers must issue compliant GST tax invoices and file GST returns electronically through myIR on a monthly, two-monthly, or six-monthly filing cycle depending on turnover and registration preference. E-invoicing is voluntary and available through the Peppol network; Inland Revenue may access transactional records through electronic data capture or information requests, but there is no mandatory API clearance stamping requirement.

Inland Revenue may audit zero-rated export supplies and retrospectively assess GST, plus use-of-money interest, if the exporter or service supplier cannot substantiate the non-resident status of the recipient, physical export of goods, or the fact that services were not consumed in New Zealand. Civil shortfall penalties apply on the resulting GST shortfall: 20% for lack of reasonable care or an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion; interest runs from the original due date of the output tax that would have applied had the supply not been zero-rated. New Zealand has no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline; the 180-day figure is used as an evidentiary retention and audit review benchmark rather than as a legal repatriation requirement.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised capital and current account convertibility framework. There are no foreign exchange controls, no central bank approval requirements for inward or outward capital flows, and no restrictions on the repatriation of profits, dividends, royalties, or capital. The New Zealand dollar is freely floating and trades actively in global markets. Resident and non-resident entities may hold foreign currency bank accounts domestically and abroad without prior authorisation. There are no surrender requirements for export proceeds, and businesses are not required to convert foreign currency receipts into NZD by any statutory timeline. The only relevant obligations flow from the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, which requires banks to conduct customer due diligence and report suspicious transactions. For a licensed AI/LLM API platform generating revenue from global customers, the convertibility of USD, EUR, or other settlement currencies into NZD for operational expenses and onward dividend distributions is entirely unrestricted. Profit remittance hurdles are effectively nil from a regulatory perspective, provided the underlying transactions are commercially genuine, fully documented, and consistent with the company's registered business purpose. Banks may still apply internal compliance screening to high-risk payment sources such as cryptocurrency-derived funds, but this is a KYC matter rather than a statutory exchange control barrier.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand imposes no statutory surrender timeline and no mandatory conversion ratio for export foreign currency receipts. Exporters may retain all proceeds in foreign currency, hold them in offshore accounts, or convert them freely at commercial bank rates subject only to internal bank policy and AML/CFT record-keeping requirements.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) have limited viability for core treasury operations in New Zealand. The jurisdiction does not have an EU-style EMI licence regime, and non-bank digital wallet providers are not authorised to offer full corporate deposit-taking services. Offshore digital EMIs may provide payment initiation or card issuing services with limited adoption by local clearing systems, but they cannot replace a licensed New Zealand bank for capital escrow, foreign exchange settlement, or commercial trade finance. Legal constraints include the need for registration on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and compliance with the AML/CFT Act if operating locally. For the AI/LLM API platform, an EMI could be used solely as a supplementary collections gateway, but the primary treasury account must be held with an NZ-registered licensed bank to secure FMA/SOE oversight, clearing payment access, and credible audit trail.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan capital for the NZ limited company must arrive via SWIFT MT103 from the originating shareholder's bank account using the company's Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) and NZ BIC. The remittance reference should include 'Shareholder Capital Subscription — NZ_LTD ISIC 6201' to prevent misclassification. Since New Zealand has no exchange control approval or mandatory FX declaration, the receiving bank will generate a post-verification certificate confirming settlement and credit of funds. The company should then issue shares or a registered loan agreement, and complete a capital verification memorandum signed by a New Zealand chartered accountant. For funding routed through a crypto or USDC intermediary, the bank will require a detailed crypto-to-fiat conversion flow and a source-of-funds trail before accepting the credit. Use of an underlying purpose code is optional, but if required, banks commonly reference 'capital payments' or 'investment in enterprise' under the IMF BPM6 classification. No lodgement of the investment is required under the Overseas Investment Act for the AI/LLM API sector, unless sensitive land or significant business assets thresholds are triggered.For outbound trade disbursements the company should maintain segregated clearing rails. Standard supplier and infrastructure payments are made through SWIFT MT103 messages using NZD, USD, or CNH accounts, with invoice reference numbers embedded in the payment details. Large capital expenditures for GPU cloud capacity or model inference hardware are best settled via commercial documentary letters of credit (L/C) when the counterparty requires proof of shipment; otherwise wire transfer with a clean shipping guarantee and bill of lading copy is viable. For recurring API licensing fees, a master service agreement plus monthly statement invoice is sufficient for direct SWIFT transfers. Companies may also use documentary collections (D/P at sight) for moderate-value import/export arrangements where neither full prepayment nor L/C is preferred. Non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends is 30%, but reduced to 5% if the dividend is fully imputed and an approved issuer levy (AIL) election is made. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not have a distinct local currency threshold (NZD) for mandatory Local File / Master File preparation like OCDE countries. However, the tax administration requires transfer pricing documentation to be prepared and retained if the value of cross-border related-party transactions exceeds NZ$1 million for purchases or NZ$1 million for sales (and for loans, if the value of the loan exceeds NZ$5 million). In practice, any material related-party transaction must be documented on an arm's-length basis.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a formal separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) law like the UAE, but the CFC and general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) require an NZ company to have real economic substance. Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) within the context of the OECD BEPS Actions 8-10 and a limited-risk entity analysis apply. For an AI LLM API platform, the company must demonstrate that it has the significant people functions (management, development, and risk control) in NZ to justify its profits and prevent profit shifting to or from related overseas entities. The Inland Revenue can recharacterize transactions under the GAAR (section BG 1, Income Tax Act 2007) if the economic substance of the arrangement is a tax avoidance scheme.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Under-Reporting

Statutory sanction: Tax Administration Act 1994, sections 143A and 143B: evasion shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax; criminal conviction fines up to NZ$50,000 for individuals and NZ$100,000 for companies; imprisonment up to 5 years for directors or officers under section 143A(2)(g) and 146.

Director personal liability: Under the Companies Act 1993 section 135, a director who allows trading while insolvent or engages in tax evasion may be personally liable for company debts. The court can order civil asset restitution under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009. Travel can be restricted through a customs departure ban under the Tax Administration Act 1994 section 157 if tax debt remains unpaid. Imprisonment is possible for up to 5 years.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Thin Capitalisation

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act 2007, sections GC 6 to GC 14 (transfer pricing adjustment) and section GC 15 (thin capitalisation) deny excess interest deductions, resulting in additional income tax of 28%, plus use-of-money interest, and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 (up to 40% for lack of reasonable care or 100% for abusive tax position).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign tax returns with false transfer pricing statements can be personally liable for penalties under section 148 of the Tax Administration Act 1994. Courts may pierce the corporate veil under the Companies Act 1993 if the company is an agent or sham, and directors can be barred from managing a company for up to 5 years under section 383.
AML and Shell Company Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT) imposes civil penalties up to NZ$200,000 for an individual and NZ$2 million for a body corporate for each violation of customer due diligence or record-keeping requirements, and criminal penalties for money laundering under the Crimes Act 1961 (sections 243-245) with imprisonment up to 7 years.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for AML/CFT compliance obligations if they knowingly allow the company to be used for money laundering or terrorist financing. Financial Markets Authority or reserve bank can issue a Pecuniary Penalty order against the director. Courts can order asset forfeiture and impose travel bans. Additionally, the OECD-based 'global transparency' regime under the Companies Act requires directorship details to be publicly available, so a director cannot rely on nominee secrecy.
