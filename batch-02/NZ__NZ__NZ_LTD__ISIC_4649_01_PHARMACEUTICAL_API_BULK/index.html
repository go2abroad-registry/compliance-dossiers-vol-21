Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A New Zealand limited company must maintain a registered office in New Zealand at a physical street address where documents may be served; a post office box is not sufficient. A virtual office provided by a licensed corporate services provider with a genuine physical street address, staffed reception, and secure mail handling may satisfy the Companies Act for service-of-document purposes. However, for an ISIC 4649 high-risk pharmaceutical API wholesale entity, the Inland Revenue Department and commercial banks will generally reject a pure commercial mailbox or shared reception address. The company must demonstrate a dedicated physical premises, accessible records, source-document storage, and personnel who can receive unannounced inspectors. A virtual office will be accepted only if it is a compliance-forward registered office arrangement under a reputable corporate services provider that maintains UBO files and does not operate as a shell mailbox.

E-government filing portal

For the New Zealand resident company: (1) incorporate NZ_LTD with the New Zealand Companies Office and obtain the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) and certificate of incorporation; (2) apply to Inland Revenue through myIR for income tax registration to obtain the company's IRD number, which is the statutory tax identification number; (3) if the company makes or intends to make taxable supplies exceeding the GST registration threshold, submit the GST registration application in myIR under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, using the IRD number as the GST registration identifier; (4) if exporting goods, register with the New Zealand Customs Service to obtain a Customs Client Code and lodge export entries through the Trade Single Window; (5) for a non-resident foreign entity without New Zealand incorporation, apply directly to Inland Revenue through myIR for an IRD number and GST registration using the non-resident supplier registration pathway; no minimum paid-up capital is required for a New Zealand limited company.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

The New Zealand income tax system has no check-the-box election. A closely held New Zealand limited company may elect look-through company (LTC) treatment under subpart HG of the Income Tax Act 2007, making the company fiscally transparent for shareholder purposes, subject to eligibility criteria. New Zealand also applies hybrid mismatch rules under subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007, but these are anti-avoidance rules, not elective regimes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 (excluding GST) of taxable supplies made in New Zealand in any 12-month period; for non-resident suppliers of remote services to New Zealand resident consumers, the compulsory registration threshold is also NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, a registered supplier may apply the 0% GST rate to goods physically exported from New Zealand where the goods are exported in the course of the supply and the supplier retains sufficient evidence of export. Services supplied by a New Zealand resident supplier to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time of performance may also be zero-rated under the statutory services tests, provided the services are not directly connected with New Zealand land or goods. Inbound remote and low-value digital services supplied by non-resident suppliers into New Zealand are not zero-rated and are instead subject to GST at 15% when the statutory nexus threshold is met.

  • New Zealand Customs Service Export Declaration / Export Entry lodged through the Trade Single Window system
  • Bill of Lading (ocean) or Airway Bill (air) evidencing physical export from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or New Zealand bank inward remittance / foreign exchange record evidencing repatriation of sale proceeds to the exporting entity

No mandatory real-time electronic invoicing, fiscal clearance, API pre-clearance, or anti-fraud digital stamping regime is currently legislated in New Zealand. Registered persons must issue tax invoices containing the prescribed particulars under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and the Tax Administration Act 1994. PEPPOL e-invoicing is available on an opt-in basis through the New Zealand e-Invoicing network; Inland Revenue does not require pre-clearance of individual invoices.

If export evidence is deficient, Inland Revenue will reassess GST at 15% rather than at 0%, with use-of-money interest and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994, including penalties up to 150% for evasion or abusive tax positions. Fraudulent or unverified export zero-rating claims may also lead to criminal prosecution, and false export entries may attract penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised and freely convertible currency regime under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is traded without exchange controls, and both current account and capital account transactions are fully convertible without prior approval from the central bank. There are no surrender requirements for export proceeds, no forced conversion into local currency, and no restrictions on the repatriation of dividends, profits, or capital. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not administer foreign exchange controls; its role in this context is monetary policy, financial system stability, and licensing of banks and non-bank deposit takers. A wholesale pharmaceutical API distribution entity is free to maintain foreign currency accounts, settle trade obligations in USD, EUR, or any other major currency, and transfer funds in or out of New Zealand. The primary operational hurdles are not regulatory FX constraints but rather standard bank onboarding due diligence, anti-money laundering obligations, and the requirements of the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. For profit remittance, the only relevant considerations are compliance with the Companies Act 1993 regarding solvency tests, payment of any applicable withholding taxes to the Inland Revenue Department, and maintaining proper transfer pricing documentation for related-party cross-border transactions. In summary, New Zealand presents essentially zero currency convertibility friction for a multinational treasury, making it a highly efficient jurisdiction for global liquidity management, although the bank and trust-account oversight remain robust and require substantive compliance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory surrender requirement; export foreign currency receipts may be retained in original currencies indefinitely. Mandatory conversion ratio is zero percent, and there is no prescribed timeline for repatriation, requiring only normal commercial settlement in accordance with bank credit terms and a company's own treasury policy.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Digital-only financial institutions and electronic money institutions (EMIs) are permissible in New Zealand to the extent they are registered as financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. However, true bank-grade clearing services, SWIFT membership, and direct local NZD settlement rails are dominated by licensed registered banks. Most offshore EMIs are not locally licenced banks and therefore rely on sponsor-bank arrangements; their ability to issue NZD-denominated accounts to a New Zealand entity is legally possible but operationally constrained. For a high-risk pharmaceutical API wholesale business, reliance on an offshore EMI is inadvisable for primary treasury operations because of heightened correspondent-bank de-risking, strict KYC expectations, and limited access to trade finance instruments such as commercial letters of credit. The recommended route is a licensed New Zealand incorporated bank account with a full-service relationship manager, using SWIFT MT103 and documentary collections. Fintech EMIs can only be considered as a secondary supplemental collection rail for small- value low-risk transactions, and only with a registered financial service provider number and clear AML/CFT compliance programme.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan injections into a New Zealand limited company are routed via standard SWIFT MT103 wire transfers from the overseas corporate parent or ultimate beneficial owner. The transfer must clearly state the remittance purpose using a standard economic purpose code, commonly 'capital injection', 'shareholder loan', or 'intercompany funding'. Although New Zealand does not impose exchange control approval, the receiving bank will record the capital contribution for AML/CFT purposes and may require a copy of the board resolution authorising the issuance of shares, a share subscription agreement, and a certificate of incorporation. Capital verification is evidenced by the company's annual return filed with the Companies Office, and where the contribution is made as equity, a share allotment must be lodged. The funds must be credited to the designated capital escrow or operating account, and the source-of-funds documentation must be retained to support future dividend repatriation or loan interest deductions. In practice, for a pharmaceutical distribution entity, the bank will expect a clear written explanation of the ultimate intended use of the injected capital, particularly if it is to prepay suppliers for API inventory.Outbound trade disbursements for pharmaceutical API wholesale transactions are cleared primarily through SWIFT MT103 bank-to-bank customer credit transfers for smaller settlements, commercial letters of credit (LCs) for large and high-value shipments, and documentary collections (D/P and D/A) for established trading relationships. For LC-backed trades, the issuing bank in New Zealand will transmit SWIFT MT700 instructions to the beneficiary's bank, and settlement will occur upon presentation of compliant documents including commercial invoice, bill of lading, certificate of analysis, and pharmaceutical import licences. Documentary collections are used to reduce risk when counterparties are known but not sufficiently creditworthy for open account terms, with payments made against sight drafts or time drafts. Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: Under the Companies Act 1993 and Financial Reporting Act 2013, a New Zealand company must undergo a mandatory independent statutory audit if it has total assets exceeding NZD 60 million at balance date, or annual revenue exceeding NZD 30 million; a company that is an FMC reporting entity (for example, a listed issuer or an issuer with more than 50 holders of equity or debt securities) is always subject to audit regardless of asset or revenue size; a headcount trigger is not separately used in New Zealand, but employer and payroll reporting obligations attach at headcount milestones. Smaller companies are exempt from audit unless shareholders holding 5% or more of shares request an audit or a shareholder majority resolves that audited financial statements be prepared; a large consolidated group must also audit the parent and controlled entities. The audit must be carried out by a licensed statutory auditor authorised under the Auditor Regulation Act 2011; a 'CPA' in New Zealand context must be a member of Chartered Accountants Australia + New Zealand and licensed by the Financial Markets Authority.
