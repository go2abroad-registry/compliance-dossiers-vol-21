Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office, commercial mailbox, or mail-forwarding address is not permissible as the statutory registered office of a New Zealand company because section 189 of the Companies Act 1993 requires a registered office to be a physical address within New Zealand where documents can be served during ordinary business hours. Furthermore, the Inland Revenue Department and New Zealand commercial banks generally reject a virtual-office-only arrangement for an offshore secretarial and company administration business because it lacks operational substance, local decision-making capacity, and personnel. A serviced professional suite with a dedicated physical desk, file storage, personnel, meeting space, and signage can meet the registered office and commercial substance threshold, provided it is not merely a postal forwarding point and is included in the company's books and records as the head office.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. New Zealand law imposes no minimum paid-up capital for a limited liability company and no blocked escrow account rule. Shares may be allotted for any consideration or for no paid-up capital at formation, subject only to solvency and the constitution. If foreign ownership is involved, the Overseas Investment Act 2005 screening may apply only to specified land or sensitive assets, not to ordinary share capital.

Funding & unlock path: Building Warrant of Fitness and Fire Safety Certificate for the occupied premises under the Building Act 2004; annual fire evacuation procedures and safety checks as required by Fire and Emergency New Zealand; Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 worker engagement, first aid, welfare and hazards register; and Civil Defence Emergency Management Act 2002 business continuity measures applicable to registered-agent and secretarial office operations.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue (IRD) of New Zealand; online portal: myIR at ird.govt.nz. Entity formation also requires registration with the New Zealand Companies Office and allocation of a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN).

E-government filing portal

For the NZ_LTD company: (1) incorporate through the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993, obtaining a company number and NZBN; (2) apply to Inland Revenue via myIR for an IRD number, providing the certificate of incorporation, registered office address, director and shareholder particulars, consent-to-act forms for non-resident officers, and bank account information for refunds; (3) apply for GST registration through the same myIR channel or separately where only GST obligations apply. For a foreign non-resident entity not incorporated in New Zealand but making supplies subject to NZ GST, the non-resident registers for an IRD number and GST using non-resident registration procedures in myIR, submitting equivalent foreign entity identification and proof of nexus. There is no minimum capital requirement and non-resident ownership is permitted for NZ limited companies, but a New Zealand registered office and a resident agent or director must be maintained.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation (a New Zealand limited company is a separate corporate tax payer assessed on worldwide income; there is no default pass-through classification and no export free-zone standalone tax classification). — For the NZ_LTD company: (1) incorporate through the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993, obtaining a company number and NZBN; (2) apply to Inland Revenue via myIR for an IRD number, providing the certificate of incorporation, registered office address, director and shareholder particulars, consent-to-act forms for non-resident officers, and bank account information for refunds; (3) apply for GST registration through the same myIR channel or separately where only GST obligations apply. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (a New Zealand limited company is a separate corporate tax payer assessed on worldwide income; there is no default pass-through classification and no export free-zone standalone tax classification).

Available election forms

None comparable to a US check-the-box regime. New Zealand law provides a consolidated group election for wholly-owned company groups under the Income Tax Act 2007 and a GST grouping election under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Hybrid mismatch rules apply as mandatory statute, not as elective classifications.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any consecutive 12-month period for compulsory registration; voluntary registration is available below the threshold. Non-resident suppliers of remote services to New Zealand resident consumers are required to register and account for GST once their taxable supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, goods physically exported from New Zealand are eligible for the 0% GST rate when the supplier or a person on the supplier's behalf exports the goods and proper evidence of export is retained. Supplies of services to non-residents may be zero-rated under the offshore services rules if the services are performed and consumed outside New Zealand and are not caught by the New Zealand land/property or goods exceptions. For an ISIC 8299 registered-agent and offshore secretarial business, corporate secretarial, compliance and administrative services supplied to an overseas client for use wholly outside New Zealand are treated as exported services eligible for zero-rating when the non-resident status of the recipient and the offshore consumption are documented. Remote/digital services to non-resident consumers likewise follow the destination principle; in all cases, the taxpayer must retain the statutory evidence before claiming 0%.

  • New Zealand Customs Service electronic Export Entry lodged through the Trade Single Window clearly linking the exporter, consignee, goods value and export movement
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure of the goods from New Zealand customs territory
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing the exported goods and the sales contract
  • Bank payment or remittance records, settlement advice or foreign currency conversion statement showing the foreign buyer's actual payment (New Zealand does not issue a statutory FIRC; equivalent bank FX records are retained as the forex repatriation evidence)
  • Carrier statement, container tracking record or port/airport release documentation corroborating the outbound movement

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, no government API pre-approval, and no anti-fraud digital stamping. E-invoicing is voluntary in New Zealand, with the Peppol infrastructure used when taxpayers opt in. Tax invoices must contain the content required by the GST Act 1985, and GST returns and payments are submitted electronically to Inland Revenue through myIR.

Inland Revenue matches GST returns, NZCS export entries, carrier records and banking data to verify exports. If a 0% GST export claim is unsupported, the supply is recharacterized as taxable at 15% GST and the unpaid tax is clawed back. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply: 20% for failing to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position and 150% for evasion; use-of-money interest runs from the original GST due date, and criminal prosecution may follow for fraudulent or knowingly false export zero-rating documentation.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully convertible currency regime. The New Zealand Dollar is free-floating and traded actively in global offshore markets. There are no exchange control laws requiring central bank approval for current account or capital account transactions. Residents and non-residents may freely transfer funds in and out of the country subject only to general anti-money laundering and countering financing of terrorism verification requirements. The Reserve Bank, while supervising licensed banks, does not ration currency or impose surrender obligations. Dividends, management fees, and loan repayments can be remitted abroad without a foreign exchange license, provided underlying transactions are commercially genuine and comply with tax disclosure and New Zealand Inland Revenue obligations. For inbound direct investment, the Overseas Investment Act 2005 may require consent for certain sensitive land and significant business assets, but this is a foreign investment screening regime, not a currency control. Capital account transactions are equally open, with no central bank filing or pre-approval requirement beyond AML/CFT-induced documentation and financial institution risk tolerance. Therefore, liquidity planning focuses on banking counterparty access, SWIFT rails, tax withholding costs, and documentary integrity rather than currency convertibility constraints.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. New Zealand does not impose any statutory surrender requirements or mandatory conversion ratios for export foreign currency receipts. Exporters may retain foreign currency earnings indefinitely, hold them in foreign currency accounts, or convert them to New Zealand Dollars at commercial market rates. There is no prescribed timeline for conversion and no Reserve Bank of New Zealand reporting obligation unless the transaction is subject to separate AML/CFT threshold reporting.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Fintech EMIs are not legally barred from serving New Zealand entities, but their practical viability is low-to-medium for this corporate secretarial structure. EMIs must register on the New Zealand Financial Service Providers Register but do not hold a banking licence, cannot accept NZD deposits, and lack direct access to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand settlement system or domestic sort-code clearing. They can provide convenient multi-currency IBANs, virtual accounts, and payment cards for secondary disbursements, but they are not suitable as the primary treasury rail for a medium-risk enhanced company secretarial entity. A licensed domestic Tier-1 bank is mandatory for capital escrow, genuine trade settlement, and FX hedging due to credit and regulatory certainty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a standard 31 March balance date, the IR4 return must be filed by 7 July of the same calendar year. Tax agents operating under an extension arrangement may defer the filing due date, typically to 31 March of the following year; provisional tax instalment obligations remain payable on their statutory due dates.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10 million aggregate annual cross-border related-party transactions (this threshold is the Inland Revenue risk-based expectation for contemporaneous documentation; no explicit general statutory minimum exists, but documentation is essential for shortfall penalty relief under the Transfer Pricing Guidelines and the Tax Administration Act 1994).

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations. However, the Companies Act 1993 requires a registered office, a director, and proper accounting records in New Zealand. For tax purposes, the general anti-avoidance rule in Income Tax Act 2007 section BG 1, controlled foreign company rules, and transfer pricing rules operate to recharacterise artificial arrangements lacking real commercial substance. An entity in the ISIC 8299_01 registered-agent and offshore secretarial sector must additionally satisfy AML/CFT reporting-entity obligations and demonstrate genuine client-administration functions to avoid being treated as a shell or conduit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty of up to 150% of the tax shortfall under Tax Administration Act 1994; criminal prosecution under section 143B exposes individuals to imprisonment of up to 5 years and fines up to NZD 50,000, while a body corporate may be fined up to NZD 200,000 for each offence; use-of-money interest and late payment penalties also accrue.

Director personal liability: Directors who orchestrate or knowingly assist in the filing of false returns are personally exposed to criminal prosecution and may be disqualified from managing companies under Companies Act 1993. Inland Revenue may obtain a departure prohibition order preventing a director from leaving New Zealand while significant unpaid tax liabilities remain.
Shell Company / Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may apply the general anti-avoidance rule, disregard the corporate structure, recharacterise income, and deny treaty benefits under the Multinational Instrument Principal Purpose Test. Shortfall penalties of up to 50% apply for an abusive tax position, increasing to 150% for evasion. Repeated failure to maintain a genuinely operative registered office can lead to company deregistration under the Companies Act 1993.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders who use a shell company as a facade may be jointly and severally assessed on the adjusted taxable income. Inland Revenue may pursue civil recovery against directors personally, impose management bans, and use asset-restraint or departure-prohibition measures where tax debts are being stripped through dividend distributions.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue will recompute taxable income under the arm's-length principle. Penalties range from 20% of the tax shortfall for failure to take reasonable care or adopting an unacceptable tax position, 50% for an abusive tax avoidance position, and 150% for evasion. The disallowed payments may be recharacterised as non-deductible distributions or dividends subject to NRWT.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve transfer pricing declarations without reasonable care can be subject to shortfall penalties in their capacity as tax filers. In fraud cases, veil-piercing may occur, making directors personally liable for the tax shortfall and applicable penalties.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Unremitted NRWT becomes a debt due to the Crown; the payer is subject to shortfall penalties, late payment penalties, and use-of-money interest. For deliberate evasion, criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994 applies. The statutory rates are 30% for dividends and 15% for interest and royalties, unless a reduced treaty rate or Approved Issuer Levy applies.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for withheld or deductible tax that the company fails to remit, especially where the company is liquidated and the Crown is an unsecured creditor. If a director knowingly participates in a scheme to strip assets and avoid NRWT, the corporate veil may be pierced and travel bans or imprisonment imposed.
Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT)

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, a body corporate can face civil penalties of up to NZD 2 million for a significant contravention, and individuals can face criminal penalties including imprisonment for up to 7 years for money laundering offences. The statutory supervisor may suspend or cancel the reporting entity's registration and issue enforceable remediation orders.

Director personal liability: Directors, compliance officers, and senior managers who knowingly participate in laundering proceeds of crime are exposed to personal criminal liability and imprisonment. They may also be disqualified as officers of reporting entities, banned from managing companies, and subjected to civil asset-recovery proceedings and travel restrictions.
