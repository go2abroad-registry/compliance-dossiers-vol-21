Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office or commercial mailbox is not permissible as a registered office for the New Zealand company. Section 192 of the Companies Act 1993 requires the registered office to be a physical address in New Zealand that is open to the public during normal business hours and is not a post office box. A serviced-office arrangement with a physical meeting room, dedicated desk, and signed lease may satisfy the Companies Office and Inland Revenue Department. However, for the ISIC 6201 EMR SaaS business, most New Zealand trading banks reject a virtual-only address for AML/CFT due-diligence purposes, and the Inland Revenue Department generally requires a physical location where records can be inspected. The company should therefore secure a physical registered office under contract and use a separate correspondence address if desired.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue / Te Tari Taake) of New Zealand; online portal: myIR Secure Online Services at ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

A New Zealand-incorporated limited company is automatically issued an IRD number via the Companies Office and Inland Revenue data exchange at incorporation. A foreign non-resident entity that is not incorporated in New Zealand must apply for a non-resident company IRD number through Inland Revenue's prescribed application channel. The application must be supported by certified evidence of the foreign entity's legal existence (certificate of incorporation or equivalent from the home registry), the entity constitution, registered office address, directors and shareholder register, foreign taxpayer identification numbers, and a statement of intended New Zealand activities or remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers. If the entity is GST-registerable, the same IRD number is used to enrol for GST through myIR; a simplified non-resident GST registration option is available for offshore suppliers of remote services.

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in New Zealand in any 12-month period; the same NZD 60,000 threshold applies to non-resident suppliers of remote services to New Zealand resident consumers

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods are zero-rated under section 11(1)(c) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 when the supplier exports them from New Zealand or delivers them to a carrier for transport to a destination outside New Zealand. Services are zero-rated under section 11A of that Act when supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply and the services are not supplied in connection with land or tangible property in New Zealand. For an EMR SaaS product supplied to an overseas medical clinic, zero-rating applies if the recipient is a non-resident business, the SaaS contract identifies an offshore place of use, the service is delivered for use wholly outside New Zealand, and the ordinary place of application of the service is offshore. No statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline exists in New Zealand; exporter records must nevertheless evidence the cross-border nature of the receipt.

  • For physical goods: New Zealand Customs Service export declaration lodged through the Trade Single Window, carrier bill of lading or airway bill, and commercial invoice
  • For services: executed SaaS agreement / order form identifying the non-resident customer and offshore place of supply, service-delivery records showing offshore user access, and bank remittance evidence (SWIFT/MT103 or bank statement) showing settlement from a foreign account

No real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, or anti-fraud digital stamping mandate exists in New Zealand. E-invoicing is voluntary and operated through the Peppol network. GST-registered persons must still issue tax invoices containing the information required by section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and file GST returns using Inland Revenue's myIR portal.

Inland Revenue may audit export zero-rating claims and require the full evidentiary bundle. If a supply is zero-rated without substantiation, or is subsequently diverted or reimported to New Zealand, the supply is recharacterised as standard-rated, output tax is assessed at 15%, and use-of-money interest accrues. Tax shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply: up to 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and 100% for evasion; deliberate falsification of export proof can also lead to prosecution. Time-barred reassessment periods generally do not protect taxpayers in cases of fraud or evasion.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised and open capital account with no general foreign exchange controls. The New Zealand dollar is freely convertible on both current and capital account transactions, and the country accepts Article VIII obligations of the International Monetary Fund, meaning there are no restrictions on making payments and transfers for current international transactions. Banks, registered exchanges, and licensed providers may execute foreign exchange transactions without requiring prior central bank approval, although all residents must retain records that allow the Reserve Bank of New Zealand to compile balance-of-payments statistics under the Foreign Exchange Transactions (Records and Reporting) Regulations 2017. There are no surrender requirements, no rationing regimes, no dual exchange rates, and no approval thresholds for ordinary corporate dividend or profit remittances. The only practical hurdles relate to tax compliance, not exchange control: non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) is generally assessed on outbound dividends, interest, and royalties at treaty-approved rates, and the beneficiary must provide a certificate of residency and applicable tax forms to the paying bank. Additionally, share transfers or equity capital injections are unrestricted, but a foreign direct investment in sensitive land, significant business assets, or fishing quota may require consent under the Overseas Investment Act 2005. For a medical clinic EMR SaaS entity, the ordinary operating and treasury flows face no convertibility, surrender, or profit repatriation barrier beyond ordinary tax documentation and standard bank due diligence.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rule applies in New Zealand. Exporters may retain all export proceeds in foreign currency accounts without mandatory conversion to New Zealand dollars and without a statutory conversion timeline. Resident banks must report aggregate foreign currency receipts and payments for balance-of-payments statistics, but there is no legally prescribed surrender ratio or repatriation deadline.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand - Te Putea Matua

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Conditional and medium-term viability. New Zealand law permits non-bank electronic money institutions and fintech payment providers to operate as Financial Service Providers registered on the Financial Service Providers Register and supervised under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. They can facilitate payment services, digital wallet operations, and cross-border account issuance only if they maintain client funds in a segregated trust account held with a licensed New Zealand bank and do not engage in unauthorised deposit-taking. Offshore fintech EMIs without a New Zealand Financial Service Provider registration, an approved AML/CFT supervision arrangement, and a local banking partner are legally barred from offering onshore payment or currency services to this entity. A reputable regulated EMI, such as a fully licensed New Zealand FSP with access to SWIFT and local clearing infrastructure, can be used as a supplementary settlement rail, but the primary treasury relationship should remain with a Tier-1 bank. Given the low-risk EMR SaaS profile, a fully licensed EMI is feasible, yet lingering legal constraints around deposit-taking, capital sufficiency, and service continuity reduce its viability below that of the domestic commercial banks.

Turnover: An independent certified audit is mandatory under the Financial Reporting Act 2013 and the Companies Act 1993 for: (1) any FMC reporting entity, including an NZX-listed company, an issuer of equity or debt to the public, a managed investment scheme manager, a bank, an insurer, or a non-bank deposit taker; (2) a large company, namely a company whose total consolidated assets at balance date exceed NZ$30,000,000 or whose total consolidated annual revenue exceeds NZ$60,000,000, measured at parent or group level where a parent-subsidiary structure exists; (3) an overseas company carrying on business in New Zealand that meets the same large-company thresholds or would be large in its home jurisdiction; (4) any company that is otherwise not exempt under the Companies Act 1993 and whose shareholders holding at least 5% of the voting rights, or whose board, has validly required an audit; and (5) any company whose sectoral registration or contract terms, such as a health-platform license or financial institution authorization, impose an audit condition. There is no standalone headcount trigger in the New Zealand statutory audit regime, but employee count remains relevant to financial reporting scale assessments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10 million in aggregate cross-border related-party transactions for taxpayers operating within a multinational group with global group revenue exceeding NZD 50 million. A lower NZD 1 million threshold may trigger IRD review for SMEs; all taxpayers must maintain contemporaneous documentation commensurate with the risk.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations but applies OECD permanent establishment principles and a broad General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR). For a medical EMR SaaS provider, the core income-generating activities are software development, data hosting, contract negotiation, and ongoing clinical support. To claim treaty benefits and avoid being recharacterised as a shell, the New Zealand entity must employ managers and developers with authority in New Zealand, maintain a physical office, and make strategic decisions locally.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion via Offshore Revenue Suppression

Statutory sanction: A shortfall penalty of up to 150% under s 141E of the Tax Administration Act 1994, plus imposed interest, and criminal liability under s 143A for fraudulent evasion with imprisonment up to 5 years and a fine up to NZ$50,000.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly enable the evasion may be personally liable for the company's unpaid tax under s 132B of the Tax Administration Act, be ordered to compensate creditors, and face a ban under the Companies Act 1993 for failing to keep proper records. Travel restrictions and blacklisting can be ordered where the director attempts to remove funds from New Zealand while tax debt exceeds NZ$50,000.
Fatal Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Related-Party Mis-Pricing

Statutory sanction: The Commissioner may make an arm's length adjustment and impose a 20% shortfall penalty for an unacceptable tax position under s 141B, or 150% if the taxpayer deliberately mispriced the transaction. Interest applies from the original due date. IRD can also disregard the arrangement under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorise the mispricing without obtaining transfer pricing advice and documentation may be held personally liable for the resulting tax shortfall on a 'knowing participation' basis, face civil claims for reckless trading under s 135 of the Companies Act 1993, and be disqualified from management for up to five years.
Shell Company / Abusive Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The IRD may invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule in s BG1 and recharacterise the company as a non-resident or invalidate the treaty claim, denying all DTA relief. A shortfall penalty of 100% under s 141D may apply. The company may also be struck off the New Zealand register under s 336 of the Companies Act 1993.

Director personal liability: Directors face civil penalties for breach of director duties and may be personally liable to creditors for trading recklessly. Criminal fraud charges under the Crimes Act 1961 may be brought for false statements to the IRD, carrying up to seven years' imprisonment, in addition to personal forfeiture of assets derived from the sham arrangement.
