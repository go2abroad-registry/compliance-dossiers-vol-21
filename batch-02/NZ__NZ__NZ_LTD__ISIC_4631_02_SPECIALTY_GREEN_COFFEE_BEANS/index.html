Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None under New Zealand law. A limited liability company may be incorporated with no issued share capital or with nominal capital as determined by the Constitution. The Companies Office does not require a blocked escrow account or a minimum capital contribution. Section 46 of the Companies Act 1993 permits consideration for shares to be satisfied in cash, property, services, or other lawful consideration, but the constitution may impose restrictions. For banking and AML purposes, the bank may require proof of source of funds and may require a capital deposit only as a commercial decision.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification is effected through the Building Act 2004 Building Warrant of Fitness (BWOF) and a Fire and Emergency New Zealand compliance schedule for fire alarm, sprinkler, and egress systems. If ten or more employees habitually work in the warehouse, an approved fire evacuation scheme must be submitted to Fire and Emergency New Zealand. WorkSafe New Zealand requires a worker engagement and participation system, an initial safety induction, and an emergency response plan under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015. Registered office and storage premises must have a building compliance schedule, and any seismic or structural load issues require a geotechnical assessment before storage racks are loaded.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in New Zealand. A New Zealand limited company is by default taxed at the entity level on its worldwide income at the corporate rate, unless the company is eligible and validly elects to be a Look-Through Company (LTC) under subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, in which case tax attributes pass through to shareholders. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are determined by subpart FH of the Income Tax Act 2007 and do not provide a discretionary entity reclassification election for the company. Separate indirect-tax elections, such as GST grouping, may be available under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period; the same threshold applies to non-resident suppliers of remote services and offshore suppliers of low-value imported goods to New Zealand consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating is available under section 11 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 where goods are supplied in New Zealand and exported by the supplier, the recipient, or an agent of either within 28 days of the time of supply. The exported green coffee beans must not be used or consumed in New Zealand, and the export must be supported by customs and transport evidence showing movement to a final destination outside New Zealand. A properly zero-rated export sale attracts GST at 0%, so the exporter may recover GST input credits on the related importation of the green coffee beans and on local acquisition costs, provided the full export documentation dossier is retained.

  • Approved New Zealand Customs Service export entry (CUSR declaration) lodged through the Trade Single Window (TSW) showing the export of green coffee beans
  • Original or certified true copy of the Bill of Lading, Sea Waybill, or Air Waybill evidencing physical removal of the goods from New Zealand to the overseas destination
  • Commercial invoice, export packing list, and purchase contract or purchase order identifying the non-resident buyer and final destination country
  • Foreign exchange receipt evidence, including SWIFT MT103 or MT202 confirmation, bank statement crediting the NZ company's account, or documentary letter of credit negotiation documents; New Zealand does not issue FIRCs, and such certificates are not part of the NZ statutory export regime
  • Container number and seal number records, where applicable, providing a traceable chain from the New Zealand port to the overseas container terminal

No mandatory real-time fiscal clearance or anti-fraud digital stamping regime currently applies to GST-registered businesses in New Zealand. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and operates through the PEPPOL-based New Zealand e-Invoicing framework. GST returns are filed electronically through myIR on the applicable filing cycle. There is no government API-based pre-clearance of GST invoices before issue.

IRD audit review will compare the time of supply, the export departure date, customs declarations, shipping documents, and the incoming payment trail. If the exporter cannot substantiate physical export within 28 days of the time of supply, the zero rate is reversed and 15% GST output tax is assessed on the transaction; associated input tax credits may be clawed back. Inland Revenue may impose shortfall penalties under Part 9 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, ranging from 20% to 100% depending on the taxpayer's conduct, plus use-of-money interest. False or misleading statements to New Zealand Customs may separately attract penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018. New Zealand has no statutory exchange-control FIRC filing obligation, but a consistent FX repatriation file evidencing foreign currency receipt within 180 days of export is expected as commercial substance evidence and will mitigate transfer-pricing and zero-rating inquiry risk.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully liberalised and freely floating foreign exchange regime. There is no exchange control legislation, no surrender requirement, and no restriction on the purchase, holding, or transfer of foreign currency by residents or non-residents. Under the Reserve Bank Act 1989 and the Exchange Control Act 1953 (largely repealed), the Reserve Bank of New Zealand supervises the banking system and may impose prudential capital flow measures in extreme systemic stress, but in ordinary circumstances both current account and capital account transactions are fully convertible. Companies may repatriate capital, dividends, and profits without prior central bank approval, subject only to standard corporate tax obligations such as non-resident withholding tax on dividends and royalties. Import payments for goods, including specialty green coffee beans, can be settled freely by any licensed bank. No SAFE-style licences, no quota systems, and no balance of payment notification thresholds apply to routine trade transactions. The practical hurdles are limited to bank-level AML/KYC documentation for wire transfers and trade finance, not FX approvals. New Zealand's compliance with FATF standards ensures that financial institutions impose robust verification for cross-border payments, but this does not constrain currency convertibility. Overall, New Zealand is classified as a no-control, high-convertibility jurisdiction, making it highly attractive as a treasury anchor for Asia-Pacific green coffee distribution.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender requirement. Companies may retain 100% of export proceeds in foreign currency accounts or convert to New Zealand dollars at prevailing market rates, with no mandated timeline or ratio. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not enforce any repatriation obligation on trade receipts.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise, Airwallex, and OFX can provide payment initiation and cross-border settlement services in New Zealand if they are registered on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and, where applicable, hold a money remittance licence under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. However, EMIs are not licensed banks and cannot accept deposits or provide commercial credit in the form of loans or overdrafts. For a wholesale green coffee importer establishing a permanent treasury presence, EMIs are viable as supplementary payment corridors for vendor bills and transit expense reimbursements, but they are not legally permitted to hold a company's primary operating capital escrow accounts. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not supervise EMIs as banks, and such entities are not covered by the depositor compensation scheme. Therefore, the Treasury blueprint advises using only Tier-1 domestic commercial banks for the capital escrow account, primary NZD operating account, and documentary credit issuance, while allowing EMIs solely for low-value operational transfers to overseas cupping labs and logistics providers where the counterpart accepts non-bank payout rails.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity and shareholder loans from non-residents must be transferred using SWIFT MT103 wire instructions to the company's NZD capital escrow account at a Tier-1 New Zealand bank. The remittance field must clearly state the purpose as 'Shareholder Equity Subscription' or 'Shareholder Loan' to permit the receiving bank's capital verification process. New Zealand does not impose a specific foreign exchange purpose code, but banks require the settlement instruction to align with the company's registered business activity under ANZSIC code G3399 or the relevant wholesale trade classification. For capital verification, the New Zealand company must provide a statutory declaration from its director, a capital allocation statement signed by the shareholder, and a compliance certificate from a New Zealand Chartered Accountant confirming that the funds originated from a legal banking channel and do not breach the AML/CFT Act. If the entity is designated as a large overseas investment under the Overseas Investment Act 2005, prior consent from the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) may be required, but for a small green coffee import company with no sensitive land or significant business assets, an exemption is typically applicable. The receiving bank will verify each transfer against the beneficial ownership schedule filed in the entity's AML disclosure, and all transfers should be tagged with the company's tax identification number to facilitate later reconciliation by Inland Revenue.Outbound settlement for green coffee purchases will primarily use the SWIFT MT103 system for direct telegraphic transfers to the seller's account, especially for shipments under USD 250,000 or where the exporter requires pre-shipment wiring. For larger or repeated shipments, a commercial documentary letter of credit issued by a New Zealand Tier-1 bank is the most secure clearing rail; the LC will be advised through a nominated bank in the exporting country, with payment matched against presentation of a draft, commercial invoice, insurance certificate, phytosanitary certificate, and full set of clean on-board bills of lading. Documentary collections (D/P and D/A) are also permissible but carry non-performance risk for the importer; they may be used for established supplier relationships in Kenya, Colombia, or Vietnam. All outbound disbursements must be accompanied by the purchase agreement reference number and a declaration that the goods meet the New Zealand Biosecurity Act 1993 import health standards for green coffee beans, including the presence of a valid import permit. The treasury desk will time disbursements against expected coffee arrival dates to minimise the cost of carry, and the bank's trade finance team will maintain an audit trail of each MT103 UETR to satisfy both clearing and anti-money laundering obligations.Plain-vanilla deliverable forward foreign exchange contracts (FX forwards) between NZD and USD, EUR, and major origin-country currencies such as the Kenyan Shilling, Colombian Peso, Ethiopian Birr, and Vietnamese Dong, with terms up to 12 months supported by underlying coffee purchase agreements.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) only for currencies with limited offshore deliverability, including the Vietnamese Dong and Ethiopian Birr, using an agreed onshore reference rate published by a central bank or leading financial data vendor; NDF settlement in NZD or USD with no physical delivery of the restricted currency.Cross-currency basis swaps (CCS) between NZD and USD, limited to shareholder loan hedging and operational refinancing, with a maximum maturity aligned to the inbound loan term and board-approved VaR limits.Currency options (plain call/put) on NZD/USD as a hedge against catastrophic adverse FX moves during large seasonal purchase cycles, provided the bank documentation includes ISDA agreements and the credit committee accepts the counterparty credit risk.Intercompany netting and treasury pooling for internal loans denominated in NZD and USD, executed through the bank's electronic banking platform to reduce settlement exposure and eliminate unnecessary conversions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

30% NRWT on dividend distributions to non-resident shareholders. Fully imputed dividends qualify for a reduced rate of 15%; if a fully imputed dividend is supplemented by a supplementary dividend, the effective NRWT burden can be 15% of the total cash dividend available to the foreign recipient.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10,000,000 of aggregate cross-border related-party transactions in an income year triggers the mandatory preparation and retention of contemporaneous Transfer Pricing Documentation (Master File and Local File). CbC reporting applies to NZ resident groups with consolidated annual group revenue exceeding approximately NZD 1,200,000,000 (equivalent to EUR 750 million). No transfer pricing return (TPR) is separately required in NZ, but the IRD may serve a notice to produce documentation for audit.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a formal Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) code akin to the Cayman Islands or UAE. Nevertheless, the Income Tax Act 2007 contains a general anti-avoidance rule (GAAR) under sections BG 1 and GB 1 that allows IRD to disregard or recharacterise arrangements lacking commercial substance, including shell holding structures that artificially divert coffee trading profits from New Zealand. For treaty purposes, a company is treated as a NZ resident if it is incorporated in New Zealand, or if its centre of administrative and effective management is in New Zealand. Cross-border trading structures should evidence substantive purchasing, warehousing, risk, and management functions in NZ to avoid adverse TP adjustments and residency challenges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Civil shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 range from 20% (lack of reasonable care) to 150% (abusive tax position) of the tax shortfall. UOMI is charged on unpaid tax. Criminal prosecution for tax evasion under section 143A carries a fine of up to NZD 50,000 per offence and imprisonment up to 5 years for individuals; a company may face a fine of up to NZD 250,000 per charge.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who knowingly participate in the evasion can be prosecuted jointly as principals. Where the company is used as a mere façade or sham, the corporate veil may be pierced to impose personal liability for tax debts, and directors may face travel bans, asset restraint orders, and disqualification from holding directorships under the Companies Act 1993.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Base Erosion

Statutory sanction: IRD may adjust assessable income under section GC 8 of the Income Tax Act 2007. Shortfall penalties apply at 40% (lack of reasonable care or not taking reasonable steps) and up to 150% for an abusive tax position. Interest is payable on the resulting tax shortfall, and material false documentation may attract a criminal penalty of imprisonment up to 5 years under the tax evasion provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve transfer pricing positions without reasonable commercial basis and without proper documentation may be liable for penalties for taking an unacceptable tax position. In severe cases, the personal liability of directors arises for restitutionary damages to the group, and directors may be banned from managing companies in NZ / Australia under cross-border director disqualification arrangements.
Shell Company / Artificial Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The GAAR allows IRD to reconstruct the arrangement and attribute income to the person or country where real economic functions occur, with penalties for evasion. The company may lose all treaty benefits under the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) provisions in NZ DTAs, resulting in full NRWT at 30% on dividends and denial of the reduced withholding rates.

Director personal liability: Directors of shell companies are exposed to civil liability to creditors and tax losses. Under the Companies Act 1993, reckless trading liability may be imposed, and directors who sign defective declarations concerning economic substance can be fined NZD 200,000 for false statements.
AML / CFT and Offshore Banking Compliance

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, the Department of Internal Affairs, the Reserve Bank, and the Financial Markets Authority may impose civil penalties up to NZD 1,000,000 for each contravention by a company; criminal penalties can reach NZD 2,000,000. Suspicious transaction reports (STRs) are mandatory where money laundering or terrorism financing is suspected.

Director personal liability: Senior managers and directors who knowingly permit AML breaches incur criminal fines up to NZD 200,000 per offence and imprisonment up to 2 years. The High Court may also impose management exclusion orders and asset confiscation under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.
