Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

The Companies Act 1993 requires every New Zealand company to maintain a registered office in New Zealand at a physical street address where documents can be served and where the office is open to the public during ordinary business hours. A licensed virtual office or commercial postal mailbox at a physical New Zealand street address can satisfy the literal registered-office requirement and will not be rejected by the Companies Office for a low-risk online retail company. However, the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) and New Zealand trading banks will reject an arrangement that lacks a physical operating premise for GST registration, corporate bank account opening, merchant acquiring with Stripe, or Affirm BNPL onboarding because the economic substance test requires a place of business, management premise, bank account records, and inventory or fulfillment arrangements. For ISIC 4791 e-commerce retail, IRD is likely to treat a non-resident-managed company with only a mailbox as a tax shell, re-characterize the permanent establishment risk, or deny GST registration. Therefore the prudent structure is a true serviced office with a managed reception, a New Zealand-resident director and/or office manager, dedicated racking for returns, and local books and records; this materially reduces risk while retaining a virtual administrative front.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand; online portal myIR (myir.ird.govt.nz) and the New Zealand Companies Office (for entity registration).

E-government filing portal

For a foreign non-resident entity establishing a New Zealand limited company, the procedure is: (1) Incorporate the company with the New Zealand Companies Office via the online portal (NZBN registration); the Companies Office will issue a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN). (2) Apply for an IRD number for the company using the IRD application form IR742 ('Non-resident company tax registration') or through myIR, providing certified copies of the certificate of incorporation, the NZBN confirmation, and proof of directors' identities. (3) If the company expects to make taxable supplies exceeding NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period, or intends to claim input tax credits, it must separately register for GST using form IR356 ('GST registration') or via myIR, supplying the same identification data. (4) The IRD will issue an 8-digit IRD number for income tax purposes and a separate GST registration number (the same IRD number with a GST suffix) upon successful registration. The entire process can be completed remotely; no physical presence or local director is mandated if a registered agent is appointed, though the Companies Office requires a local registered office address.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None for generic limited companies under New Zealand law. However, if the entity qualifies as a closely held company (five or fewer shareholders), it may elect look-through company (LTC) status under Subpart HB of the Income Tax Act 2007, which allows shareholders to be taxed on their share of the company's income and losses. This election is made via form IR427 and is an annual irrevocable election. No check-the-box or hybrid mismatch elections are available beyond the LTC regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in a 12-month period (including zero-rated supplies) or NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies expected to be made in the next 12 months; voluntary registration is permitted.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to the supply of goods exported from New Zealand within 28 days of the time of supply, provided the supplier obtains and holds sufficient evidence of export, including a customs export declaration or a transport document (e.g., bill of lading or airway bill). Additionally, the supply of goods to a non-resident who is not in New Zealand at the time of supply, if the goods are removed from New Zealand by the recipient, is zero-rated under section 11(1)(a) of the GST Act 1985. For services, zero-rating applies to services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed (section 11A), except services directly connected with real property in New Zealand or certain personal services physically performed in New Zealand. A portable solar power retail business qualifies for zero-ratings on outbound goods exports, but online digital services (e.g., downloads) supplied to non-residents may also be zero-rated where the recipient's normal place of residence is outside New Zealand.

  • Customs Export Declaration (CusDec) submitted through the New Zealand Trade Single Window (or a copy of the Bill of Entry for the export shipment)
  • Bill of Lading (sea freight) or Airway Bill (air freight) evidencing that the goods left New Zealand
  • Commercial invoice and packing list describing the goods and their destination
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) or bank/Stripe payment records clearly showing that the consideration was received from an offshore account in the name of the non-resident purchaser (or a certificate issued by the receiving New Zealand bank confirming the overseas origin of funds)

New Zealand does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping. E-invoicing is voluntary and based on the Peppol network. Businesses may issue tax invoices in any format, provided they contain the prescribed GST information under section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, including the supplier's name and GST number, the date of supply, the description, and the total amount payable. For GST purposes, buyers cannot claim input tax credits without a valid tax invoice. There are no API-based clearance requirements for standard B2B/B2C sales.

Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and the Tax Administration Act 1994, if zero-rating is applied without sufficient supporting evidence, the supplier is liable for the unpaid GST at 15% plus interest. The Inland Revenue may impose a shortfall penalty of 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or fraudulent intent. If documents are found false or fraudulent, the IRD can disallow the zero-rating retrospectively and assess GST on the original supply date, with backdated interest and penalties. willful evasion may also lead to criminal prosecution, fines of up to NZD 50,000 for individuals (and up to NZD 250,000 for companies), and potential imprisonment. In practice, the IRD scrutinises export records for consistency between the customs declaration, transport documents, and the foreign exchange receipts; any unexplained gap beyond the 28-day export rule triggers a reassessment and clawback.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a widely recognized free-floating exchange rate regime with full convertibility of the New Zealand dollar (NZD) on both the current account and capital account. There are no statutory exchange control restrictions, no prior government approval requirements for cross-border transfers, and no limits on the repatriation of profits, dividends, or invested capital for foreign-owned entities. The Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 and the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 impose compliance obligations but do not restrict currency movement. Companies can maintain foreign currency bank accounts, invoice in multiple currencies, and convert NZD to any foreign currency at market rates without surrender obligations. Profit remittance hurdles are minimal and generally only require standard corporate governance approvals (board resolution) and tax clearance from Inland Revenue to demonstrate that withholding tax obligations have been settled. A commercial bank may request supporting transactional documentation to satisfy its own internal cross-border payment acceptance policy. The absence of surrender requirements and capital controls makes New Zealand a highly efficient jurisdiction for a cross-border e-commerce treasury operation, provided the entity maintains robust AML/KYC files for all counterparties and complies with tax reporting under the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and the Common Reporting Standard (CRS) where applicable.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory requirement. Export proceeds in foreign currency are not required to be surrendered to the Reserve Bank of New Zealand or any domestic bank. The exporter may retain foreign currency indefinitely, convert to NZD at any time, or use funds directly to satisfy overseas operational liabilities. The default conversion ratio is 0% mandatory; however, if a receivable is settled in a foreign currency, the company may voluntarily choose to convert any portion into NZD subject to the bank's normal foreign exchange pricing and transaction due diligence. The only documentary requirement is accurate record-keeping for tax and financial reporting under the Financial Reporting Act 2013.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Putea Matua) acting also as the prudential regulator and financial stability authority; exchange control functions reside with the Reserve Bank of New Zealand under the Banking Act and the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, with no separate foreign exchange administration.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High with legal structuring. Offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g. Stripe, Airwallex, Wise Business) may legally service a New Zealand-registered entity, but they must comply with the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 (FSP Act). To hold customer funds and execute NZD domestically, an EMI must either be registered as a New Zealand financial service provider or operate through a licensed New Zealand commercial bank partner. Stripe uses a third-party NZ bank sponsorship for local NZD collection and payout; Airwallex holds an authorised deposit-taking institution (ADI) status in Australia, which allows them to offer NZD payment services through cross-border partnerships. A pure fintech EMI without FSP registration and AML/CFT compliance cannot legally service New Zealand entities. The viability for a low-risk e-commerce retailer is high when using established EMIs that partner with major NZ banks, but the primary operating account should remain with a Tier-1 domestic bank to ensure reliable clearing, capital verification, and future credit access. The treasury design should use the EMI for customer payment intake (Stripe, Affirm) and the Tier-1 bank as the ultimate settlement hub.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections into NZ_LTD should be routed via SWIFT MT103 with clear reference field 70 offering the purpose of payment code P-ACCESS1 (IFS Direct Investment) or using the New Zealand standard tax category for inbound services. Since New Zealand has no exchange controls, the funds can be remitted in NZD or USD and converted by the receiving bank at floating market rate. The receiving Tier-1 bank may require a completed International Transaction Reporting System (ITRS) form for balance-of-payments statistics. Capital verification is performed by the company's external auditor using the bank's stamped confirmation, the MT103 inbound telegram, and the shareholder resolution. Recommend using a dedicated inbound capital escrow account at the primary Tier-1 bank (e.g. ANZ 'Capital Escrow Account') where funds are held until the Companies Office registry is updated with share allotments. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand applies transfer pricing documentation rules (sections GC 6 – GC 13 of the Income Tax Act 2007) but does not prescribe a specific local currency threshold for mandatory contemporaneous documentation. Inland Revenue's Operational Statement 17/02 requires entities with cross-border related-party transactions to prepare and maintain Transfer Pricing Documentation (Local File, Master File, and Country-by-Country Report if the group consolidates above NZ$1.3 billion) on a self-assessment basis; practical thresholds approximate NZ$1 million for intercompany services/loans and NZ$3 million for goods transactions, below which simplified documentation may be acceptable.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand applies the Economic Substance Requirement through the general anti-avoidance rule (sections BG 1 and GA 1) and specific transfer pricing rules. A non-resident company or a New Zealand company in a multinational group must demonstrate real commercial activity, with strategic decision-making, key assets, and core income-generating activities (CIGA) located in New Zealand for income attribution. The absence of substance triggers a reallocation of profits by the Commissioner under section GC 1(1); shell companies with no local employees or premises risk having their intercompany payments disallowed and profits reconstructed. Relevant CIGA for online retail includes procurement, inventory ownership, pricing algorithms, fulfilment (unless contracted), and customer support.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion – Dishonest Conduct

Statutory sanction: Sections 143A and 143B of the Tax Administration Act 1994: fine up to NZ$50,000 plus unpaid tax; section 143C evasion of assessment imposes additional penalties of up to 200% of the tax evaded; imprisonment up to 5 years (7 years for amounts exceeding NZ$100,000).

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly assist in tax evasion breach the Companies Act 1993 section 135 duties and the Financial Reporting Act; the corporate veil is pierced under section 193 of the Companies Act 1993 for dishonest or fraudulent conduct. Directors face civil liability for the company's unpaid tax only if they have knowingly participated in the evasion and personally benefited; travel bans may be imposed under section 171 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 (taxpayer blacklisting / departure prohibition orders) for unpaid assessments exceeding NZ$20,000, and criminal conviction carries imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance / Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act 2007 section GC 1 – the Commissioner may reconstruct the transaction to reflect the arm's length amount; section GB 2(1B) applies a non-deductibility adjustment and assesses additional tax; late payment penalties and use-of-money interest apply under the Tax Administration Act 1994; shortfall penalties under section 141B range from 20% (not taking reasonable care) to 150% (abusive tax position) of the resulting tax shortfall.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or cause the company to file false transfer pricing documentation commit an offence under section 143H of the Tax Administration Act, punishable by a fine up to NZ$50,000 and imprisonment up to 3 years for wilful obstruction or knowing false statement; the Court may order the director to repay personal benefits received from the profit shifting and may freeze assets. Inland Revenue may issue a departure prohibition order preventing directors from leaving New Zealand while substantial tax liabilities remain outstanding.
Shell Company / Colourable Arrangement

Statutory sanction: General anti-avoidance rule (section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007) invalidates the tax advantage; the Commissioner may disregard the structure and treat the underlying economic substance as being in New Zealand (section GB 1); failure to file accurate CbC reports or master file under the OECD BEPS standards incurs civil penalties up to NZ$20,000 under sections 78EBC and 78EBR of the Tax Administration Act 1994; providing false import/export documentation violates the Customs and Excise Act 2018 (offence under section 369) with penalties of up to NZ$50,000 plus triple the unpaid duty.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable as shadow directors under section 8 of the Companies Act 1993 if the shell arrangement amounts to insolvent trading or fraud. Under the veil-piercing test in section 193, if the company is used for a dishonest purpose, directors can be ordered to pay restitution for all avoided taxes plus interest. The same conduct may constitute a criminal offence of obtaining pecuniary advantage by dishonesty under section 228 of the Crimes Act 1961, punishable by imprisonment of up to 7 years. Directors may also be banned from managing a company for up to 5 years under section 383 of the Companies Act 1993.
