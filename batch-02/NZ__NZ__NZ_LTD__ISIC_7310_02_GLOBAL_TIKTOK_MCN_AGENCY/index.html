Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD), New Zealand; secure digital portal: myIR (myIR Secure Online Services) for company income tax, GST, PAYE, and correspondence.

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must first obtain a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) from the New Zealand Companies Office. It must then apply to Inland Revenue for a New Zealand IRD number using the non-resident company or foreign entity application form (IR742) or through an approved agent via myIR. GST registration is made simultaneously or subsequently through myIR or the standard GST registration form. The application must identify the foreign entity s country of incorporation, directors, ultimate beneficial owners, and a New Zealand registered office or agent for service. There is no minimum capital requirement and non-resident ownership is permitted without prior exchange control approval.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation; a New Zealand resident limited company is taxed at entity level on worldwide income. A non-resident company is subject to New Zealand income tax only on New Zealand-sourced income under Territorial_Scope principles. No Tax_Exempt_Zone presumption applies. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a New Zealand resident limited company is taxed at entity level on worldwide income. A non-resident company is subject to New Zealand income tax only on New Zealand-sourced income under Territorial_Scope principles. No Tax_Exempt_Zone presumption applies. Pass-through treatment is not the default but may be elected in limited close-held circumstances.

Available election forms

Available: a close-held New Zealand company may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status under section HB 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, with IR461 election form and shareholder consent, causing profits and losses to be transparent to shareholders while retaining limited liability. Foreign companies cannot use an elective check-the-box mechanism; instead, classification follows tax treaty and domestic law. Hybrid mismatch rules under Part FM of the Income Tax Act 2007 are mandatory and do not allow elective opt-out. Other available elections include GST cash vs invoice accounting basis, provisional tax payment options, and carried-forward loss continuity elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies supplied in New Zealand during any consecutive 12-month period; remote services supplied to New Zealand consumers by a non-resident supplier also cross the NZD 60,000 registration threshold. Voluntary GST registration is permitted below the threshold. The threshold is calculated on GST-exclusive taxable turnover and includes zero-rated supplies for determining liability, though exports are subsequently returned at 0%.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating at 0% GST is available for exported supplies under sections 11 and 11A of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. For goods, the goods must be physically exported from New Zealand either before the time of supply or within the statutory permitted period after supply, generally 28 days. For services, including creator agency and MCN representation services supplied to a non-resident TikTok entity, the service must be supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the service is performed, and the service must not be directly connected with New Zealand land, goods in New Zealand, or a New Zealand-resident recipient. A supplier must hold sufficient commercial and customs documentation to prove the offshore destination and the non-resident character of the recipient.

  • Customs Export Declaration (filed through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window / list of export entry details)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill issued by the exporting carrier
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank receipt confirming receipt of foreign exchange payment from outside New Zealand
  • Commercial Invoice including full description of goods or services
  • Packing List for exported goods
  • Evidence of export timing, such as carrier proof of departure and terminal receiving records
  • Service agreement or creator representation contract evidencing the non-resident recipient and place of performance

New Zealand has no real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or continuous transaction control mandate. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and operates through the PEPPOL network using accredited access points. New Zealand government agencies progressively accept PEPPOL e-invoices. Inland Revenue requires electronic filing of GST returns through myIR or an approved tax agent gateway, but there is no transaction-level API reporting mandate. Tax invoices must include the supplier s NZBN, date, description, GST-exclusive amount, GST amount, and GST rate; the invoice must be issued within 28 days of supply.

Inland Revenue scrutinises export zero-rating claims closely, particularly for cross-border service transactions where intangible benefit may arise in New Zealand. Where documentation is incomplete, false, or insufficient, the zero-rating is reversed and output GST is assessed at 15% on the original consideration. Retrospective clawback includes disallowed input credits, additional GST, use-of-money interest from the original due date, and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994. Penalties range from 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for failure to exercise reasonable care, and 150% for an abusive tax position. Fraudulent export zero-rating claims, forged customs documents, or deliberate wire-transfer manipulation are criminal offences subject to fines and imprisonment. Directors and shareholder agents may be personally liable for arrangements designed to defeat GST assessment or recovery.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand is an IMF Article VIII jurisdiction with full current-account and capital-account convertibility. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely floating; there are no exchange controls, no central-bank approval requirements for peacetime remittances, no dividend-repatriation licensing, and no restrictions on holding foreign-currency accounts domestically. For a non-resident-owned NZ Ltd, the only remaining practical hurdles are (i) the bank's AML/CFT documentation requirements for inbound capital, and (ii) the New Zealand non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on dividends (25% default, up to 30%; treaty-reduced as applicable) and, where relevant, Approved Issuer Levy (AIL) at 2% on interest paid to offshore lenders.

Export proceeds surrender rules

New Zealand abolished exchange controls in 1984; no statutory surrender timeline, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no repatriation mandate exists for export proceeds. Exporters may retain receipts indefinitely in foreign currency, deposit them offshore, or convert to NZD at market rates. The sole binding obligations arise from the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act), which requires banks and reporting entities to verify cross-border wire-transfers, maintain transaction records, and report suspicious activity; the $10,000 NZD threshold for routine transactional reports is the de facto statutory touchpoint.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) – prudential regulator and monetary policy authority; the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) administers foreign-direct-investment screening under the Overseas Investment Act 2005. New Zealand has no dedicated foreign-exchange-control administration and imposes no exchange-control licensing regime.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

High feasibility as a supplementary low-value payout rail for creator remittances (Wise Business B2B, Payoneer, Airwallex), but Low-to-Medium viability as primary treasury or capital-escrow infrastructure. Offshore digital EMIs are not registered banks in New Zealand, are not licensed to accept deposits under the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 1989, and do not carry NZ deposit-insurance protection; they are typically registered as Financial Service Providers (FSPs) and as 'reporting entities' under the AML/CFT Act. Recommended architecture: tier-1 NZ bank for capital custody, escrow, and settlement finality; digital EMIs for high-frequency, low-value creator payouts where FX all-in cost is materially lower.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Equity injection protocol: SWIFT MT103 from the parent or beneficial owner's bank to the NZ Ltd account, using Field 70 remittance text exactly as 'SHARE SUBSCRIPTION / ADDITIONAL PAID-IN CAPITAL / NZ COMPANIES OFFICE ALLOTMENT [DATE]', supported by a signed NZ Companies Office share-allotment resolution and the bank's own source-of-funds and source-of-wealth questionnaires. Debt-injection protocol: director's loan or intercompany loan via SWIFT MT103 with Field 70 referencing 'INTERCOMPANY LOAN AGREEMENT DATED [DATE]', supported by a fully executed written loan agreement, an interest-rate and AIL analysis, and a board resolution authorising indebtedness. The inbound wire must never be structured into split tranches below the $10,000 reportable threshold; the bank may require a funds-flow statement evidencing the end-to-end sender-to-beneficiary chain and confirmation that no third-party payment intermediaries obscure the economic substance of the contribution.Clearing rails and selection criteria: (1) SWIFT MT103 bank-grade wire transfers for high-value creator payouts and supplier settlements, with precise purpose-code mapping prescribed by the receiving bank's country; (2) Wise Business B2B API for multi-currency, low-value creator payouts (AUD, USD, GBP, EUR, THB, IDR, PHP, MYR, JPY, KRW) with transparent FX mid-market conversion; (3) Commercial Letters of Credit governed by UCP 600 where the MCN procures physical media-production inventory, merchandise, or advertising inventory from overseas vendors; (4) Global Mastercard/Visa commercial cards for incidental digital-ad-spend and SaaS subscriptions on a no-foreign-transaction-fee basis where available; (5) Local real-time rails (BECS in NZ, SEPA Instant in EU, Fedwire/CHIPS in US, and CHAPS in UK) only where latency and cost analytics justify their use over SWIFT.Deliverable outright FX forwards (FXA) in NZD against AUD, USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, and CNY for forecast creator-revenue conversion, subject to bank-credit two-way CSA documentationNon-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) into THB, IDR, VND, PHP, CNY as applicable for currencies without full onshore deliverability, governed by ISDA / NDF Master AgreementsCross-Currency Swaps (CCS) and FX swaps between NZD and the relevant payout currencies for long-dated creator-fee liabilities and match-funded escrow obligationsEuropean-style FX options (zero-premium collars and participating forwards) for prudent hedging of material profit-repatriation tranches

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Statutory NRWT on dividends is 30% (non-resident withholding tax) under s 43-44 Income Tax Act 2007. The imputation credit 'fully imputed' dividend regime reduces effective economic burden for NZ-resident shareholders, but non-resident shareholders generally bear NRWT at 30% unless treaty relief reduces the rate. For a fully imputed dividend, the ratio of imputation credit to cash dividend is 28/72, and NRWT applies to the dividend grossed-up? Caution: NRWT applies at 30% of the cash dividend plus any imputation credits attached (i.e., gross dividend), but for non-resident investors, the typical practical rate is 30% of the cash distribution if no imputation credits are attached. Treaty rate under NZ-United States DTA is 15% (or 5% for 10% corporate shareholders); NZ-China DTA is 15% (or 5% for shareholding of at least 25%); NZ-Australia DTA is 15% (or 5% for 10% shareholder); NZ-Singapore DTA is 15% (or 5% for 25% shareholder).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand transfer pricing documentation obligations apply under s GC 1-GC 18 and Tax Administration Act 1994 s 78CA-78DD. Formal documentation (contemporaneous TP documentation) is not mandatory for all taxpayers, but must be prepared if the taxpayer has cross-border related-party transactions and the transactions are not at arm's length. Inland Revenue's 'transfer pricing guidelines' (TPG 2018) require a taxpayer to document the transfer pricing analysis if the aggregate value of cross-border related-party transactions exceeds NZD 1 million for 'low-risk' taxpayers or if the transactions involve 'significant' value. For the Local File / Master File: New Zealand does not implement the OECD's three-tier standard as a statutory filing requirement, but as a member of the Inclusive Framework it follows the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines. Inland Revenue may request a TP document within 30 days. The NZD threshold for mandatory transfer pricing documentation in the IRD's Operational Statement 18/04 is: (i) cross-border related-party transactions exceeding NZD 1 million for goods/services, or (ii) intra-group services exceeding NZD 1 million, or (iii) any transaction involving a 'high-risk' category (e.g., intangibles, complex financing) regardless of amount. However, the legislated 'transfer pricing record-keeping' threshold under s 78CA is NZD 10 million of cross-border revenue for non-resident-owned or controlled entities, or NZD 10 million of connected-party transactions; below this, limited record-keeping obligations apply. The effective documentation threshold for Local/Master File compliance is NZD 10 million of related-party revenue or assets, consistent with NZ's implementation of country-by-country reporting (CbCR) for MNE groups with consolidated revenue over EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not have a standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime akin to the UAE or BVI. Instead, substance requirements are embedded in: (i) the corporate residence test under s YD 2 (incorporation in NZ or centre of management), (ii) thin capitalisation rules under s FE 4-FE 28, (iii) anti-avoidance general rule (GAAR) under s BG 1, and (iv) the transfer pricing rules. For a TikTok MCN agency, economic substance is evidenced by the physical presence of directors and staff in New Zealand, execution of management decisions in NZ, active negotiation and monitoring of creator contracts, and maintenance of an office and bank accounts. The IRD may deny treaty benefits or recharacterise arrangements lacking economic substance under the tax avoidance provisions. For AML purposes, the entity must maintain a 'place of business' and comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 obligations, including a documented risk assessment and compliance programme.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Under ss 141A-141GB Tax Administration Act 1994: evasion or similar offence attracts a civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax; knowledge-based evasion can be prosecuted criminally under ss 143A and 143B, with a maximum fine of NZD 50,000 for individuals and NZD 250,000 for companies, plus imprisonment of up to 5 years. Further, under s 143A, the court may order repayment of unpaid tax plus interest. Under the Crimes Act 1961, obtaining a tax advantage by deception may constitute a crime punishable by up to 7 years' imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for civil penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 149A-149B for arranging a company to fail to pay tax or for knowingly participating in tax evasion. Under the Companies Act 1993 s 135-138, directors who recklessly trade while insolvent or cause a loss to the Inland Revenue by incurring obligations without reasonable grounds may be personally liable for the amount of tax debt. The IRD can issue a director penalty notice (DPN) requiring personal payment of unpaid PAYE, GST, or other withheld taxes. Travel bans can be imposed under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 203 (civil detention) for individuals who fail to pay tax debts and attempt to leave New Zealand. Bankruptcy and disqualification from managing companies may also apply.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Under s GC 1-GC 18 Income Tax Act 2007, Inland Revenue may adjust the taxable income by applying the arm's-length price, resulting in additional tax, plus use-of-money interest. Shortfall penalties for transfer pricing misstatements: 20% for not taking reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. The absence of TP documentation shifts the burden of proof to the taxpayer to demonstrate that the pricing was arm's length (s 138Q Tax Administration Act 1994). CbCR non-compliance incurs a penalty of NZD 20,000 under the Tax Administration Act.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign defective TP documentation or who cause the company to enter into artificial arrangements may be personally liable under s 149A. In extreme cases, directors can be held liable as 'tax agents' under s 34C. Criminal liability may arise if false documents are provided to the IRD, leading to imprisonment up to 5 years. Insurance or indemnity from the company is void for tax penalties.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance (GAAR and Anti-Treaty Abuse)

Statutory sanction: The general anti-avoidance rule under s BG 1 Income Tax Act 2007 empowers the Commissioner to void an arrangement and reconstruct the tax position. Specific anti-conduit rules (s GB 39-48) deny treaty benefits and NRWT exemptions. A finding can result in full reassessment of income tax, withholding tax, penalties (up to 100% abusive tax position penalty), and interest. The company may also be struck off the New Zealand Register under the Companies Act 1993 s 318 if it has no proper purpose or is an agent of fraud; striking-off results in asset forfeiture to the Crown as bona vacantia, leaving principals without recourse.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell company face personal liability for taxes owed if the company fails to meet its obligations and the director allowed the company to carry on business in a manner likely to create a serious risk of loss to creditors, including Inland Revenue (Companies Act 1993 s 135). Breach of director duties can lead to compensation orders, disqualification for up to 10 years, and criminal sanctions under s 373. For AML non-compliance related to shell structures, directors face fines up to NZD 200,000 and imprisonment up to 2 years under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009.
GST and Withholding Tax Compliance Failure (Cross-Border Digital Payments)

Statutory sanction: Under s 141A-141GB Tax Administration Act 1994, failure to withhold NRWT results in liability for the amount of NRWT plus interest and a shortfall penalty of 20% (unacceptable tax position) to 150% (evasion). GST registration failure attracts a late registration penalty of NZD 250 and a 10% late payment penalty on any GST due; repeated failure can lead to assessment based on estimated supplies and a pecuniary penalty of up to NZD 15,000 for the company. Under s 142, an additional penalty of 100% of the unpaid amount can be imposed. The Commissioner may also prosecute for failing to furnish returns under s 143A (maximum NZD 50,000 for individuals / NZD 250,000 for companies).

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable under s 149A-149B for the company's failure to deduct or pay withholding taxes and GST if the director knew or ought to have known of the failure. The 'director penalty' regime allows the IRD to recover the unpaid amounts from directors personally without requiring liquidation or demand against the company. Directors can be placed on a stop-list for departure through New Zealand border control (under the Tax Administration Act 1994 s 203). Persistent non-compliance may lead to imprisonment of up to 5 years. Additionally, for AML/CFT breaches, directors may be disqualified and held criminally liable with imprisonment up to 2 years.
