Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (Inland Revenue Te Tari Taake), New Zealand; official portal: myIR Secure Online Services via www.ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

A non-resident or foreign-incorporated entity carrying on taxable activity in New Zealand must apply for an IRD number using Form IR742 (Non-Resident Company IRD Number Application), providing certified certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents, director and ultimate shareholders details, registered office or New Zealand address for service, and evidence of the New Zealand business or investment nexus. If the entity is registered under the Companies Act 1993, the NZ Companies Office issues the NZBN and IRD number is obtained through an IRD registration application; thereafter, GST registration is made separately or as part of the combined Tax Registration application. In all cases, applicant identity verification, beneficial ownership disclosure, and compliance with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 are required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; NZ_LTD is a standard New Zealand limited company taxed at entity level on its worldwide income if resident, and subject to ordinary corporate income tax rules without general pass-through. Overseas direct-operated activities are separately taxed under foreign branch or controlled foreign company rules. Non-resident limited companies are taxed only on New Zealand-sourced income, with no deemed remittance of offshore income.

Available election forms

New Zealand has no domestic check-the-box election. A standard NZ limited company may not elect pass-through status; only qualifying closely-held entities may use Look-Through Company status under the Tax Administration Act 1994, which is unavailable to NZ_LTD as a standard ordinary company. Available elections include GST group registration and branch election under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, Commissioner-approved GST accounting basis options (payments, invoice, hybrid), and loss carry-forward / imputation credit election under compliance rules. Hybrid mismatch rules operate by statute under Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018; no elective hybrid-neutralizing mechanism exists.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 taxable supplies in any 12-month period, or NZD 60,000 expected taxable supplies in the next 12 months where the entity carries on or intends to carry on a taxable activity; NZD 120,000 projected first-12-month threshold may apply to new registrants depending on IRD practice.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 to: (i) goods exported by the supplier to a place outside New Zealand, provided goods leave New Zealand within 28 days and the supplier has sufficient documentary evidence of export; (ii) services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed, provided the services are not directly connected with real property or tangible personal property in New Zealand, and are not supplied in connection with a taxable activity carried on by the non-resident in New Zealand; (iii) certain offshore services such as export freight, transport, and intellectual property supplied for use outside New Zealand, subject to specific statutory exceptions.

  • Customs Export Declaration (NZ Customs Trade Single Window / Electronic Export Declaration)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical departure from New Zealand
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank payment advice showing settlement of the export invoice
  • Signed commercial sales contract or overseas purchase order
  • Packing list and overseas port/warehouse delivery records

No real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory digital stamping regime. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and operates via Peppol BIS 4.0 through IRD-approved e-invoicing providers; GST invoices must be issued in a searchable preserving format, with IRD accepting PEPPOL e-invoices, EDI, PDF, and paper invoices. Invoice completion and document retention obligations are set out in section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and Tax Administration Act 1994.

Inland Revenue strictly scrutinizes zero-rated export claims; if documentation is insufficient, the supply is recharacterized as standard-rated and GST becomes payable, with use-of-money interest calculated from the original tax return date. Shortfall penalties range from 20% for lack of reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or an arrangement designed to defeat the intent of GST zero-rating provisions. Criminal prosecution is possible under the Tax Administration Act 1994, with fines up to NZD 50,000 and imprisonment for wilful evasion; retroactive GST assessments and recovery of refunds are not time-barred where fraud or evasion is established.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible. New Zealand operates an open current account and capital account with no general exchange-control regime. There are no restrictions on inward or outward remittances for equity capital, shareholder loans, dividends, or royalties. Profit repatriation is subject solely to standard tax compliance, principally non-resident withholding tax on dividends and interest, and to AML/CFT verification under the AML/CFT Act 2009. The only material constraint is the Overseas Investment Act 2005 screening process where the investment involves sensitive land, significant business assets (asset value exceeding NZD 150 million), or fishing quota, which is not expected to apply on its face to a standard restaurant operating chain.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no statutory export-proceeds surrender rules in New Zealand. Exporters and trading entities may retain foreign currency proceeds freely, hold foreign-currency settlement accounts domestically or offshore, and have no mandatory conversion ratio or repatriation timeline. The only obligations are declarative and record-keeping: cross-border physical currency movements of NZD 10,000 or more (or equivalent) must be reported under the Financial Transactions Reporting Act 2022, and underlying export/import transaction documentation must be preserved to satisfy Inland Revenue and AML/CFT audit requirements.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua), operating in conjunction with the Overseas Investment Office (OIO) for foreign-investment screening under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 and with the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) for Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) supervision of reporting entities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium to Low feasibility for pure offshore digital EMIs. New Zealand does not license offshore fintech firms as deposit-taking banks; the Banking Prudential Supervision Act 1989 reserves deposit-taking activities for registered banks. Offshore EMIs may operate only through a locally registered financial service provider under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008, become reporting entities under the AML/CFT Act 2009, and maintain a partnership with at least one Tier 1 domestic bank for segregated escrow settlement competently. A bank-BaaS (Banking-as-a-Service) arrangement with ANZ, ASB, BNZ, or Westpac is the most viable submission path for cross-border payment volumes and POS/merchant-acquiring settlement.

Strike-off trigger

Mandatory Audit Threshold Mandatory Audit for All Entities

Turnover: Mandatory independent audit applies if a NZ limited company is not classified as 'small'; a company is 'small' in a financial year if both total assets are NZ$5 million or less and total revenue is NZ$10 million or less. Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The ordinary New Zealand non-resident withholding tax rate on dividends is generally 30%. Treaty relief commonly reduces the rate to 15%, subject to the applicable double tax agreement and beneficial-ownership requirements. Fully imputed dividends may qualify for a domestic reduction or exemption from non-resident withholding tax in specified circumstances. Resident withholding tax on dividends is generally 33%, subject to imputation credits and statutory exceptions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand has no general monetary threshold that automatically triggers a mandatory Local File or Master File for every taxpayer. Section 22 of the Tax Administration Act 1994 requires sufficient records to establish arm's-length pricing. Inland Revenue expects documentation proportionate to the size and complexity of the transactions. A New Zealand entity belonging to an MNE group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million may have Country-by-Country Reporting obligations, including notification and, where applicable, submission of a CbC report. OECD-style Local File and Master File documentation is strongly advisable for material cross-border related-party dealings even where no formal local monetary threshold applies.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a single, standalone Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to the regimes of certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under transfer pricing, controlled foreign company rules, the general anti-avoidance rule, thin-capitalisation rules, beneficial-ownership analysis, financial-arrangement rules, and treaty interpretation. A New Zealand company should maintain genuine local commercial activity, appropriate directors and governance, operational records, personnel or outsourced operating capacity, local banking and accounting records, and evidence that related-party transactions reflect actual functions, assets, risks, and commercial benefit.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax returns

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalties may reach 150% of the tax shortfall for evasion. Criminal conviction may result in imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to NZD 50,000 for a first relevant offense, with higher statutory fines for subsequent offenses, together with payment of tax, interest, and restitution.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal criminal liability where they knowingly participate in, authorize, procure, or aid the conduct. The company generally remains a separate legal person, but personal liability can arise under tax, criminal, insolvency, employment, and aiding-and-abetting provisions. Imprisonment is possible after conviction. Travel restrictions are not automatic and require a specific court or enforcement basis.
GST fraud and cash-register suppression

Statutory sanction: GST reassessment, repayment of refunds, use-of-money interest, shortfall penalties up to 150%, civil recovery, and possible criminal prosecution under tax administration legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for knowing involvement and may become liable for penalties, fines, restitution, or insolvency-related claims. A director disqualification order may be possible in serious cases.
PAYE, payroll, and employment-tax default

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid deductions, late-payment penalties, use-of-money interest, shortfall penalties, employment-law penalties, and possible criminal sanctions for deliberate conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable where they knowingly participate in non-remittance or breach duties during insolvency. Payroll withholding amounts must not be treated as available working capital.
Transfer pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may substitute an arm's-length result, deny or reduce deductions, impose tax shortfall penalties, charge interest, and apply anti-avoidance rules. Significant cross-border arrangements may also create withholding-tax and reporting exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal consequences for deliberate tax fraud or reckless conduct, but ordinary transfer pricing adjustments normally remain company liabilities unless a separate personal-liability basis exists.
Thin capitalisation and excessive related-party debt

Statutory sanction: Interest deductions may be denied or limited, transfer pricing adjustments may apply, and interest withholding tax and shortfall penalties may arise. New Zealand thin-capitalisation rules generally use a 60% debt-to-asset safe-harbour ratio, subject to detailed statutory calculations and exceptions.

Director personal liability: Personal liability is not automatic, but directors may be exposed for knowing tax fraud, unlawful distributions, reckless trading, or breach of duties in an insolvency context.
Treaty abuse and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief may be denied, domestic withholding tax may be reassessed, penalties and interest may apply, and anti-avoidance provisions may be invoked. Unpaid withholding tax can be recovered from the payer.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal criminal or civil exposure if they knowingly submit false declarations or intentionally facilitate withholding-tax evasion.
Shell company and economic-substance deficiency

Statutory sanction: Transactions may be recharacterized or disregarded under transfer pricing and general anti-avoidance rules. Tax, penalties, interest, withholding tax, and reporting obligations may be imposed based on the substantive arrangement.

Director personal liability: Directors may face claims for breach of duty, reckless trading, misleading conduct, or participation in tax offenses. Corporate separateness is not a defense to personal wrongdoing.
AML, proceeds of crime, and unexplained cash

Statutory sanction: Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime offenses can result in asset restraint or forfeiture, substantial fines, and imprisonment. AML breaches may also cause regulatory enforcement, transaction restrictions, and reporting consequences.

Director personal liability: Individuals involved in laundering or knowingly facilitating it may face imprisonment, fines, confiscation, and management or directorship consequences. Travel restrictions may be imposed by a court or enforcement authority where legally available.
Insolvent trading and unlawful distributions

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unlawful distributions, compensation orders, liquidation, voidable transaction proceedings, creditor claims, and director disqualification. Tax debts remain enforceable against the company.

Director personal liability: Directors may be ordered to compensate the company or creditors and may be prohibited from managing companies. Imprisonment is not the ordinary consequence of a civil breach but may arise where fraud or a criminal offense is established.
Unlicensed restaurant operations and regulatory non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Improvement notices, suspension or cancellation of registration, infringement fines, prosecution, closure orders, and civil liability for unsafe products or consumer harm.

Director personal liability: Directors and managers may be personally prosecuted where they consent to, direct, or materially contribute to the breach. Immigration or travel consequences are fact-specific and are not automatic.
