Under Companies Act 1993, section 189, a New Zealand company must have a registered office that is a physical location in New Zealand, open to the public during business hours, and cannot be a P.O. Box. A virtual office service with a real street address and staff accepting service may satisfy strict legal registration, but Inland Revenue and AML-reporting banks generally reject a mailbox-only or corporate-agent-only address for this industry because it does not evidence commercial substance. Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. The Companies Act 1993 imposes no minimum paid-up capital requirement. A company can be incorporated with one share and no value fully issued and unpaid. The directors must maintain solvency and satisfy any contractual lender capital requirements; no blocked escrow account is mandated.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (New Zealand tax authority) and its myIR online portal; New Zealand Companies Office for NZBN/company registration; New Zealand Customs Service for export and border control.

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity seeking a New Zealand Tax Identification Number (IRD number) must first determine whether it is required to register with the New Zealand Companies Office under the Companies Act 1993. For a non-resident entity carrying on a taxable activity in New Zealand, the steps are: (1) register for a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) where required; (2) submit the IRD non-resident registration application securely through myIR, attaching the certified overseas incorporation certificate, constitutional documents, foreign tax identification number, ultimate beneficial ownership schedule, director and secretary details, and either a New Zealand address for service or a New Zealand resident agent appointment; (3) obtain the IRD number from Inland Revenue, which is the New Zealand TIN and is also used as the GST registration number; (4) register for GST through myIR when taxable supplies made in New Zealand exceed or are expected to exceed NZD 60,000 within 12 months; (5) appoint tax representatives and file any required non-resident withholding tax registrations; and (6) retain the IRD confirmation notice and all myIR acknowledgements as conclusive registration evidence.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - a standard New Zealand limited company is a separate taxable legal entity subject to New Zealand corporate income tax at 28%; it is not a pass-through entity in default form, but shareholder dividends may impute corporate tax credits.

Available election forms

No U.S.-style check-the-box election exists. Statutory elections available include the look-through company (LTC) election, tax consolidation election, and imputation group election; hybrid mismatch rules are applied under New Zealand domestic law with no elective opt-out.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in New Zealand in the past 12 months or reasonably expected in the next 12 months; compulsory GST registration applies above this threshold, with voluntary registration available below it.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under sections 11 and 11A of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, supplies of goods exported from New Zealand in the course of a taxable activity are zero-rated if the supplier holds satisfactory evidence of export. Services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand are zero-rated if the services are not supplied in connection with New Zealand land or goods. For intermodal ISO shipping container leasing, cross-border leases of containers to non-resident shipping operators for use outside New Zealand can be zero-rated as exported goods or as offshore services to non-residents, while container leases for use within New Zealand are standard-rated at 15%. Zero-rated exporters claim a GST refund of attributable input tax through the myIR GST return; no separate refund form is required.

  • New Zealand Customs Service electronic export declaration acknowledged through the New Zealand Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Sea Waybill showing container number, vessel, port of loading, port of discharge, and actual export date
  • Detailed commercial invoice and packing list describing the lessee, container identification, lease term, and lease/export proceeds
  • Foreign exchange receipt evidence, such as bank SWIFT credit advice, telegraphic transfer, foreign currency account statement, or overseas payment record showing receipt of proceeds from outside New Zealand

No statutory real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or mandatory e-invoicing. GST is self-assessed and returns are filed through myIR; registered persons must issue compliant GST tax invoices, retain records, and may voluntarily use PEPPOL BIS Billing 3.0 e-invoicing.

Inland Revenue may audit the entire export transaction, comparing the GST return, Customs export declarations, and bank receipts. If the zero-rating evidence package is missing, late, or inconsistent, the supply is reclassified as taxable at 15% GST and Inland Revenue may assess the unpaid GST, use-of-money interest, and shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 ranging from 20% for failure to take reasonable care up to 150% for evasion. False export declarations may also trigger penalties under the Customs and Excise Act 2018 and criminal liability under the Crimes Act 1961. Since New Zealand imposes no statutory 180-day foreign exchange repatriation deadline, the 180-day field is applied as the group's maximum evidence-package completion standard, after which zero-rating is no longer supported and records must be retained for seven years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand maintains a fully convertible, floating New Zealand dollar and does not impose exchange controls on current or capital account transactions. The country has no surrender requirements for export receipts, no central bank conversion approval, and no restriction on the opening of foreign currency accounts by resident companies. Current account payments for leasing, charter hire, royalties, dividends, and service fees are freely executed by commercial banks once AML/CFT due diligence, tax withholding documentation, and a legitimate payment narrative are provided. Capital account flows, including equity injections, shareholder loans, acquisition of foreign assets, and repatriation of capital, are also free from RBNZ approval; investments that trigger sensitive-asset thresholds under the Overseas Investment Act need separate consent from the Overseas Investment Office inside MBIE. Profit remittance is not blocked, but New Zealand non-resident withholding tax and, for related parties, transfer pricing documentation must be handled. Banks must report suspicious transactions and large cash movements under the AML/CFT Act, but that is a supervisory reporting obligation rather than an exchange-control gate. Overall, the regime is best described as open, investor-friendly, and compatible with normal treasury liquidity planning.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio applies: New Zealand has no export surrender rule. Earners of foreign currency from leasing or any other export can lawfully hold 100% of proceeds in an onshore or offshore foreign currency account for an indefinite period, use them directly for foreign payables, or convert them at the market rate. The only practical controls are bank-level AML/CFT monitoring and the cross-border cash reporting threshold of NZD 10,000 for physical cash declarations under the Customs and Excise Act 2018.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) is the central bank and prudential supervisor under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 and the Banking (Prudential Supervision) Act 2010; there is no separate foreign exchange control authority and RBNZ does not administer exchange-control permits or conversion licences.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Offshore fintech EMIs are not deposit-taking banks in New Zealand and cannot issue letters of credit or act as the principal escrow agent for capital injection where a New Zealand bank source is required. A foreign EMI may be registered as a money service business or financial service provider under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and must carry AML/CFT obligations. From the treasurer's seat, the legal viability is medium-low: acceptable for non-critical payment rail or topping-up USD settlement with Wise/Airwallex-style platforms, but not for long-term liquidity clearing, LC issuance, or capital verification reports, all of which require a prudentially supervised New Zealand registered bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The primary rail is an institutional SWIFT MT103 from the parent or shareholder bank to the NZD account of NZ Ltd. The remittance must identify the beneficiary company's NZ Companies Office number and the purpose statement 'Share subscription' or 'Paid-in capital - container fleet acquisition'. The bank may request the tax registration number, source of funds evidence, share allotment resolution, and board meeting minutes. After receipt, the company's chartered accountant issues a capital contribution confirmation, and the auditor can verify the bank statement against the share register. If the same transaction constitutes foreign investment in a sensitive sector, clearances under the Overseas Investment Act must be obtained before settlement. For foreign currency capital, a MT103 in USD or EUR can be credited to an onshore NZD account via an FX spot at the bank's treasury desk; alternatively, keep a USD sub-account and convert only the amount needed for local operating expenses.For trade settlements the following clearing rails are available: (1) SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfer, including SWIFT gpi, for lease security deposits, rental disbursements, and vendor payments below the LC threshold; (2) commercial letters of credit opened via SWIFT MT700 and advised by the beneficiary's bank, used when purchasing containers or fleet assets from overseas vendors; (3) documentary collections on D/P or D/A terms for lessees with acceptable country and sovereign risk; and (4) domestic NZD high-value clearing through Payments NZ HVCS for New Zealand counterparties. From the treasurer's seat, the legal viability is medium-low: acceptable for non-critical payment rail or topping-up USD settlement with Wise/Airwallex-style platforms, but not for long-term liquidity clearing, LC issuance, or capital verification reports, all of which require a prudentially supervised New Zealand registered bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10,000,000 in cross-border related-party transactions within the New Zealand entity triggers mandatory Local File preparation; a Master File is required where the multinational group has consolidated revenue exceeding NZD 1,100,000,000 and the New Zealand entity is a participant in the group.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the Income Tax Act 2007 general anti-avoidance rules (s BG 1 and s GB 1) deny any tax advantage where an arrangement lacks commercial substance or has an avoidance purpose. For the container leasing entity, Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) should be located in New Zealand: acquisition and financing of the ISO container fleet, negotiation of lease terms, fleet utilisation management, maintenance scheduling, invoicing, credit control, and final acceptance of commercial risk. A structure that is a mere mailbox with no New Zealand staff or premises may be reconstructed and DTA benefits denied.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Returns

Statutory sanction: Under the Tax Administration Act 1994, ss 141A-141C, a shortfall penalty of up to 150% of the tax shortfall may be imposed for evasion or an arrangement with a dominant purpose of tax avoidance. Criminal prosecution under s 143A carries a fine up to NZD 50,000 and/or imprisonment up to 5 years. IRD may also issue a late filing penalty of NZD 250 and a 10% additional penalty on the unpaid tax.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced when directors knowingly participate in the evasion. Directors may be personally liable for restitution of the diverted leasing revenue, subject to IRD departure prohibition orders and travel bans, and disqualified from managing companies under the Companies Act 1994. A director who signs a false return can be imprisoned for up to 5 years as a party to the offence.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalisation Failure

Statutory sanction: IRD may invoke Income Tax Act 2007 s GC 7 to substitute an arm's length consideration and disallow excessive interest under the thin-capitalisation rules in subpart FE. The associated shortfall penalty for an unacceptable tax position under TAA s 141B is up to 100% of the tax shortfall where reasonable care has not been exercised; if avoidance is the dominant purpose, the penalty escalates to 150%. Late payment penalties accrue at 1% per month.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly direct or implement the transfer-pricing scheme can be sued for the tax shortfall as accessaries. Civil orders may compel them to repay diverted intercompany lease profits, restore assets to the New Zealand company, and produce banking or wire transfer records. Repeated failure makes directors personally liable for corporate tax debts where they have knowingly allowed the company to trade while insolvent through artificial offshore fees.
AML/CFT and Shell Company Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, failures to undertake customer due diligence or to report suspicious transactions can expose the company to penalties up to NZD 2,000,000. Violation of economic substance reconstruction under the Income Tax Act 2007 s BG 1 may be reconstructed by the Commissioner, and the company may be treated as not resident in New Zealand for treaty purposes, exposing all profits to tax with no treaty access.

Director personal liability: Directors can be prosecuted for money laundering under the Crimes Act 1961 with imprisonment up to 7 years if they use the container leasing operations to conceal proceeds of crime. Shell company misuse also allows courts to pierce the corporate veil, making directors personally liable for the company's tax and AML penalties, freezing personal assets, issuing travel bans, and blacklisting the directors from acting as company officers in New Zealand.
