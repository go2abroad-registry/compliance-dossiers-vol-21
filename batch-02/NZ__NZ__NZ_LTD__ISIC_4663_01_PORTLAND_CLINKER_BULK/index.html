Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. New Zealand law imposes no minimum paid-up share capital for a limited company, and there is no blocked escrow account requirement at incorporation. The company must have at least one shareholder and at least one director ordinarily resident in New Zealand (or a director resident in Australia with the company maintaining a New Zealand registered office). Shares may be fully or partly paid, and any non-cash consideration must be certified by the board in accordance with section 58 of the Companies Act 1993.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand; online electronic portal is the myIR secure digital services platform, with business registration integrated through the New Zealand Companies Office and the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) register.

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must first determine whether it is carrying on business in New Zealand. If it is required to register as an overseas company under the Companies Act 1993, the company must first complete that registration and obtain an NZBN. The entity must then apply to the Inland Revenue Department for a non-resident company IRD number through myIR, supplying the overseas certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents, registered office address, director details, ultimate shareholder details, and any relevant agency/permanent establishment information. The IRD number is the single statutory tax identification number for income tax and GST; there is no separate TIN. If taxable supplies exceed or are expected to exceed NZD 60,000 in any 12-month period, the entity must file the GST registration application through the same myIR portal. The GST number is the IRD number. An offshore non-resident exporter selling goods from New Zealand must follow the same IRD/GST registration path in order to claim zero-rating on exports and to recover New Zealand input tax if otherwise entitled.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A supply of goods is zero-rated under section 11(1)(a) of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 when the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand in the course of, or as part of, the supply. For bulk Portland cement clinker shipped by ocean vessel, the exporter must establish that the goods were physically removed from New Zealand and consigned to the foreign buyer, with export evidence ordinarily expected within 28 days of the time of supply. Input tax attributable to the exported goods is creditable through the GST return mechanism, and any resulting excess credit is refundable by the Commissioner. Services directly connected with the exported goods may also be zero-rated where the statutory tests are satisfied; qualifying offshore digital services are separate and governed by place-of-consumption rules.

  • Customs export declaration (export entry) filed with the New Zealand Customs Service through the Trade Single Window / Customs Management System under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, identifying the vessel, HS code 2523 (Portland cement clinker), foreign destination, and consignee.
  • Original ocean Bill of Lading issued by the carrier showing the New Zealand port of loading, the offshore port of discharge, the bulk cargo description, and the foreign consignee/notify party.
  • Commercial invoice and packing list in English stating Incoterms, metric tonnage, unit price, total consideration, and payment terms.
  • Bank-issued inward foreign exchange remittance evidence, namely SWIFT MT103, telegraphic transfer credit advice, or a bank statement showing settlement by the overseas buyer in a convertible foreign currency; this serves as the New Zealand equivalent of an inward remittance certificate.
  • Cargo and vessel movement records including the charter party or booking confirmation, cargo manifest, stevedore tally report, and voyage order confirming physical uplift from the New Zealand port.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised, free-floating exchange rate regime under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989. There are no exchange controls, no central bank permission screens, and no surrender requirements for any currency receipts. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is fully convertible for residents and non-residents on both current account and capital account transactions. Trade payments, loan repayments, dividend remittances, royalties, and freight settlements can be freely executed with any registered bank. Profits remitted to non-resident shareholders are subject only to standard corporate income tax at 28 percent and, where applicable, non-resident withholding tax at treaty-dependent rates (frequently 15 percent on dividends), but no additional exchange control approvals or liquidity retention obligations apply. For bulk Portland clinker wholesale activity, invoices may be denominated in USD, CNY, EUR, or NZD, proceeds can be retained in the originating foreign currency, and hedging instruments can be carried with any New Zealand registered bank. The only practical compliance overlay is the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, which requires banks to verify economic substance, UBOs, and trade documents before expediting cross-border wires.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No surrender applies. New Zealand does not impose any statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio for export foreign currency receipts. Exporters may keep proceeds in offshore or onshore FX accounts, settle invoices in any currency, and the Reserve Bank does not require repatriation or conversion.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ) — Te Pūtea Matua

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low for this entity. New Zealand's prudential regime does not license cross-border fintech EMIs as full deposit-taking banks. Offshore EMIs such as Wise or Airwallex can facilitate payment collection and payouts through New Zealand banking partners, but they cannot hold NZD settlement accounts in their own name, issue local IBANs, or provide committed trade finance, guarantee, and documentary credit services. A bulk clinker wholesale operation requires LC, guarantee, and documentary credit services that only locally incorporated banks can underwrite. Fintech or EMI rails should be used only for de minimis administrative FX payments after the primary corporate banking sweep account is opened.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 8,000,000 for annual cross-border related-party goods transactions; NZD 4,000,000 for intercompany loan balances; NZD 1,000,000 for related-party services, royalties, and intangibles; country-by-country reporting applies when a multinational group's consolidated annual revenue exceeds NZD 1,125,000,000.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply Caribbean-style Economic Substance Regulations, but treaty and domestic anti-avoidance rules require the NZ company to demonstrate substantive local activity. Core income-generating activities (CIGA) must include physical New Zealand office premises, locally resident procurement and logistics staff, independent board decision-making, bank accounts, inventory risk management, and negotiation of freight contracts. A letter-box director or nominee shareholder structure will be disregarded under the New Zealand general anti-avoidance rule (Income Tax Act 2007 sections BG 1 and GA 1) and the OECD Principal Purpose Test, causing profits to be re-attributed to the foreign shareholder or controlled foreign entity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Non-Arm's-Length Related-Party Clinker Purchases

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue may reconstruct assessable consideration under Income Tax Act 2007 section GC 13, disallow the excessive cost of sales and fees, and impose a 40% shortfall penalty for an unacceptable tax position under Tax Administration Act 1994 section 141C or a 100% penalty for an abusive tax position under section 141D, plus use-of-money interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve inflated related-party invoices breach their duty of proper purpose and care under Companies Act 1993 sections 135 and 137, allowing the court to pierce the corporate veil under section 301 and order personal asset restitution, director disqualification for up to 10 years, border alert/blacklisting associated with serious fraud investigations, and criminal imprisonment of up to three years under Tax Administration Act 1994 section 144.
Thin Capitalisation and Excessive Related-Party Debt Profit Stripping

Statutory sanction: The interest deduction on the excessive debt is denied under Part FE of the Income Tax Act 2007, and the recharacterised loan is subject to transfer pricing adjustment under section GC 13; Inland Revenue will impose shortfall penalties of 40% to 100% for a tax position that is not reasonably arguable or is abusive, plus interest and any NRWT on the non-deductible interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who authorise the excessive financing arrangement with knowledge of the stripping can be held jointly personally liable for the company's unpaid tax and restored debts under Companies Act 1993 sections 135, 136, and 301; civil asset restitution may extend to tracing transferred funds; repeated abuse triggers director blacklisting and can support a criminal prosecution for fraud offences involving dishonesty with imprisonment of up to seven years under the Crimes Act 1961.
NRWT Evasion and Unsupported Treaty Relief on Outbound Dividends, Interest, or Royalties

Statutory sanction: The payer becomes personally liable for the unpaid NRWT under Tax Administration Act 1994 sections 156 and 157, along with late payment penalties and use-of-money interest; if the omission was deliberate, a 150% evasion shortfall penalty is applied under section 141E and the expense associated with the payment may be disallowed entirely.

Director personal liability: Directors who deliberately fail to withhold or who fabricate treaty documentation are personally liable for the NRWT debt to the extent of company assets stripped, and a court may order restitution of unlawful distributions under Companies Act 1993 sections 135, 136, and 301; the director faces criminal imprisonment of up to three years for tax evasion under Tax Administration Act 1994 section 144, as well as cross-border travel-restriction/blacklisting measures in serious fraud cases.
