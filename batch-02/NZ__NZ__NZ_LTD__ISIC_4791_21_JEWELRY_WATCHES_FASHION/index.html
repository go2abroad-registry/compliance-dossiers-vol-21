Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. New Zealand law imposes no minimum paid-up share capital, no authorised capital concept, and no escrow, blocked-account, or pre-deposit capital rule. Shares may be issued fully paid, partly paid, or for non-cash consideration subject to the directors' determination that the consideration is fair and adequate. The company must have at least one share, one shareholder, and one director, but no minimum monetary subscription is required before incorporation.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand, also known as Te Tari Taake. The primary online taxpayer portal is myIR. Export customs control is administered separately by the New Zealand Customs Service through its Trade Single Window.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Obtain legal standing in New Zealand. A foreign non-resident entity must either incorporate a New Zealand Limited Company or register as an overseas company with the New Zealand Companies Office. This generates a New Zealand Business Number (NZBN). Step 2: Apply to Inland Revenue for a company IRD number using the myIR for Business online service. The IRD number is the taxpayer identification number used for all New Zealand tax obligations including income tax and GST. Step 3: Where a taxable activity is carried on in New Zealand and annual taxable supplies are NZD 60,000 or more, apply for GST registration through myIR. The GST registration is recorded under the same IRD number. Step 4: If the entity will have New Zealand employees, register for PAYE; if paying assessable income from New Zealand to non-residents, register for Non-Resident Withholding Tax (NRWT) obligations. Step 5: Appoint and retain a New Zealand address for service if required by the Companies Act regime for overseas companies, so that Inland Revenue and Customs notices can be validly served. Supporting documents include the certificate of incorporation or overseas company registration, NZBN certificate, registered office details, director and beneficial ownership information, and a home-jurisdiction tax residence certificate if treaty relief is sought.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None. New Zealand does not have a US-style check-the-box election for ordinary limited companies. A New Zealand Limited Company is automatically subject to entity-level taxation. Hybrid mismatch rules apply under the Income Tax Act 2007 and the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018. Look-through company status is a separate statutory eligibility category for closely held companies, not a general election for an ordinary NZ Ltd.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month period. Non-resident suppliers of remote services and low-value goods supplied to New Zealand consumers are also required to register for GST once those supplies exceed NZD 60,000 in a 12-month period.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985, sections 11 and 11A, goods may be zero-rated when supplied for export and physically removed from New Zealand. For an online jewellery and watches business, the standard qualifying supply is a sale of physical goods to an overseas customer where the supplier exports the goods from New Zealand in the course of the supply and retains evidence of export. Qualifying offshore services supplied to non-resident recipients may also be zero-rated if not performed in New Zealand or not connected with New Zealand land. The exporter is entitled to claim input tax credits on costs attributable to the zero-rated export supply, and resulting GST refunds are payable by Inland Revenue.

  • New Zealand Customs Service electronic Export Entry declaration lodged through the Trade Single Window
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing the goods were consigned to the overseas customer
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the exported jewellery, watches and fashion items
  • Proof of physical departure such as customs export clearance, carrier cargo manifest, or freight forwarder certification
  • Bank records evidencing receipt of foreign currency payment from the offshore buyer, including SWIFT MT103 or equivalent remittance credit advice

New Zealand has no mandatory real-time fiscal clearance regime, no anti-fraud digital stamping requirement, and no IRD API pre-clearance for invoices. Tax invoices must nevertheless meet the content requirements of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985. Electronic invoicing is voluntary and operates on the New Zealand e-Invoicing network using the Peppol framework through certified access points. GST returns are filed through Inland Revenue's myIR portal, and records supporting tax invoices and zero-rated exports must be retained under the Tax Administration Act 1994.

If Inland Revenue determines that export zero-rating is not substantiated, the supply is re-classified as taxable at 15% GST and the supplier is re-assessed for the output tax on the full consideration, with late payment interest. Shortfall penalties apply under sections 141A to 141E of the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for lack of reasonable care or an unacceptable tax position, 40% for gross carelessness, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion. Criminal prosecution may also follow for fraudulent export zero-rating claims, and the exporter may be liable for a retrospective clawback of all refunds previously paid.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully floating exchange-rate regime with no exchange controls. The New Zealand dollar is freely convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions. There are no central-bank approvals, no foreign-exchange budget, and no surrender requirements for export receipts. Non-resident equity investment, shareholder loans, trade payments, and profit remittances can be paid in and out without foreign-exchange authorisation. For an e-commerce jewellery and fine-watch trading company, the main practical hurdles are not currency-convertibility limits but tax clearances: dividends and interest paid to non-residents are subject to non-resident withholding tax, often reduced by double-tax agreements and the Approved Issuer Levy on New Zealand source interest. Banks also classify unusually large or structurally illogical payments for AML/CFT review. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand maintains the currency and prudential stability of the banking system, but it does not approve individual FX transactions. Thus, the treasury can structure inward equity injections, offshore settlement accounts, and external trade disbursements with full market-based conversion and no earmarked surrender requirement.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory timeline or mandatory conversion ratio applies to export proceeds. Exporters may retain foreign currency receipts in domestic or offshore bank accounts indefinitely and may convert to New Zealand dollars at commercial market rates without approval. Banks may require the underlying export invoice, packing list, customs export declaration, or transport waybill to classify the inwards remittance correctly for statistical reporting and to avoid a suspicious transaction report.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand - no separate Foreign Exchange Administration exists; exchange controls were abolished in 1984 and no central bank approval is required for outward or inward FX transactions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Rating: Conditional Medium for settlement liquidity, but Low as the primary treasury relationship. New Zealand does not maintain a domestic electronic-money-institution (EMI) licensing category equivalent to the EU/UK EMI regime. Payment service providers, including offshore digital EMIs, may lawfully operate if they are registered on the Financial Service Providers Register or rely on an applicable overseas licensing arrangement, and if they comply with the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009. Offshore EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, or specialist FX payment platforms are not legally barred from servicing New Zealand corporate entities, provided their New Zealand offering is compliant; however, they do not hold a full banking licence and are not covered by the existing bank deposit compensation arrangements. For an e-commerce jewellery/watches treasury, EMIs are acceptable for merchant settlement and low-value disbursements, especially when integrated with Stripe or PayPal, but they should not replace a domestic tier-1 bank for capital injection, LC issuance, high-value trade payments, or capital verification evidence.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder-loan funds must be remitted through the regulated banking system using SWIFT MT103 from the overseas remitting bank. New Zealand does not use IBAN; the sending bank must use the receiving bank's SWIFT BIC plus the company's New Zealand bank account number in the beneficiary field. The remittance advice should state the exact purpose, such as share capital contribution or shareholder loan, and reference the board resolution, subscription agreement, or loan agreement. The receiving bank will apply standard source-of-funds due diligence, so send the supporting board resolution, shareholder register, source-of-wealth report, and signed loan agreement in advance to prevent quarantine. After the funds are cleared, a New Zealand chartered accountant should issue a formal capital verification certificate based on the bank credit statement, the share register, and the board minutes. For any foreign direct investment above the statutory screening threshold, apply for the relevant Overseas Investment Act consent before making the transfer.For first-time, high-value jewellery or fine-watch supplier payments, use an irrevocable confirmed commercial Letter of Credit issued by the New Zealand clearing bank in favour of the overseas supplier, with a sight draft and the required inspection certificate. For established counterparties and repeat funding of inventory, use SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfers with the invoice number, purchase order number, incoterm, and customs tariff line in the payment reference. Documentary collections are also permissible for post-shipment settlement where the supplier retains title risk. All disbursements should be executed only from the tier-1 corporate account, not from merchant platform balances, and should be reconciled against Stripe, PayPal, and other settlement statements. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 aggregate cross-border related-party transactions is the administrative threshold commonly triggering mandatory contemporaneous Local File preparation in New Zealand; multinational groups with consolidated group revenue above NZD 1,125,000,000 are expected to prepare a Master File and Country-by-Country Report.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not apply Gulf-Cooperation-Council-style Economic Substance Regulations, but economic substance is enforced through tax residence, permanent establishment, general anti-avoidance and transfer pricing rules. Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) for an online jewellery and watch business are the functions of product sourcing, inventory risk-taking, pricing strategy, customer contracting, payment acceptance, marketing and after-sales service. If these activities are actually performed in New Zealand while profits are booked offshore, Inland Revenue will reallocate the profit under the arm's-length principle and may invoke the GAAR in ITA 2007 s BG 1.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / False Filing

Statutory sanction: Tax Administration Act 1994 ss 141A-141E: shortfall penalty up to 150% of the evaded tax; s 143B criminal penalty of a fine up to NZD 50,000 and imprisonment for up to 5 years. Inland Revenue may also assess penal tax at 100% for abusive tax avoidance and may apply criminal asset recovery under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced for directors who aided, abetted or were knowingly involved in the false return. Directors may be jointly and severally liable for the unpaid tax and shortfall penalties, subjected to civil asset restitution orders, restrained from leaving New Zealand through an Inland Revenue departure prohibition order, disqualified from managing companies under the Companies Act 1993, and sentenced to a maximum of 5 years imprisonment.
Transfer Pricing / Shell Company Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act 2007 subpart GC (ss GC 1-GC 14) authorises the Commissioner to reconstruct arm's-length transfer prices; s BG 1 is the general anti-avoidance rule. TAA 1994 s 141D imposes a 100% shortfall penalty for an abusive tax position, and s 141E may impose 150% where evasion exists. Thin capitalisation rules under subpart FE further apply to shareholder debt.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for invalidity of the tax avoidance arrangement if they signed the transfer pricing documentation or structured the shell companies without commercial substance. The court can order restitution of the diverted profits plus penalty interest, issue director disqualification or blacklisting under the Companies Act 1993 ss 382-383, prohibit departure from New Zealand where tax debt is material, and impose criminal imprisonment of up to 5 years for fraudulent or reckless trading contrary to ss 135-136 and s 380 of the Companies Act 1993.
AML-CFT / High-Value Dealer Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 ss 80-81: a body corporate faces civil penalties up to NZD 2,000,000 for serious non-compliance; infringement and criminal penalties also apply for failure to report suspicious transactions. Where money laundering is established, the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009 permits assets to be restrained and forfeited as tainted property.

Director personal liability: Directors who were knowingly concerned in the AML/CFT breach can be made personally liable for civil penalty amounts, disqualified from being directors or managing a company, blacklisted by the regulator, and subjected to restraining orders and foreign travel bans in cross-border money laundering investigations. In aggravated cases the directors face imprisonment under the Crimes Act 1961 for money laundering and under the Companies Act 1993 s 380 for fraudulent purposes.
