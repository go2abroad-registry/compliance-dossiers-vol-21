Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Services supplied to a non-resident who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply are zero-rated if the services are not supplied to a person in New Zealand and are not directly in connection with land or goods in New Zealand. Goods exports are zero-rated if exported within 28 days of supply. The supplier must hold sufficient evidence to prove the offshore supply or export.

  • Service contract with non-resident client
  • Invoice
  • Bank statement evidencing receipt of foreign currency
  • Statutory declaration confirming non-resident status

No real-time fiscal clearance or electronic invoicing mandate. GST invoices must contain prescribed details (supplier name, GST number, date, description, total). No government API or digital stamping requirement.

If zero-rating is claimed without proper substantiation, the supplier may be liable for GST at 15%, plus penalties and interest. Penalties can be up to 150% of the tax evaded for fraudulent evasion, and late payment penalties apply. The Inland Revenue Department may conduct audits and require retrospective recovery.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a fully liberalised monetary and exchange control system. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is independently floating and freely convertible for both current account (trade in goods and services, interest, dividends) and capital account (foreign direct investment, securities, loans, and real estate) transactions. There are no exchange controls, restrictions on repatriation of capital, or requirements for central bank approval for foreign exchange transactions. Residents and non-residents may hold foreign currency accounts domestically and offshore, and all foreign exchange receipts and payments may be settled at prevailing market rates. Companies are permitted to remit profits, dividends, and liquidation proceeds to foreign shareholders without prior government consent, provided that tax obligations are settled and standard anti-money laundering (AML) declaration forms are filed with banks. While the Reserve Bank of New Zealand has no direct exchange control mandate, it maintains regulatory oversight of foreign exchange dealers and infrastructure to ensure market integrity. Any perceived hurdles are administrative only, such as bank compliance verification of the underlying economic substance, source of funds, and beneficial ownership, rather than regulatory restriction on convertibility. The OECD-based legal framework makes the jurisdiction a stable platform for cross-border treasury operations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to the central bank or any other authority in New Zealand. Exporters retain 100% of foreign currency earnings and may hold them in offshore bank accounts or convert to NZD at will, without any mandatory conversion ratios or time limits. However, substantial foreign currency holdings must be disclosed in annual financial statements for tax and AML compliance.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Wise (formerly TransferWise) and Airwallex are registered as financial service providers under the Financial Service Providers (Registration and Dispute Resolution) Act 2008 and supervised by the Financial Markets Authority for AML purposes. These platforms can legally service New Zealand entities for cross-border payment facilitation and multi-currency settlement, but they are not licensed as registered banks by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand and therefore cannot offer deposit-taking products that constitute banking under New Zealand law. As a primary treasury account, an EMI is not viable because corporate payroll, tax withholdings, and high-volume transaction requirements typically mandate a recognised bank account. However, EMIs are highly viable as supplementary clearing rails for low-cost international transfers and supplier payments, provided the entity maintains a full-service relationship with at least one licensed bank. Overall feasibility: Medium as primary account; High as ancillary payment gateway.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections into the NZ Ltd from foreign shareholders should be transferred via SWIFT MT103 wire only, using the bank's BIC code and account IBAN (or New Zealand account number). Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10,000,000 annual group turnover threshold for mandatory Master File and Local File documentation under New Zealand's transfer pricing rules; documentation is also required for all cross-border related-party transactions with an aggregated annual value exceeding NZD 1,000,000 where the IRD deems the arrangement high risk, although the formal documentation threshold is aligned with OECD guidance at the NZD 10 million group revenue level. In all cases, contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation must be prepared before the tax return filing date.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) regime comparable to the UAE or Barbados, but the IRD applies general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) under section BG 1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 and transfer pricing rules under sections GC 13 to GC 15 of the Income Tax Act 2007, which mandate that cross-border related-party arrangements achieve an arm's length result. A New Zealand PR agency with a non-resident parent must demonstrate real operational substance, including active client contracts, locally employed professional staff, genuine decision-making authority for the PR engagement, and a substantive New Zealand profit margin for the functions performed. Companies that function as shell entities or that accept low functional margins without local economic substance face recharacterization, additional tax assessments, and penalty exposure. The OECD's principal purpose test and the Multilateral Instrument (MLI) also apply to deny treaty benefits where the main purpose of an arrangement is tax avoidance.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Prosecution under section 143 of the Tax Administration Act 1994, with imprisonment of up to 5 years, plus a criminal fine of up to NZD 50,000 and an additional penalty of treble the evaded tax shortfall (150% shortfall penalty on top of the tax itself)

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in evasion face personal criminal liability, imprisonment up to 5 years, and civil restitution for the evaded tax, including interest and penalties; travel bans may be sought by the IRD as a debt collection measure under section 34A of the Tax Administration Act 1994
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization of the arrangement under the GAAR or transfer pricing reallocation, resulting in additional taxable income, interest, and shortfall penalties up to 150%; denial of double tax relief and loss of imputation credit management status; possible removal from the New Zealand companies register under the Companies Act 1993 for serious non-compliance

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the unpaid tax of a shell company if they allowed the company to operate while insolvent or engaged in phoenix-company behavior; they may face civil penalty proceedings under the Companies Act 1993 and disqualification for up to 10 years
AML / Anti-Money Laundering Compliance

Statutory sanction: Fines of up to NZD 4 million for a company, plus NZD 1 million for each individual involved, per contravention; criminal liability with imprisonment up to 5 years for deliberate money laundering or terrorist financing facilitation under the Crimes Act 1961 and the Terrorism Suppression Act 2002

Director personal liability: Directors face personal criminal liability for willful blindness or participation in AML failures, including fines and up to 5 years imprisonment, as well as disqualification from managing companies under the Companies Act 1993
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment under sections GC 13-15 of the Income Tax Act 2007, with the Commissioner determining an arm's length result, imposing additional tax, paying prescribed interest from the original due date, and a shortfall penalty of up to 150% for gross carelessness or intentional behavior; document deficiencies attract a separate documentation penalty of up to NZD 20,000 per year

Director personal liability: Directors who sign inaccurate tax returns or knowingly authorize false transfer-pricing documentation become jointly and severally liable for the shortfall penalties and interest if the corporate veil is pierced; serious cases trigger director disqualification and personal non-asset-procedure proceedings
Non-Reporting of Foreign Bank Accounts and International Transactions

Statutory sanction: Late payment penalties of up to 150% of the unpaid withholding tax, late filing penalties, and prescribed interest; where the omission is deliberate, criminal prosecution under the Tax Administration Act 1994 with imprisonment up to 5 years

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally admonished through the IRD's director penalty notice procedure, making them personally liable for unpaid PAYE and certain withholding amounts under section 156 of the Tax Administration Act 1994; the notice may be followed by civil asset recovery and travel restriction procedures
Breach of Statutory Document Destruction Rules

Statutory sanction: Criminal fine of up to NZD 40,000 under the Tax Administration Act 1994 and an additional shortfall penalty of 150% if the destruction was intended to obstruct the IRD; individual directors face imprisonment of up to 5 years for perverting the course of justice

Director personal liability: Any director who directs, approves, or knowingly assists in the destruction of tax records is personally liable for fines and imprisonment, and will be subject to civil penalties for the value of the tax shortfall caused, with the corporate veil pierced under the equitable fraud exception
