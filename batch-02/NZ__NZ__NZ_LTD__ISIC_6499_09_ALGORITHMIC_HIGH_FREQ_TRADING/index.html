Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD), New Zealand; online portal myIR at ird.govt.nz

E-government filing portal

For an NZ limited company, the New Zealand Companies Office issues the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) at incorporation. The company then applies for its IRD number (the New Zealand TIN) and GST registration number (the New Zealand VAT equivalent) through myIR using the IRD's entity registration pathway. A foreign non-resident company that is not incorporated in New Zealand but carries on a taxable activity in New Zealand must obtain an IRD number by lodging the IRD's non-resident company application, submitting certified copies of the foreign incorporation certificate, constitutional documents, a certified English translation if applicable, identity documents for directors, and evidence of a New Zealand registered office or resident agent. Once registered, IRD will issue an IRD number and, if annual turnover exceeds or is expected to exceed NZD 60,000, a GST number.

15%

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from New Zealand are zero-rated under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 where the supplier exports the goods and retains satisfactory evidence of export. Services, including remote or digital services, supplied to a non-resident recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time the services are performed are zero-rated unless the services relate to New Zealand land or goods situated in New Zealand. For proprietary algorithmic high-frequency trading conducted for the company's own account, trading gains are not consideration for a taxable supply and therefore fall outside the GST system; any market-making or brokerage fees would generally constitute exempt financial services and would not qualify for zero-rating.

  • Customs Export Declaration / Export Entry lodged with the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window for goods
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing removal of goods from New Zealand
  • GST tax invoice complying with section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985
  • Foreign exchange inward remittance evidence (bank credit or remittance statement; New Zealand does not issue a statutory FIRC)
  • Non-resident status confirmation and offshore location evidence for zero-rated services

No real-time fiscal clearance, tax-authority API, anti-fraud digital stamping, or electronic invoice clearance requirement is imposed in New Zealand. GST-registered suppliers must ensure that any issued tax invoice complies with section 24 of the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985; a registered recipient may request a tax invoice and the supplier must provide it within 28 days of the request. E-invoicing via the Peppol network is voluntary and permitted.

If Inland Revenue determines that an export zero-rating claim is not substantiated, the supply is reassessed at 15% GST, use-of-money interest applies from the original due date, and shortfall penalties may be imposed under the Tax Administration Act 1994: 20% for failure to take reasonable care, 40% for gross carelessness, and 100% for evasion or intentional concealment. Supporting export and foreign exchange documentation must be retained for seven years and produced during audit; failure to do so will trigger clawback of any GST refunds and input credits claimed.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand applies a comprehensive liberalised capital and current account regime. There are no general exchange controls, no restrictions on the purchase or sale of foreign exchange by residents or non-residents, and no requirement to repatriate or surrender export proceeds. Capital account transactions, including inward direct investment, loans, derivatives and portfolio flows, are freely permitted, provided they do not trigger screening under the Overseas Investment Act 2005 or breach sectoral licensing laws. The New Zealand dollar is independently floated and fully deliverable in the global FX market, with spot, forward, swap and option liquidity available via tier-1 banks. Profit remittances in the form of dividends may be declared and paid without central bank approval provided the company is solvent and complies with the Companies Act 1993 and tax withholding rules. Certain non-bank lending or deposit-taking activities require licensing, but a proprietary algorithmic trading treasury is eligible for straightforward FX conversions at bank-negotiated rates without administrative restrictions. This makes New Zealand highly attractive for cross-border treasury clearing, with no bottleneck on rapid cross-border settlement for market-making flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory timeline, mandatory conversion ratio, or surrender obligation exists under New Zealand law. Exporters may hold foreign currency proceeds in domestic or offshore accounts and can deploy them freely within the scope of their broader treasury policy, subject only to general anti-money-laundering reporting thresholds.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua) – the central bank and prudential supervisor of New Zealand's banking system; formal foreign exchange controls were abolished in 1984

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low-Medium for a proprietary algorithmic high-frequency trading treasury. Offshore digital EMIs are generally not permitted to take deposits from New Zealand entities unless they hold a banking licence or an exemption from the Reserve Bank of New Zealand. Even when licenced as non-bank deposit takers, their capital capacity, direct clearing membership, and operational resilience are insufficient for ultra-low-latency settlement flows. The legally and commercially viable route is direct clearing with domestic tier-1 commercial banks, with correspondent banking rails used for offshore currency clearing.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 10,000,000 aggregate annual cross-border related-party revenue or expense is the administrative threshold above which contemporaneous Local File and Master File documentation is expected; for aggregate related-party transactions below NZD 1,000,000 a simplified one-page TP declaration suffices. IRD may nevertheless request documentation for any material transaction, particularly where high-value algorithm licence fees or management fees exceed NZD 1,000,000.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not maintain a separate Economic Substance Regulations regime for ordinary private companies; however, a New Zealand company claiming treaty benefits or a reduced NRWT rate must possess real economic substance in NZ. Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) for the HFT desk are the development and maintenance of trading algorithms, the placement of orders on licensed exchanges, the conduct of risk management, and the employment of locally resident quantitative traders and compliance officers. A principal-purpose test sits inside every NZ DTA, and GAAR under Income Tax Act 2007 s BG1 can cancel any tax advantage obtained by a shell company that lacks substance, contractual reality, or a commercial purpose.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Deliberate Tax Evasion and False Return

Statutory sanction: Shortfall penalty of 150% of the tax shortfall, criminal conviction under s 143A with a fine up to NZD 50,000, imprisonment up to five years, plus use-of-money interest. A careless but non-evasionary return triggers the 20% or 40% shortfall penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign or direct the false return have personal civil liability for the shortfall penalty where the company cannot pay, can be removed from the board under the Companies Act 1993 s 385, are exposed to travel restriction under TAA s 157 for unpaid debt, and may face imprisonment for evasion. Civil asset recovery may be brought under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009.
Transfer Pricing Failure and Profit Stripping

Statutory sanction: The IRD may re-determine taxable income under Income Tax Act 2007 s GC 13, deny the deduction in full, recharacterise the excess payment as a dividend subject to NRWT, and impose shortfall penalties under TAA ss 141-141C (20-150%). Additional failure-to-document penalties apply when no Local File or Master File exists.

Director personal liability: Directors approving the false TP policy are personally assessable for civil penalties if the company is insolvent; they may be sued for breach of director duties under Companies Act 1993 ss 131-138; travel bans and freezing orders can be imposed; criminal liability arises if the documentation is fabricated. PIERCING THE CORPORATE VEIL under s 299 permits the court to look through the company to recover assets improperly transferred out of the company.
Shell Company and AML/CFT Beneficial Ownership Breach

Statutory sanction: Under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009, a reporting entity that breaches customer due diligence or record-keeping obligations can face civil penalty up to NZD 2 million for each act or omission; the Companies Act 1993 imposes fines for false statements in the share register; GAAR under s BG1 invalidates the tax structure.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in an unlawful shell arrangement are personally liable for AML penalties, can be disqualified from managing companies for up to five years, may be subject to asset forfeiture under the Criminal Proceeds (Recovery) Act 2009, and may be imprisoned for up to two years for false sworn declarations. The corporate veil is pierced where incorporation was a sham or façade, allowing creditors and the IRD to execute against personal assets of the directors.
