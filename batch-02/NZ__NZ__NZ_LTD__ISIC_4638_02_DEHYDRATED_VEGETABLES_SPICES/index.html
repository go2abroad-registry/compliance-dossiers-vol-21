Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD) of New Zealand, with the online electronic portal known as myIR

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity that derives New Zealand-sourced income or is required to register for New Zealand GST must apply for an IRD number. The application for a company is made using the IRD’s online non-resident company registration form (IR742) submitted through myIR, together with certified copies of the certificate of incorporation, the constitutional documents, a notice of directors, and proof of the registered office address. The IRD will issue a single IRD number that serves as the company's income tax and GST identification number. If the entity will only make purchases and is not carrying on a taxable activity, a non-resident GST registration may be required solely for refund purposes if the entity is in the business of supplying goods or services and is registered or required to be registered. The IRD number application must be completed before the entity begins any taxable activity in New Zealand and before the first supply of goods or services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating applies to supplies of goods exported from New Zealand by a GST-registered supplier, provided the supplier exports the goods within 28 days of the time of supply and retains the specified evidence. For goods, the supplier must hold a New Zealand Customs export declaration and proof that the goods left New Zealand, such as a bill of lading or air waybill. For qualifying services supplied to non-residents, zero-rating applies where the services are supplied to a recipient who is outside New Zealand at the time of supply, or where the supply consists of remote services to a non-resident recipient. The exporter must also obtain and retain sufficient documentary evidence in the ordinary course of business to substantiate the export.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or bank remittance advice evidencing foreign currency receipt
  • Commercial invoice and purchase order
  • Packing list clearly identifying the dehydrated vegetables and spices
  • Certificate of origin (where applicable)

New Zealand currently requires no real-time fiscal clearance or mandatory electronic invoicing clearance. Tax invoices must be issued in accordance with the GST Act, and may be issued electronically. There is no pre-clearance API stamping or anti-fraud digital stamping mandate. The IRD has adopted voluntary e-invoicing interoperability through the PAN-NZ and PEPPOL framework for government suppliers, but it does not impose a universal clearance obligation.

If the IRD determines that an export zero-rating was claimed without valid supporting documentation, the supplier becomes liable to account for GST at 15% on the export sale, plus interest under the Tax Administration Act 1994. Where a false document is knowingly supplied or a fraudulent export declaration is made, penalties may apply under the Crimes Act 1961 and the Customs and Excise Act 2018. The IRD can assess shortfall penalties of 20% for unacceptable tax positions, 40% for gross carelessness, or 150% for fraudulent evasion. Additionally, any subsequent diversion of the goods back into New Zealand without payment of GST triggers a full clawback of the zero-rating, with penalties and interest.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates a full open capital account with no general exchange control restrictions on current account transactions (goods, services, interest, dividends) or capital account transactions for non-residents. The New Zealand dollar (NZD) is freely convertible under IMF Article VIII status. Profit remittance via dividends, management fees, or royalties is not subject to exchange control approval by the RBNZ. However, tax compliance obligations apply: non-resident withholding tax (NRWT) on interest, dividends, and royalties must be withheld or an approved issuer levy (AIL) paid for certain interest payments. Any remittance is reportable under the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2009 (AML/CFT Act) obligations for transaction monitoring and prescribed transaction reporting thresholds. There are no statutory hurdles to convertibility, but banking instructions may require declaration of the underlying commercial activity and New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) verification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory requirement in New Zealand mandating surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion of export proceeds to NZD. Export receipts may be held in foreign currency bank accounts (offshore or onshore) at the exporter’s discretion, subject to the AML/CFT regime and the Inland Revenue’s financial reporting rules on attributable income from offshore accounts. For NZ-resident companies, foreign currency gains/losses on foreign currency bank accounts may be taxable under the financial arrangements rules (FA rules) in the Income Tax Act 2007; hedging with permitted instruments is recommended to stabilize NZD reporting. No timeline is imposed for conversion or repatriation.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI/neobank accounts (e.g., Wise Business, Revolut Business, Airwallex) are operationally viable in New Zealand as payment aggregation and cross-border settlement tools, but they are not licensed as locally regulated banks under the Reserve Bank of New Zealand prudential framework. Legal constraints: (1) Offshore EMIs are not appropriate for holding client trust funds or for long-term treasury balances due to lack of New Zealand deposit guarantee coverage; (2) A New Zealand incorporated company using an offshore EMI is subject to FATCA/CRS reporting; (3) EMI onboarding commonly requires proof of NZBN registration, director ID verification, bank statements from a NZ bank, and source-of-funds documentation; (4) Some offshore EMIs will not support New Zealand GST tax invoicing requirements or local trade finance instruments such as documentary collections or Letters of Credit; (5) For bulk dehydrated vegetable and spice exports, SWIFT-based local bank rails remain necessary for LC transactions, while EMIs may serve as supplementary settlement for small/medium TT wires. Feasibility rating: Medium — acceptable as supplementary clearing but not as primary banking relationship.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity or shareholder loan funding must be routed via SWIFT wire transfer to the New Zealand corporate operating account. Required purpose codes and disclosures: (1) For equity capital injection use bank instruction narrative 'Share Capital Contribution — NZ Companies Act 1993' alongside the NZBN and company registration number; no specific statutory purpose code is mandated by the RBNZ, but banks will categorize payment as 'capital account' under their internal foreign exchange reporting. (2) For shareholder loans use a notarized loan agreement and an Acknowledgment of Debt; interest must be at arm’s length to avoid deemed dividend and NRWT exposure. (3) Confirm the source of funds and conduct sanctions screening with the sending and receiving banks. (4) If the amount exceeds NZD 10,000 (or equivalent), the AML/CFT prescribed transaction reporting threshold may apply; banks will require evidence of the underlying corporate action (board resolution for capital increase, share allotment form, or loan contract). (5) No RBNZ approval is required, but the bank will verify directors’ identities and may require the company’s registered office address to match the onboarding profile. For large injections exceeding NZD 50,000, a bank compliance interview and source-of-funds dossier are expected.For international trade settlements (bulk dehydrated vegetable and spice exports), the primary clearing rails are SWIFT MT103 (single customer credit transfer) for TT/Wire remittances and commercial Letters of Credit (UCP 600) for documentary trade. The company may receive open account payments via MT103; for exports to buyers demanding documentary security, a confirmed irrevocable Letter of Credit is accepted. New Zealand operates on SWIFT payment architecture with the Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS) for domestic NZD payments; cross-border outbound disbursements for refunds, supplier payments, or commission payments via SWIFT are processed by the commercial bank’s international division. Supporting trade documentation for every outbound trade disbursement should include: commercial invoice, bill of lading/airway bill, packing list, certificate of origin (Form A or NZ-China FTA certificate as applicable), phytosanitary certificate (for agricultural products) if required, and insurance document. Banks are required to conduct transaction monitoring and sanctions screening on each leg; any trade payment to high-risk jurisdictions will trigger enhanced due diligence. Payment rails for LC: BP/BO (bills purchased/negotiation) may be used to obtain liquidity before maturity; bank undertaking fees and discount rates apply. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: NZD 1,000,000 in aggregate cross-border related-party transactions per income year – taxpayers at or above this threshold are expected to maintain a Local File with full functional analysis, comparability analysis, and arm's length pricing evidence. Global groups with consolidated revenue equal to or exceeding NZD 1 billion are subject to Master File and Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) obligations if New Zealand is the parent jurisdiction or through a notification requirement.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand has no codified Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) in the manner of Caribbean jurisdictions, but the Inland Revenue robustly applies the general anti-avoidance rule (tax laws section BG1) and the principal purpose test embedded in New Zealand's DTAs. A limited company that exists only as a mailbox or conduit without real board meetings, local business functions, commercial decision-making, or meaningful operational substance may be disregarded, reconstructed, or denied treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraud

Statutory sanction: Civil shortfall penalty of up to 150% of the evaded tax, plus use-of-money interest from the due date of the original return. Criminal liability: imprisonment for a term of up to 5 years in New Zealand for tax evasion, with unlimited fines in the discretion of the court.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly participate in evasion face personal civil liability for the shortfall penalty, are criminally prosecutable, and may be subject to court-ordered travel bans, asset restraint orders, and disqualification from managing companies.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The Inland Revenue may apply the general anti-avoidance rule (s BG1) to disregard the arrangement, reconstruct the taxable position, and assess the controlling parties on the underlying income. A denial of treaty benefits can also result in a penalty of up to 100% of the shortfall income tax.

Director personal liability: Directors and shareholders may be made personally liable for the reconstructed tax liabilities, and corporate veil-piercing is available where the company is a mere facade. Relevant trust assets can be clawed back under voidable transaction and anti-assignment rules.
AML / CFT Violations

Statutory sanction: Under the AML/CFT Act 2009, corporate bodies face fines up to NZ$10 million for a serious breach, or twice the value of the transaction where determinable. Individual officers can be imprisoned for up to 7 years. Regulatory compliance orders and suspension of bank account may also be imposed.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers can be held personally liable for AML/CFT fines if they aided or abetted the breach, were grossly negligent, or failed to implement a compliant AML programme. Travel bans may be sought by authorities if there are reasonable grounds to believe an individual is fleeing the jurisdiction to avoid prosecution.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: Inland Revenue can issue a transfer pricing adjustment to recompute taxable income at arm's length, applying shortfall penalties ranging from 20% (failure to take reasonable care), 40% (gross carelessness), up to 100% (abusive tax avoidance) or 150% (evasion), plus late payment interest and use-of-money interest.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve false or misleading transfer pricing documentation can be personally responsible for civil penalties, and may be caught by the general anti-avoidance liability provisions. In severe cases, criminal liability for tax evasion could attach to directors with direct involvement in price manipulation.
