Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. New Zealand imposes no minimum issued or paid-up capital for a limited company, and there is no requirement for authorized share capital, par value, or a blocked escrow account prior to incorporation. Shares may be issued at any consideration approved by the Board of Directors, and subscriber funds may be placed in an ordinary corporate operating account. The only statutory filing cost is the Companies Office incorporation fee, plus optional fees for certified copies or special resolution filings if a custom constitution is adopted.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Inland Revenue Department (IRD / Te Tari Taake) of New Zealand; online taxpayer portal is myIR, accessible through ird.govt.nz, with company incorporation and business registration also anchored in the New Zealand Companies Office and the New Zealand Business Number (NZBN) regime.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Incorporate the New Zealand limited company (NZ LTD) under the Companies Act 1993 through the New Zealand Companies Office online register; the company automatically obtains an NZBN and an IRD number (tax file number). Step 2: For a non-resident foreign entity not incorporated in New Zealand, apply through myIR for a New Zealand IRD number using the non-resident tax registration process. This requires proof of the foreign entity's legal existence (certificate of incorporation or equivalent), the entity's registered business address, full legal name and date of birth for each natural-person director or principal, a New Zealand address for service or an electronic equivalent accepted by IRD, and a declaration of intended New Zealand business activity and estimated turnover. Step 3: If the entity is or will be carrying on taxable activity in New Zealand or supplies remote services to New Zealand residents above the statutory threshold, complete GST registration through the same myIR profile by disclosing the actual or expected 12-month taxable supply value. Step 4: IRD activates the GST registration under the same IRD number; no separate GST number is issued. Step 5: Maintain the Companies Office and myIR records as the source of truth and update any changes in directors, shareholders, address for service, or business activity within statutory timeframes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (a New Zealand-resident limited company is taxed at the entity level on worldwide income; a non-resident foreign company is within Territorial_Scope for New Zealand-sourced income only; no general pass-through eligibility and no Tax_Exempt_Zone status for an ordinary NZ LTD).

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in New Zealand. Eligible closely-held New Zealand-resident limited companies may elect Look-Through Company (LTC) status under the Income Tax Act 2007 subpart HB, but that election is available only where all shareholders are natural persons satisfying New Zealand residency conditions. New Zealand also applies hybrid mismatch rules from the Taxation (Neutralising Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) Act 2018, which restrict double-non-taxation outcomes for hybrid financial instruments and hybrid entities; no generic pass-through election is available for a non-resident shareholder.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

15%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

NZD 60,000 of taxable supplies in any 12-month rolling period triggers mandatory GST registration; non-resident suppliers of remote services (including digital and low-code / software-as-a-service offerings) must register if they supply more than NZD 60,000 of remote services to New Zealand residents in a 12-month period. Registration is also mandatory where the person intends to make taxable supplies exceeding the threshold in the next 12 months.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating at 0% GST is available under the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 when the supplier makes a supply of goods that are physically exported from New Zealand, either by the supplier or by the overseas recipient, and the goods are removed from New Zealand within the statutory timeframe; or when services are supplied to a non-resident person located outside New Zealand and the services are not directly connected to New Zealand land, not supplied in respect of tangible goods situated in New Zealand at the time of supply, and not services supplied to a person in New Zealand. For the no-code website builder and software-as-a-service scope, qualifying digital services supplied to an overseas non-resident are eligible for zero-rating if the supplier holds two or more consistent, non-conflicting pieces of business evidence proving the recipient is outside New Zealand, such as the recipient's business address, IP country, bank account country, telephone country code, billing address, or foreign tax identification number. Zero-rating is only substantiated if the exporter keeps complete records showing the true destination of the goods or the true non-resident identity of the digital-service recipient; a bare assertion of foreign sale is insufficient.

  • New Zealand Customs export declaration (electronic export entry filed through the New Zealand Customs Service Trade Single Window and cleared for departure)
  • Bill of Lading, Master Air Waybill, or House Air Waybill showing the New Zealand port or airport of departure and the overseas destination
  • Commercial invoice and contract of sale with the overseas buyer, clearly identifying the goods or services, the incoterm, and the 0% GST treatment
  • Proof of physical export, including container tracking, customs departure confirmation, or departure report evidencing that the goods left New Zealand
  • Foreign exchange and inward remittance evidence, including SWIFT MT103, bank remittance advice, foreign exchange contract, or, where required, a bank-issued Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or equivalent New Zealand inward remittance certificate proving export proceeds were received from the foreign buyer

New Zealand does not impose a mandatory real-time fiscal clearance, continuous transaction control, government API authorization, or anti-fraud digital stamping regime. E-invoicing is voluntary and operates through the New Zealand PEPPOL network. Inland Revenue accepts electronic records and tax invoices, provided the invoice contains the statutory content prescribed by the Goods and Services Tax Act 1985 and Tax Administration Act 1994: supplier legal name and IRD number, issue date, description of supply, tax-exclusive amount, and either the GST amount or a clear statement that the price includes GST, the total payable, and the required tax-invoice wording where applicable. There is no pre-clearance requirement before issuing a GST invoice.

Inland Revenue can audit export zero-rating through review of GST returns, invoicing, customs records, shipping documents, and bank remittance evidence. If an export zero-rating is unsupported, incorrectly claimed, or later discovered to involve false or misleading documentation, the supply is recharacterised as taxable at 15% GST, with use-of-money interest running from the original return due date and retrospective clawback of any resulting refund. Shortfall penalties under the Tax Administration Act 1994 apply: 20% for an unacceptable tax position, 40% for failure to disclose a tax position, 100% for an abusive tax position, and 150% for evasion or fraudulent activity. Criminal prosecution is possible for knowingly providing false information or creating false documents to support a zero-rated export claim. The taxpayer bears the burden of proving the export documentation requirements, and all supporting records must generally be retained for at least seven years.

Stage 04

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

New Zealand operates under a free-float exchange rate regime and maintains no comprehensive foreign exchange control legislation. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand does not require prior approval for typical current-account or capital-account transactions, and the New Zealand dollar is fully convertible without mandatory surrender requirements. Exports may retain receipts in foreign currency indefinitely, and no conversion ratio is imposed by statute. Profit remittance, dividend distribution, and loan repayments are freely permitted subject to standard tax withholding and corporate documentation requirements. Non-resident ownership of the New Zealand limited company is allowed, and the business scope of a no-code website builder does not trigger sector-specific FDI screening. The practical constraints are therefore not FX controls but banking due diligence, because banks must independently verify beneficial ownership, source of funds, and legitimate business purpose. The Reserve Bank focuses on prudential supervision and systemic stability, leaving transaction-level currency freedom to market participants. For a standard SaaS entity, no special central bank license or exchange approval is required for treasury operations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender rules apply in New Zealand. Exporters and digital service providers may retain all foreign currency earnings offshore or in local foreign currency accounts, with no mandatory conversion timeline and no required percentage sale of export proceeds to the local market.

Central bank supervision: Reserve Bank of New Zealand (Te Pūtea Matua)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Offshore fintech electronic money institutions are not legally barred from supporting New Zealand entities, but they are not a primary treasury rail. New Zealand does not issue UK-style EMI licences, and most foreign EMIs must partner with a licensed local bank or rely on a transactional banking wrapper to obtain a NZD settlement capability. For a corporate exporter with cross-border SaaS billing, an offshore EMI can serve as a secondary collection and disbursement channel, particularly for multi-currency payouts and low-fee trade settlements. However, the onboarding viability as a main operating account is Medium-to-Low because of reputational risk, the absence of full NZ banking licence protections, limited capital verification capacity, and the requirement to maintain a domestic bank relationship for statutory and liquidity purposes. The prudent treasury structure is to keep the primary NZD operational account at a Tier-1 bank and use a regulated international EMI only for ancillary billing streams such as Stripe/Billing or Paddle settlements.

The payment instruction must include the correct remittance currency, entity legal name, unique corporate registration number and the relevant purpose code for foreign investment or equity capital. For the no-code software builder, the inbound funds are likely intercompany loans or paid-up capital from shareholders; the bank will require a board resolution, a funds-receipt declaration, and a source-of-funds statement. There is no central bank conversion approval needed, but the injecting entity must ensure that all taxes, including non-resident withholding on interest or dividends, are addressed. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: New Zealand does not prescribe a single statutory de-minimis threshold for all related-party transactions; however, Inland Revenue’s transfer-pricing compliance framework requires contemporaneous documentation for material cross-border related-party transactions and expects a Local File/Master File structure for significant MNE operations; CbCR is mandatory for groups with consolidated revenue of at least NZD 1.13 billion; in practice, related-party transactions above approximately NZD 10 million annual value are treated as material, but the absence of any threshold does not eliminate the obligation to maintain arm’s-length pricing evidence and IRD records under Tax Administration Act 1994.

Economic substance scope: New Zealand does not operate a Caribbean/BVI-style Economic Substance Regulations statute, but economic substance is embedded in the corporate residency rules, permanent establishment thresholds, transfer-pricing principles, the general anti-avoidance rule in sections BG1/GA1 of the Income Tax Act 2007, and anti-sham doctrine. For a no-code website builder and low-code platform provider, the Core Income-Generating Activities — product development, contract execution, user data control, pricing, risk management, and revenue settlement — must be performed by employees and assets physically located in New Zealand for the NZ company to be regarded as the true beneficial owner of the income. If the foreign parent or a Singapore/Hong Kong/BVI affiliate performs the CIGA while the NZ entity merely invoices subscribers, Inland Revenue can re-allocate profits to the entity where the economically significant people and functions are located, or deny treaty benefits on the ground that the NZ entity is a shell without real business purpose.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Omission of Foreign Digital Revenue

Statutory sanction: Tax Administration Act 1994 sections 141A and 143A: civil shortfall penalty of 150% of the evaded tax, plus use-of-money interest; criminal liability for evasion by a company officer or director includes a fine up to NZ$50,000 and/or imprisonment for up to 5 years for each offence; the Commissioner may also issue a default assessment based on best information, and the tax debt becomes immediately recoverable.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced for fraud and evasion: a director who knowingly concealed revenue is personally liable for the resulting tax shortfall and penalties under the director-liability provisions in the Tax Administration Act 1994; the court may order restitution of company assets under section 301 of the Companies Act 1993; Inland Revenue may seek a departure prohibition order under section 157 of the TAA, effectively blacklisting the director from air travel until the tax debt is secured; conviction can also lead to director disqualification and overseas asset tracing with enforcement through mutual assistance treaties.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation / Royalty Stripping to an Offshore IP Owner

Statutory sanction: Income Tax Act 2007 section GC13 empowers the Commissioner of Inland Revenue to impute an arm's-length price and adjust the NZ company’s taxable income; the corresponding shortfall penalty under Tax Administration Act 1994 for an unacceptable tax position or abusive transfer-pricing position can reach 20% to 100%, with a dedicated transfer-pricing shortfall penalty of 40% on the quantum of adjustment in serious cases; use-of-money interest runs from the original due date.

Director personal liability: A director who signs transfer-pricing documentation or supports board minutes falsely describing non-existent functions, assets, or risks may be found to have been involved in misleading tax conduct; liability can attach personally for the unpaid tax adjustment if the company is insolvent; the court may order recovery of artificially extracted royalties from the director as transaction-concealment or sham compensation under the Companies Act 1993; travel restrictions and prosecution for false statements are available, and egregious conduct can result in criminal imprisonment of up to 5 years.
Sham Shell Company / Abusive Treaty and Economic-Substance Avoidance

Statutory sanction: The general anti-avoidance rule in sections BG1 and GA1 of the Income Tax Act 2007 permits Inland Revenue to reconstruct the arrangement and tax the economic substance; the company may be treated as non-resident or as having a permanent establishment elsewhere, thereby denying treaty benefits and making all New Zealand-source fees subject to NRWT at the statutory rate; the abusive tax position penalty is 100% of the shortfall, with 150% penalty and criminal prosecution available if the shell structure is used with intent to evade tax; false declarations made to payment processors or tax authorities also constitute criminal fraud under section 143A.

Director personal liability: Where a company is a mere facade or sham, New Zealand courts will pierce the corporate veil and hold the directing mind and hands personally liable for the tax owed; directors are exposed to civil asset restitution, recovery actions and compensation orders under section 301 of the Companies Act 1993 for knowingly participating in concealed transactions, and can be disqualified under section 383; Insolvency officers may recover fees paid through the shell as voidable transactions; Inland Revenue can use section 157 departure prohibition orders to blacklist a director from leaving New Zealand until outstanding assessments are satisfied, and fraudulent misuse of a corporate facade carries criminal liability including imprisonment for up to 5 years in addition to personal seizure of proceeds of crime.
